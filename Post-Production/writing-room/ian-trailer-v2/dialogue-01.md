# THE WRENCH TURNER, trailer v2, dialogue pass 1 (dialogue-doctor)

Mode: GENERATE. This is a room draft, not Zac's or Ian's writing, so line proposals are allowed and are marked PROPOSAL. Anything Ian rewrites after this becomes his, and the standing rule switches on.
Source read: `draft-01.md`, Section D (VO) plus all card text in B, C, E.
Voice target from the draft: warm, plain, soft Texas, one man across a kitchen table, maybe Ian himself.

---

## BLUF

Most of the VO is sayable and the voice is close. The open ("grew up under a car") and the close ("back out to the garage") sound like a person. The middle does not. It reads like a chapter list read aloud: mom cooked, girl cheated, buddy grieved, squad leader, wounded, nurse. A man telling this at a kitchen table would not list it. He would skip to the parts that matter to him.

**Worst offender:** "A buddy couldn't get past a friend they lost. His dad went with him." A stranger cannot tell who "they" is, who "him" is, or why it matters. It's there to tick off a plot point, and it costs five seconds the trailer can't spare.

**Stranger test:** fails, narrowly. With the VO alone, a stranger hears a soldier's life from boyhood to homecoming. They don't learn that this is a novel, that he comes home broken, or that the book is about fixing something. The one line that carries the promise ("even something broken can be fixed") is a card, not VO. If Ian reads this on a podcast or someone listens with the screen off, there is no story question, only a biography.

## Findings, VO in reading order

**"Brandon Turner grew up under a car."**
Holds. Full name is fine once from a narrator, and it's a real hook. Keep.

**"His dad did the work. Brandon handed him the wrench."**
Holds. Plain and spoken. Small thing, second "Brandon" in two lines. A man telling it says "he" by now.

**"Then Brandon grew up, and joined the Army."**
Two problems. "Grew up" echoes line one, and it sounds like a slip rather than a choice. The comma before "and" is a comma nobody performs. It's also the first of a "Then" chain (see Pattern 1).
Reaching for: the time jump from boy to soldier.
PROPOSAL: "Then he was eighteen, and he joined the Army." Or skip the bridge and let the boots shot do the jump.

**"Home on leave, his mom cooked his favorite."**
Read out loud, this says his mom was home on leave. A listener untangles it, but they spend a beat doing it. It's also inventory. Nothing in it costs Brandon anything.
Reaching for: home is warm, and he's loved there. That matters because it sets up home being the hard part later.
PROPOSAL: "He'd come home on leave and his mom would cook his favorite." The "would" makes it a habit, not an event, which is how a person remembers home.

**"His girl had someone else. The whole party found out when he did."**
The first sentence is spoken and good. The second is writerly. It's a clever compression a novelist writes and a man at a table never says, and on first hearing it's ambiguous (found out what, that she cheated or that he knew?).
Reaching for: public humiliation.
PROPOSAL: keep "His girl had someone else." Let the party noise cutting out do the second half. The draft already has that sound cue, so let it carry the beat.

**"A buddy couldn't get past a friend they lost. His dad went with him."** (worst offender)
Pronoun soup. "They" could be the buddy and Brandon, or the buddy and someone else. "Him" could be the buddy or Brandon. A stranger can't follow it, and even a reader of the book would need a second pass. It's also the fourth life event in twenty seconds.
Reaching for: the dad shows up for people, not only for Brandon.
PROPOSAL: cut it from the VO. If Ian considers this beat essential, it needs a name or a plain relationship ("his best friend") and a plain verb ("drove up to Oklahoma with him"). That pushes it to six or seven seconds, and the middle can't spare that. See ASK IAN 4 in the draft, which is already unsure about the facts.

**"He lost his squad leader. Then he was the squad leader."**
This is the trailer line. The mirror is built to be quoted. It does land, and a soldier could say it, but it's the first of three two-beat mirrors in a row (see Pattern 2). Keep one mirror. This is the strongest of the three, so keep this one and break the others.

**"Then he was wounded. A nurse leaned over him. He thought she was an angel."**
"Was wounded" is passive and formal. A man talking says "got hit" or "got hurt." Three short declaratives in a row make the rhythmic three. The angel line puzzles a stranger. Is she a love interest? Is he dying? It raises a question the trailer never answers, and not in a good way.
Reaching for: how close to death he got.
PROPOSAL: "Then he got hit. He woke up and thought the nurse was an angel." That's one breath, it's how a person tells it, and "woke up" makes clear he nearly didn't.

**"His father was there the whole time."**
Two problems. First, every other line says "dad." A real speaker picks one word for his own father and sticks with it, and a switch to "father" here sounds like a different writer. Second, "the whole time" of what? The rehab and Landstuhl facts are only on the card at the same moment, so a listener gets "there the whole time" with no time attached. The 30-second cut already fixes this ("Two years of rehab. His father was there the whole time.").
PROPOSAL: "Two years of rehab, and his dad never left that chair." It names the time out loud, matches the chair in the shot, and says "dad."

**"He had his brothers. He had his dad."**
Theme summary. It's the narrator telling us what the trailer just showed us. "Brothers" will read as siblings to a stranger. It's the second mirror.
Reaching for: he wasn't alone.
PROPOSAL: cut it. The chair line already says it. The folded flag and dog tags shot can play under music or room tone.

**"He made it home. That was the hard part."**
This is the right idea and the real hook of the book. But a listener can hear it two ways, "getting home was hard" or "being home was hard." The second is the one you want, and the line doesn't force it. It's also the third mirror.
Reaching for: war was the easy part, and coming home is where the story is.
PROPOSAL: "He made it home. Turned out home was the hard part." Contracted and a little rough, in a Texas cadence, and it means one thing.

**"So he went back out to the garage. To the car he and his dad worked on."**
Holds. "So" is how people start the end of a story. The fragment is fine spoken. This is the best line in the piece. One note: "he and his dad worked on" is correct grammar, but a soft Texas speaker might say "him and his dad," and Ian should read it the way he'd say it.

## Patterns (one note each)

**Pattern 1, the "Then" chain.** "Then Brandon grew up," "Then he was the squad leader," "Then he was wounded," plus "Then the Army. Then Iraq." in the 15. It's AI-trailer cadence, events counted off on fingers. Keep at most two in the 60.

**Pattern 2, the two-beat mirror.** "He lost his squad leader. Then he was the squad leader." / "He had his brothers. He had his dad." / "He made it home. That was the hard part." Three in a twelve-second run is a formula, and a listener starts hearing the rhythm instead of the words. Keep one.

**Pattern 3, no contractions.** In about 110 words of VO the only contraction is "couldn't." A warm man at a kitchen table says "he'd," "didn't," "got." Ian should feel free to contract anything as he reads. Write it the way it's said.

**Pattern 4, too much to say in the time.** Rough read at an unhurried pace runs about two words a second. Three rows are over:
- 0:18 to 0:23, 12 words in 5 seconds.
- 0:23 to 0:28, 13 words in 5 seconds.
- 0:35 to 0:40, 14 words in 5 seconds.
The draft asks for a beat at every line break as well. Read as written, the 60 will run long, or the voice will have to hurry, and that kills the kitchen-table feel. Cutting the buddy line and the brothers line (above) fixes most of it.

## Card text

- **"my wrench turner"** Fine, pending Ian's exact words (ASK IAN 1). Lowercase reads as a quote, which is right.
- **BRAGG / GERMANY / IRAQ** As a card, the rhythmic three is fine. It's a montage convention and doesn't pretend to be speech. A stranger won't know "Bragg" is a post. Acceptable, since the boots and helmet carry it.
- **LANDSTUHL. THEN TWO YEARS OF REHAB.** Two problems. Strangers don't know what Landstuhl is, and the card runs while a different sentence is being spoken, so the viewer has to read one thing and hear another. If the VO says "two years of rehab," the card can drop to LANDSTUHL alone, or drop entirely.
- **"even something broken can be fixed"** It's the book's own line, so it isn't slop, even though it's aphorism-shaped. But it's the only place the book's promise lives, and it's silent. See the stranger test. Consider having the VO speak the first half of the idea and let the card finish it, or have Ian read the line itself over the ratchet click. That's Zac's and Ian's call, since putting it on both card and VO is a style choice.
- **End card** Not dialogue. No notes.

## Stranger test, what the VO is missing

Read the VO alone and you know a man's life story. You don't know:
1. That it's a novel (a stranger could take it for a memoir or a tribute video).
2. That he comes home broken. "The hard part" hints at it, but nothing in the VO says what's wrong.
3. What the book asks. The garage line is an image, not a question.

The shape of the fix: one spoken line between "home was the hard part" and the garage that names what's broken, in plain words, without solving it. That line is the pitch, and right now the cards are carrying it. Facts for that line have to come from Ian or the back cover. The room shouldn't invent the nature of the wound or the trauma.
