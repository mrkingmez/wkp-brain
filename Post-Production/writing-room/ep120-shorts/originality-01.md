# EP120 Shorts Copy, Originality Pass 01

Source: D:\WKP\scratch\ep120-copy-draft.json (23 clips, read in file order, numbered 1 to 23). Draft not edited.
Mode: cold read of public copy, originality standard from WRITING-ROOM.md. Date 2026-11-11.

## BLUF

VERDICT: ships with changes.

None of the 23 clips copies one specific outlet's headline word for word. The closest is Clip 22 YouTube, which repeats the trade headline for the Adam Driver news and adds a stock tail. The bigger problem is sameness. 19 of 23 YouTube titles are built around a question mark, and 12 of 23 Facebook lines open with a question. Five clips use the same "X says Y. [Question]?" shape. Read as a feed, it all sounds like one hook template. Six titles have no host name and no detail of their own, so any movie channel could post them unchanged (Clips 6, 11, 21, 22, 23, and 14 to a lesser degree). Instagram is the strongest platform and is clearly different from the other two. Facebook is often a shortened YouTube description.

Must fix before scheduling:
1. Clip 22 YouTube title (trade headline plus stock tail).
2. Clips 7, 8, 18: three titles on the same news with the same opening ("out at DC"). Read in one week, they look like one post made three times.
3. Clip 7 YouTube title: "KingZ has a word for that" is a withheld-word bait formula. Also, the title states as fact something the description presents only as Oracle's claim (see Fact note).
4. Break up the question-mark run. No more than about half of the YouTube titles should be questions.
5. Clips 6, 11, 21, 23 YouTube titles: give each one a detail only this conversation has.

## Pass 1: Premise (is the hook doing work, or is it a stock formula)

Stock clickbait formulas, by clip and platform:

- **Clip 1, YouTube title.** "Is a DCU reboot the answer?" is the standard op-ed headline shape ("Is X the answer?"). The second half ("KingZ is done with three origin stories") belongs to this show. Keep the second half and drop the stock first half.
- **Clip 5, YouTube title and Instagram.** "DC has three heroes it cannot get wrong. Which three?" is the listicle curiosity gap ("X has three Y. Which three?"). This is legitimate under the teaser rule, but it is the most recognizable YouTube formula in the batch. Instagram repeats it ("Which three does KingZ mean?"). Facebook is the same move again.
- **Clip 7, YouTube title.** "KingZ has a word for that" and Instagram "a one-word take" is the withheld-word bait formula. Viewers expect a single loaded word. If the clip does not land one clear word, the comments will call it bait.
- **Clip 18, Facebook "Fair or not?" and Instagram "Where do you land?"** These are stock engagement prompts that fit any news post on any page.
- **Clip 20, YouTube "Have you?" and Facebook "Have you seen Jimmy?"** A stock engagement tail. Instagram "No argument on this one" has the same purpose and belongs to this show. Use that idea on all three.
- **Clip 22, YouTube title.** "Adam Driver says Star Wars is over" is the trade headline itself. Matching outlet headlines include "It's Over" - Adam Driver Confirms His 'Star Wars' Exit (Netflix Junkie), Adam Driver Declares Star Wars Career Is Over (Geeks and Gamers), and Adam Driver Done With Star Wars (Cosmic Book News). "Where does he go from here?" is the stock follow-up line from news writing. A viewer would read this as an aggregator post, not a show clip. The X-Men turn and Oracle's view are the parts this show owns, and the title uses neither.
- **Clip 16, YouTube title.** "Have to or get to?" is a well-known reframe from motivational and devotional writing ("change I have to into I get to"). No one owns it, and it fits the Greatest Generation topic, so this is not a finding. Note only: some viewers will recognize the phrase as a self-help line.

## Pass 2: Structure (repeated hook construction across clips)

- **Question saturation, YouTube.** Titles with a question: Clips 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23 (19 of 23). Only 3, 7, 12, and 19 are statements, and those four are among the strongest titles in the set.
- **The "statement. question?" template.** Clips 5, 8, 14, 18, 22 (and 20 with "Have you?") all use a declarative sentence followed by a short question. Clips 8 and 18 also share the same opening words: "Gunn is out at DC." and "Gunn and Safran are both out at DC."
- **Question openers, Facebook.** Clips 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 14, 15, 16, 20 open with a question (12 of 23).
- **"[Host] on why/how/what," Instagram.** Clips 6 ("KingZ on why"), 13 ("KingZ on why"), 16 ("Oracle and KingZ on what"), 17 ("Oracle on how"), and 21 ("KingZ on the question"). The same closer appears five times.
- **"The one X" withhold.** Clip 7 Instagram ("one-word take"), Clip 8 YouTube description ("names the one he thinks could be in doubt"), Clip 9 Instagram ("the big name he rules out first"), Clip 11 Instagram ("one plan missing"), Clip 15 Instagram ("what he saw in one"), Clip 16 YouTube description ("the one sign that would worry him"), and Clip 19 YouTube description ("one-line reply"). Each one follows the teaser rule, but seven in one batch turns into a recognizable tic.
- **The disagreement frame.** Clip 2 YouTube and Facebook ("do not agree"), Clip 23 YouTube description ("Oracle does not agree"), Instagram ("Oracle does not buy it"), and Facebook ("Oracle says no"), plus Clips 4 and 18 Facebook ("weigh in"). "Weigh in" appears twice on Facebook and is generic.
- **Topic clustering.** Seven DC clips (1, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 18), three on the same Gunn exit (7, 8, 18), and four on Jimmy (15, 17, 20, 21). This is a scheduling issue more than a copy issue, but the Gunn trio is where the matching titles cause the most harm.

## Pass 3: Character (are the hosts people or types)

- **KingZ and Oracle are people in about half the clips.** They come through as people where the copy uses something only they said or did: Clip 4 (5'3" against 5'7"), Clip 8 (the starting-pitcher comparison), Clip 10 (24 hours of TV against two), Clip 12 (the man on the bike), Clip 13 (training schedule against call sheet), Clip 19 (the Lone Survivor theater story), Clip 3 (Oracle saying up front that she has not read the comics).
- **They become types** where the copy only casts them as "the one who disagrees" or "the one with a take": Clips 2, 18, and 23 on Facebook, and Clip 7 YouTube. Here the names could be swapped for any two co-hosts.

## Pass 4: Texture (lines that could sit unchanged on another channel)

Titles with no host name and no detail of their own:
- **Clip 6, YouTube.** "Why are the most realistic war movies the hardest to watch?" uses the vocabulary of the ranked-list genre ("Most Realistic War Movies", "hardest to watch", in Slashfilm, Cinemablend, WatchMojo, and No Film School lists). It is not a lift from one outlet, but it could run on any of them. The clip's own angle (what he has heard from people who lived through Normandy) is not in the title.
- **Clip 11, YouTube.** "Do Lanterns and Superman look like they belong in the same universe?" reads like a standard Screen Rant or CBR style question. The Aquaman cartoon detail that Facebook uses is more personal.
- **Clip 21, YouTube and Facebook.** "When someone comes home from deployment, do you give them space?" could be a VA, USO, or military-family blog headline unchanged. On Veterans Day it will sit beside exactly that kind of post. The film pairing (Jimmy and American Sniper) and KingZ's own deployment are what make it this show's, and neither is in the title.
- **Clip 22, YouTube.** See Pass 1.
- **Clip 23, YouTube.** "Was Adam Driver the only one in the Star Wars sequels who actually tried?" Searched, and no outlet owns this headline, but it is a common fan-forum take. It is generic, not lifted.
- **Clip 14, YouTube.** "Blade Runner is not an action story" is a common critic's line. "Can a series hold that bar?" saves it somewhat. Borderline.

Platform differentiation:
- **Instagram is clearly different** and is the best writing in the set. It uses short fragments and opens on concrete images (Clip 1 "Superman's origin. The Waynes.", Clip 2 "Decades of one actor in the claws.", Clip 12 "They crossed the road. He turned around.", Clip 17 "Letters to families back home, one after another.").
- **Facebook often shortens the YouTube description** instead of being written fresh: Clip 12 Facebook (nearly the YouTube description), Clip 17 Facebook (the YouTube description with words cut), Clip 13 Facebook, and Clip 21 Facebook.
- **The same line on two platforms:** Clip 19 Instagram and Facebook both end with "Ever miss what a title was telling you?" word for word. The spoiler warnings on Clips 22 and 23 are required and are not a finding.
- The top-level title and description match the YouTube fields on every clip. That is how the file is structured, not a finding.

Fact note (outside the originality lane, send to fact research and clearance): Clip 7's YouTube title states "one Marvel producer in" as fact, while the description says only "Oracle says a Marvel guy." Clips 7, 8, and 18 all treat the exits of Gunn and Safran as confirmed news. This pass did not verify that.

## The counter (strongest case for originality)

This copy is built from what was actually said in the episode, not from a template list. That shows in the best titles: Clip 8's starting pitcher with runners on base, Clip 12's man on a bike, Clip 13's call sheet, Clip 4's four inches, Clip 19's theater line, and Clip 10's 24 hours against two. No other channel could post any of those. They are also the clips that best follow the teaser rule, because they tease a specific moment without giving the verdict. The rumor and spoiler labels are honest and consistent, which a lot of competing pages do not do. Instagram copy reads as written by someone who watched the clips. None of the findings above comes from one outlet's headline being copied. Clip 22, the closest, matches a news headline that everyone covering the story wrote. The fix is to make the other fifteen sound like the best eight, not to start over.
