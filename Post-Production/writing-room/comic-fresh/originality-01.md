## 1. Premise

**Generic logline:** a crew rescues ordinary people who are being turned into stock genre characters by a world that runs on genre rules, while a figure who wants everyone kept in their role closes in.

**Works that fit that sentence:**
- The Cabin in the Woods (2011): people forced into horror archetypes by an outside power.
- The Final Girls (2015): real people trapped inside a slasher, forced into its roles.
- Last Action Hero (1993), Stay Tuned (1992), Pleasantville (1998): ordinary people dropped into genre worlds that run on genre rules.
- Wreck-It Ralph (2012): genre worlds side by side, a character stuck in the role he was built for.
- The Lego Movie (2014): realms split by genre (Old West, Middle Zealand, Bricksburg), and a villain who wants everyone frozen in place following the instructions.
- Westworld (1973 film, 2016 HBO series): genre zoned worlds, people locked in narrative loops.
- Redshirts (John Scalzi, 2012): crewmen realize the story's rules are killing the unnamed ones. Draft-01 Section 1 Pitch A literally names "the crewman nobody names."
- Genrenauts (Michael R. Underwood, Tor.com novellas from 2015): a team travels to worlds that each run on one genre and fixes stories that break. Closest match on structure, low profile.
- Dark City (1998): a city that rearranges itself every night while figures in long coats rewrite who people are.

That list is long, so the premise on its own does no work. **What makes this not that list** is in draft-01 Section 2 "The rules" and Issue 1 P2.4 and P3.4: a blue-collar tow job, a hard clock on a dash card, and the rule that you pull the car to save the person. None of the titles above is a working-class extraction job on a timer. Nobody watching Issue 1 cold will say "this is Genrenauts" or "this is Cabin," because the camera sits in a tow truck cab eating fries. Keep that frame in front of the genre idea on every page and the premise passes.

**Cabin ruling in detail.** The room's case in Section 2 "Originality note for the gate" holds. Cabin's recognizable parts are the control room, the ritual, the specific archetype list and the reveal that it is all staged for a god. COLD TOW has a city like weather, not a staff. The one shared image is someone watching a horror scene play on a screen from a booth while a person in it gets typed (Issue 1 P3.2, Oracle against the drive-in screen showing the fog street; P3.5, the booth watching the next feed). That is the Sitterson and Hadley shot with a kinder watcher. Fix 2 removes it.

## 2. Structure

- **Issue 1 shape** (call, crossing, victim half turned, victim walks off, grab, out, villain looks back): this is the universal rescue shape. It does not follow the reveal order of any one film. Clears.
- **Ten-week arc** (Section 2 "First arc"): villain notices the crew (1), marks appear in a ring (4), a lead nearly turns (5), villain attacks the base (6), base loses sight (7), sacrifice role (9), collection broken and the taken walk home (10). Each beat is common, the sequence is not a known skeleton. Week 10's "smash the villain's collection and everyone she took comes home" is the Ursula garden and Monsters, Inc. door shape, but too generic to be named. Clears.
- **Pitches B and C** were correctly rejected in Section 1 for naming risk (cursed returned tape; Ghostbusters).

## 3. Character

- **KingZ, the talker, and Winter Wolf, the hook** (Section 2 "The three leads"): types on paper (the heart and the one who says no), but they are built from the real hosts' on-air dynamic, warm host and verdict-first host. People, not borrowed archetypes. Clears.
- **Oracle** (Section 2, Section 4): **a type, and a named one.** The name is Gabby's existing handle, so it stays. But her job as written is "wired every traffic cam, toll sensor, police scanner" into one screen, headset on, steering a field team by radio through streets they cannot see. That is Barbara Gordon's Oracle job description, and with the name attached a comics reader names it on sight. The look mitigations in Section 4 (no wheelchair, no tower, no bat cues) are good, but readers identify the role by what she does, not what she wears. Also note Dispatch (AdHoc Studio, October 2025, over a million copies sold) now owns "dispatcher at a desk sending a crew across the city" for a lot of this audience; using "dispatch" as a radio word inside a tow company is native and fine, but do not lead any promo or title with it.
- **Oracle's half mask with a single round camera lens over the right eye** (Section 4): reads as Cyborg (Teen Titans) or Deathstroke at thumbnail size, and duplicates the jeweler's loupe, which already does the "one eye reads the screen" job better.
- **The Callback** (Section 2 "Main threat", Section 4): the casting woman is fresh. "THE CALLBACK" as a person, the grease pencil, the badge lanyard, the line dragging at her heel: none of that is a known villain. What is not fresh is her stated want, "a city where everyone stays in their role." That is Lord Business in The Lego Movie and Christof in The Truman Show, and it is the line a reviewer would quote. Her long coat walking a night city that rearranges itself sits near Dark City's Strangers; the camel color, no hat and flat shoes keep it clear. Do not move her to black coat and hat.
- **Dale** (Issue 1): a person. The flashlight with the tag still on is his.

## 4. Texture

- **The Fog Ward reads as Silent Hill.** Section 2 "The wards" plus Issue 1 P1.5, P2.1, P2.6, P3.3: thick gray fog, a whole street gone colorless, phones losing signal, a man walking off with a flashlight toward a sound, and "a shape far too tall to be a person" half seen in fog. Each piece is generic. Together they are Silent Hill (fog town, flashlight, radio static, tall shape), with The Mist (the huge thing passing in fog) and John Carpenter's The Fog (the ward's own name) behind it. A nerd-verse audience will name Silent Hill by page 2.
- **Streetlights dying one at a time toward the reader** (Issue 1 P1.5): common horror staging, also echoes the Deluminator opening of Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone. Not named by most readers once the fog is gone. Keep.
- **"tk. tk. tk."** (Issue 1 P2.6): clicking from the dark near a victim sits close to the Clickers in The Last of Us. Low risk on its own, higher next to fog and a flashlight. Advisory.
- **Villain on a surveillance feed looks straight up into the camera at the watcher** (Issue 1 P3.6): a convention, not a lift. It is the most worn beat on the three pages. Advisory only; the clipboard circle around the three sketches is the original part, so the panel works even if her look is less direct.
- **Painted lines on the road as borders** (Section 2): faint echo of Dogville's chalk-line town. No reader will name it. Clears, and it is the strongest image in the draft.
- **Truck half in fog, half in amber** (Issue 1 P1.5): a common split composition. Clears.

## Name checks (web search, 2026-10-30)

- **COLD TOW:** no comic, game, film or series found under that title.
- **THE CALLBACK:** no villain or comic by that name found. Exists only as low-profile film titles: Callback (also The Final Scream, UK horror 2019, actress lured to a fake horror shoot) and The Callback Queen (Irish drama 2013). The 2019 film is horror-adjacent to the casting idea but obscure. Clear for originality.
- **Marrowgate:** no hits. Closest are Harrowgate, a homebrew fantasy city on World Anvil, and the real English town Harrogate. Clear.
- Night tow truck supernatural: nothing found. Nearest are night-driver horror games (Road to Jukai, a taxi driver; Trucking Time). The tow angle is open.

This is an originality finding only. Trademark clearance is the clearance desk's job, not done here.

## Required changes (describe only, the room writes them)

1. **Re-texture the horror ward away from fog.** Give it a signature that is not gray fog, not a flashlight walk, not a giant half-seen shape. Rename it to match. Its job (lights fail, sounds come from behind, people want to investigate) stays. Affects Section 2 "The wards", Section 4 ward tints, Issue 1 P1.5, P2.1, P3.3, P3.4. Dale's flashlight can stay as the joke; the tall shape in fog has to go or change.
2. **Change how Oracle sees.** Drop the traffic cams, toll sensors and scanners. Her sight should come from the drive-in itself, something only a projector and that screen could give her, so the role belongs to this world and not to Gotham. This also removes the Cabin control-room image in Issue 1 P3.2 and P3.5, because she stops watching surveillance feeds. Affects Section 2 Oracle paragraph, Section 4 booth paragraph, Issue 1 P3.2, P3.5, P3.6.
3. **Drop the single camera lens over Oracle's right eye.** Keep the half mask if Gabby wants it, but let the loupe be the one-eye device. Section 4.
4. **Give The Callback a want that is hers, not "everyone stays in their role."** Something only a casting woman would want, tied to the badges and to what she gets when a person is finished. Keep the coat camel and the head bare. Section 2 "Main threat" and the Pitch A threat line in Section 1.

Advisory, not required: soften the click SFX in Issue 1 P2.6 if fog stays in any form; consider a less direct stare in P3.6; Issue 9 title "Lone Wolf" is a stock phrase (Lone Wolf and Cub), fine as a pun but forgettable.

## The counter: strongest case FOR originality

Nothing in COLD TOW can be named by a cold reader as a version of one thing. The pieces that are familiar (genre rules, typecasting, a guide on the radio) are the vocabulary of the whole nerd-verse, which is exactly what a show about movies should be commenting on. The pieces that are specific are new: a night tow contract nobody else bids on, a forty-minute card on the dash, the rule that you pull the car to save the person, painted borders that crawl, and a villain who is a casting woman with a grease pencil and a lanyard of finished people. Issue 1's Dale, holding a flashlight with the price tag on it and asking why he is holding it, is a joke about horror that only this premise could make. The three name checks came back empty. With the fog and the camera feeds out, this is fresh.
