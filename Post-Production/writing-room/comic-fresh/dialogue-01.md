## BLUF

The dialogue mostly holds up. It is lean, nobody explains the forty-minute rule, Wolf is clearly himself, and every balloon is under 12 words. Two real problems.

1. **KingZ is no longer turning into Winter Wolf. He is turning into the guy with the jokes.** Five of his nine balloons are punchlines ("You said plenty last time," "Every single time," "Should you, though?", "He's got a flashlight!", "Honestly? Great question."). Only one balloon shows the warm, asking, neighbor voice (P2.2), and only one has any real mess in it (P2.6). The bible says he keeps people human by asking about their kids. On the page he mostly makes wisecracks.
2. **The clock numbers count two ways.** Wolf counts down ("Thirty-one left"). Oracle counts up ("nine minutes," "Twenty-two on him," "you're at twenty-five"). A phone reader cannot keep track, and "you're at twenty-five" has no clear addressee.

**Single worst offender:** P3.4 KINGZ "Honestly? Great question." This is the one relief beat in the issue. A man who just ran into the fog after a stranger answers with a sitcom tag. Any character in any comic could say it.

## Page 1

**P2. KINGZ: "Fog side? The line was on Pruett last night."**
Light exposition. Both men know the lines move, so this is mostly for the reader. It gets away with it because he is surprised and it is phrased as a question, which is his voice. Keep it. Still, it is the only line on the page that exists to teach a rule. Do not add another one like it.

**P2/P4. WINTER WOLF: "Two blocks." / "Thirty-one left. Plenty."**
Two voices collapsing. Wolf and Oracle are both short and full of numbers on this page. Cover the names and "Two blocks," "Calder now," and "Thirty-one left" could belong to either one. The bible gives Oracle the clock and the map and gives Wolf the verdicts. "Thirty-one left" is also the reader's math lesson (40 minus 9), said to a partner who can already subtract.
Fix shape: Oracle owns every number, Wolf owns the verdict.
PROPOSAL, P3: ORACLE (radio): "Nine minutes on him."
PROPOSAL, P4: WINTER WOLF: "Plenty."
KingZ's "You said plenty last time" still lands, and lands harder off one word.

**P4. KINGZ: "You said plenty last time."**
Good banter and it works. It is still a jab, though, not a question. With the change above it stays. It is the first of his five punchlines, so count it against the budget (see the pattern note).

**P5. KINGZ (small): "Every single time."**
A button line, there to end the slide on a laugh. It is fine as a sigh, but it is his second quip in a row on a page that has not yet shown him being kind or unsure. Wolf could say it just as easily, so the voices swap. Fix shape: make it a worried half-question that only KingZ would ask.
PROPOSAL: KINGZ (small, from inside the cab): "Is it... supposed to do that?"
(Wolf can answer with silence. That silence is his characterization.)

## Page 2

**P1. ORACLE: "Hazards on. That's a good sign."**
The draft's notes call this her dry joke. It does not read as a joke. It reads as information whose reason is never given: why are hazards good? It is fine as dispatch talk. Just do not count on it as Oracle's personality beat. If she needs a dry beat, it should come from her watching from above.
PROPOSAL: ORACLE (radio): "Hazards on. Somebody in there still thinks like a driver."
(12 words. Optional. The original can stay if the reader is supposed to work it out.)

**P2. DALE: "I heard a noise. Out back."**
Keep. "Out back" makes no sense for a man in a minivan, and that is the tell: he is already talking like the homeowner in a horror movie. It is the best line in the issue. Make sure the letterer does not "correct" it.

**P3. KINGZ (off panel): "Should you, though?"**
Winking at the audience. It is a commentary-track joke about horror characters, delivered to a man who is turning. It sounds like a WWD review, not a guy leaning in a car window. The bible gives KingZ a specific method, dumb personal questions that pull people off script, and this is exactly where the reader should see it work.
PROPOSAL: KINGZ (off panel): "Dale. Hey. You got kids? What're their names?"
(Dale hesitating over the names in a later panel would be the horror beat.)

**P4. ORACLE: "Twenty-two on him. That's too fast."**
"That's too fast" is good. It sets up the X without explaining it. The problem is "Twenty-two." It is ambiguous next to Wolf's count-down on page 1. Is it minutes in or minutes left? Thirteen minutes have not passed since page 1, so a reader cannot tell whether the clock jumped (the point) or the writer slipped. Fix shape: one direction, counting up toward 40, and say outright that it jumped.
PROPOSAL: ORACLE (radio): "He just jumped to twenty-two. That's too fast."

**P5. KINGZ (off panel): "Wait. Did somebody mark him?"**
Mild exposition, since it names what the X means. It works because it is a guess, which is KingZ's voice. Keep it.

**P6. KINGZ: "Dale? Dale, no no no."**
Keep. This is the one line where KingZ is actually feeling it rather than performing it. Use it as the model for his other lines.

## Page 3

**P1. KINGZ: "He's got a flashlight!"**
A writer's joke, and it is unclear out loud. Is the flashlight why he has to go, or why Dale is doomed? Under stress a real person bargains or pleads. They do not set up a punchline. It is also another case of KingZ playing to the audience.
PROPOSAL: KINGZ: "Two minutes! Clock me, just two!"
(It asks Wolf for something, which is KingZ's voice. It hands the clock to Wolf and Oracle, where it belongs, and it pays off P4's "you said plenty last time.")

**P2. ORACLE: "Wolf. Sixteen paces, left of the hydrant." / "And you're at twenty-five."**
The first balloon is great dispatch talk. In the second, the addressee is lost. The panel shows the booth, both men are off panel, and "you" could be either of them. Twenty-five also does not fit KingZ, who has been in the fog a few minutes, unless it means the truck's crossing time. Readers will not work that out on a phone.
PROPOSAL: ORACLE (radio): "KingZ. Twenty-five. Move."

**P3. DALE: "I just want to see what it is..." / WINTER WOLF: "No, you don't."**
Keep both. The trailing-off trance and the flat verdict are the two voices working exactly right.

**P4. DALE: "Why am I holding a flashlight?" / KINGZ: "Honestly? Great question."**
Dale's line is perfect. KingZ's is the worst offender (see BLUF). It is a stock sitcom tag, it could come from anyone, and it throws away the only relief beat. A man who just pulled a stranger back from becoming a horror cliche is out of breath and checking whether the guy is still in there.
PROPOSAL: KINGZ: "Doesn't matter. What's your last name, Dale? Say it."
(This also shows the reader the method: names bring people back.)

**P5. ORACLE (to herself): "That's not weather."**
It answers something nobody on the page has said. "Weather" is the bible's framing ("a property of the city like weather"). The reader has never heard the borders called weather, so the line refers to a document they have not read. It is also a bit of a trailer line for the one moment Oracle is supposed to be scared.
Fix shape: react to what she is seeing. Repeat it, don't sum it up.
PROPOSAL: ORACLE (to herself, normal balloon): "She's dragging it. She's dragging the line."

**P6. ORACLE: "Nine. Come home the long way." / KINGZ (radio, small): "Why the long way?"**
Keep both. She stays professional while she is being watched, and that says a lot about her. KingZ asking and not getting an answer is the right ending. Do not add a reply.

## Pattern note: KingZ

The brief was to stop KingZ collapsing into Winter Wolf. That is fixed: Wolf never asks, and KingZ almost always does. But the draft overcorrected him into the comic relief, which is the exact role he starts turning into in Arc week 5 ("Bit Part"). If he quips this much in Issue 1, week 5 has nothing to undercut. Budget for him: at most one real joke per page. The rest of his lines should ask, guess, stall, or trail off. He has almost no hesitation right now. Not one "uh," restart, or half-finished sentence outside P2.6.

## Pattern note: too clean

Page 1, P2 to P4, runs question, answer, add, question, answer in turn order. On a radio net that is partly fine. One interruption or one ignored question would make it sound like a real cab. Example: Oracle talks over KingZ's "Fog side?" and never acknowledges it.

## Phone readability

- Every balloon is 12 words or fewer. Confirmed, no violations, and none of the proposals go over.
- Pages 1 and 2 each carry about 9 to 10 balloons on a single 4:5 slide. On a phone that is dense. Recommend using the split the draft already offers (1A/1B, 2A/2B) by default, not only "if the letterer needs room."
- P3.5's shift from the radio balloon to the round balloon is the emotional turn, and at phone size it depends on a small zigzag tail. Make the difference louder: a different border weight, or the radio tick removed and the balloon set off clearly.
- P3.6's hook depends on reading three tiny sketches on a clipboard inside a drive-in screen inside a panel. That is art, not dialogue, but if the sketches cannot be read on a phone, the hook has no words to carry it. Consider a close insert.

## HANDOFF

- Clock continuity (P1.3, P2.4, P3.2) is a logic and structure issue as much as a dialogue one. Pass 2 should lock one count direction and a timeline across all three pages.
