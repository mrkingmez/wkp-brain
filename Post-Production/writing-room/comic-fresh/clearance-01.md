## BLUF

- **STOP items: none found in the text as drafted.** No verbatim dialogue from an existing work, no song lyrics, no poems, no rights-holder character or property used as owned. That covers the words on the page only. The art stage can still create a STOP (see the Oracle and truck items) because AI image tools reproduce marks and costumes on their own.
- **Biggest judgment call: Oracle.** The name alone is a flag. The bigger exposure is the role: a figure above the city, wired into every camera and scanner, steering field operatives by radio headset, whom the villain targets because she "knows where Oracle sits." That is the core function of DC's Barbara Gordon as Oracle. The look choices pull away from DC; the job description pulls toward it. Not recommending a rename, per instruction. Described in Section 2 below.
- **Real people:** three living people (Zac, Matt, Gabby) speak invented lines and are shown in invented danger. Written consent from all three is the control, not a courtesy. Gabby's handle and likeness are hers.
- **Ownership is the quietest risk and maybe the largest:** the script text and any AI art may carry little or no copyright protection as things stand, and nobody has written down who owns the new characters (Matt is a partner, Gabby owns her persona, Zac runs WKP).
- **Name searches** (web only, not a trademark register search): no existing comic, film, series or book titled COLD TOW, no villain called THE CALLBACK, no work called Marrowgate found. Close neighbors exist (Marblegate webcomic, Harrowgate novel). A web search is not a clearance search.
- **Parody:** this is not parody and should not be labeled parody. It is genre commentary on tropes, which is closer to satire. It does not need a parody defense because it borrows no specific work's expression, but calling it parody would invite the wrong test.

## 2. Judgment calls

### 2.1 Oracle and DC's Barbara Gordon (Section 2 "Oracle, dispatch"; Section 4 "Oracle"; Issue 1 P3 P2, P3 P5, P3 P6; arc weeks 6, 7, 10)

**Background.** Oracle is Gabby's existing real show handle, used before this comic. Oracle is also a long-running DC Comics identity for Barbara Gordon (former Batgirl) as an information broker and mission coordinator. Not recommending a rename. Flagging so Zac knows exactly where it sits.

**Name.** "Oracle" is a common English word and is used by many unrelated businesses (Oracle Corporation is the largest). A single common word used as a host's long-standing handle is a weaker claim against her than a name invented fresh for a comic. Not verified: whether DC holds a registered trademark on ORACLE for comics or entertainment goods. That is a register search question for an attorney.

**Role. This is where the exposure is.** The draft's Oracle:
- sits above the city and sees all of it through traffic cams, toll sensors and police scanners (Section 2)
- guides two field operatives by radio headset turn by turn, including blind navigation (arc week 7)
- is the only one "who knows where everyone is" (Section 2)
- is a target the villain locates and climbs toward (arc week 6, Issue 1 P3 P6 hook "she knows where Oracle sits")

Barbara Gordon's Oracle is defined by exactly that job: a hidden hub, city-wide surveillance, headset, steering heroes in the field, a base the villains try to find. The draft's choices are original in their details (drive-in projection booth instead of a clock tower, projector instead of monitor wall, tow crew instead of vigilantes). The combination of that name with that function is what a reader is most likely to connect. Ideas and job roles are not owned by anyone; the risk is less a copyright claim and more (a) a trademark or confusion argument tied to the name in a comics context, and (b) readers and DC's people reading it as a borrowed character. Zac should know the role choice increases the read, and the name is fixed.

**Look choices, one by one (Section 4):**
- **Headset.** The single strongest visual echo, because DC's Oracle is routinely drawn in a headset. Here it is a dispatcher's over-ear radio headset with a taped cracked ear cup, which reads as tow-company dispatch, not tech hub. Still the shared element.
- **Half mask with a single camera-style lens over the right eye.** Barbara Gordon as Oracle is not typically masked; her Batgirl identity uses a cowl. The half mask moves away from DC, not toward it. The single lens eye is not a DC Oracle signature that this agent knows of. Flag only that a domino-style mask on a red-haired woman would push back toward Batgirl; the draft avoids hair color entirely, so the artist should not default to red hair.
- **Booth.** Concrete projection booth at a dead drive-in, amber light, switch boxes and coffee cans of jacks. Distinct from the clock tower and computer banks. Helps.
- **Backlit silhouette, scarf, fingerless gloves, loupe.** None of these are DC Oracle markers. Helps.
- **No wheelchair.** The draft bars it explicitly. That removes the most identifying Barbara Gordon element. It also means nothing in the design should be read as a comment on disability.
- **Arc week 10 clue: a headset badge on The Callback's clipboard.** This makes the headset the Oracle emblem inside the strip, which strengthens the headset as her identifier. Worth knowing; not a STOP.

**Where it sits:** name fixed and pre-existing; look leans away from DC; role leans toward DC. That tension is for Zac and, if the strip ever goes to print or merch, an attorney.

### 2.2 Real people: KingZ, Winter Wolf, Oracle (all of Issue 1; arc weeks 5, 9)

All three leads are living people performing personas: Zac (KingZ), Matt (Winter Wolf), Gabby (Oracle). Every line they speak is invented by the room, and the arc puts them in invented situations.
- **Consent.** Written yes from each, covering invented dialogue, invented conduct, and future issues, not just Issue 1. Draft Section 5 item 1 already lists Matt and Gabby. Add Zac to the paper too, since partnership ownership is in play.
- **Defamation / false light.** Nothing in Issue 1 is damaging. Watch the arc: week 5 has KingZ turned into "the comic relief who never gets the girl"; week 9 has Wolf nearly sacrificing himself. Both are flattering or self-deprecating as written. The flag is for later issues: never put a real host in a criminal, sexual, or humiliating act they have not approved.
- **Right of publicity.** Georgia recognizes a right of publicity. A handle and a recognizable persona can carry it, not just a face. Gabby's handle "Oracle" and her look are hers to license, even though she is drawn as a silhouette and masked. Section 4 says "no panel imposes a face on Gabby" and "Gabby approves the look"; that approval should be in writing.
- **If any of the three leaves the show,** what happens to their character in the comic needs an answer in the same document.

### 2.4 Truck Nine and vehicles (Issue 1 P1 P1, P2 P1, P3 P4; Section 4 "Truck Nine")

The draft bars real manufacturer badges, grilles and logos. Flags:

- No real tow company name or livery. "9" in block numbers is fine.

## 3. Parody vs satire

The draft says "never a parody of one specific movie" (Section 1, Tone). Correct read. COLD TOW comments on genre conventions in general and on typecasting, using invented wards rather than any one film. That is closer to satire or plain genre commentary than to parody. If a later issue does borrow a specific film's look or line to mock it, that issue gets its own clearance read, and the parody-vs-satire line becomes live.

## 5. AI art, protectability and ownership (Section 4; Section 5 item 5)

- **AI-generated images.** US Copyright Office guidance (2023 registration guidance, January 2025 report on copyrightability) and the D.C. Circuit's 2025 ruling in Thaler v. Perlmutter hold that copyright needs human authorship. Prompting alone has been treated as not enough. The Zarya of the Dawn decision (2023) protected the human-written text and the selection and arrangement of a comic, not the AI images themselves. Practical exposure: someone could copy the panel art and WKP may have little to enforce.
- **The script text.** This draft was written in GENERATE mode by the writing room, which is AI. The same human-authorship issue applies to the words until Zac, Matt or Gabby materially rewrite them. Human editing, selection and arrangement improve the position. How much is enough is an attorney question.
- **Characters.** The Callback, Dale, Truck Nine, Marrowgate and the wards are new. KingZ, Winter Wolf and Oracle are existing personas of real people. Who owns each needs a written answer: WWD partnership (Zac and Matt), WKP (Zac), or the individual. Gabby is not a partner as far as the repo shows, and her persona is hers. Draft Section 5 item 5 lists this as open; it should be closed before Issue 1 posts, not after it succeeds.
- **Tool terms.** Firefly's commercial-use terms and any indemnity cover outputs from that tool only, under Adobe's conditions. Any other tool (Higgsfield, partner models inside Firefly) has its own terms. Check which model made each panel; partner models inside Firefly may not carry Adobe's terms.

## 6. Meta platform rules on AI disclosure (Instagram and Facebook)

From secondary sources only; an official 2026 Meta help-center page was not retrieved, so verify before posting.
- Meta applies an "AI info" label (renamed from "Made with AI" in 2024) and detects industry markers such as C2PA Content Credentials. Firefly embeds Content Credentials, so the comic will likely be auto-labeled.
- Meta's rules require self-disclosure for photorealistic video and realistic-sounding audio that is digitally created or altered. A stylized comic carousel is not plainly in that category, but the "AI info" toggle at upload is the cheap control. The WWD packages already carry an AI disclosure box; same habit here.
- Reports of an account-level "AI creator" label on Instagram exist from one undated secondary source. Not confirmed.
- Real-person issue on platform: Meta also polices manipulated media of real people. The hosts are masked or not human and consent will be on file, which is the right posture.
