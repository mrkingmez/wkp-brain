# Originality pass 1: Den Files "Fire in the Sky" teaser, draft 01

## VERDICT: SHIPS WITH CHANGES

BLUF: the teaser is not a copy of any one work, and its best choice (telling the case through the six crewmen and the fact that they went back, lines 10, 37, 48) is not the default angle. Two things a viewer could name:

1. **The closing binary, line 95** ("Either six men saw something in those trees, or six men kept a secret for fifty years. Pick the one that scares you.") has the exact shape of the famous UFO quote usually credited to Arthur C. Clarke: "Two possibilities exist: either we are alone in the Universe or we are not. Both are equally terrifying." The content is different (truth versus lie, not alone versus not alone). But the structure (either A or B, then which one scares you) on a UFO piece is what a UFO-literate viewer will hear. The attribution is shaky (Quote Investigator finds no confirmed Clarke source), but the line is famous either way, so that changes nothing. Change it: keep the two-option idea, drop the "which one scares you" turn. Or end on the crew without a fear payoff at all.
2. **A run of stock true-crime podcast phrases** (listed under Texture) puts the middle section on a crowded shelf. No single phrase fails alone. Together, lines 37, 44, 52 and 89 sound like every two-host mystery podcast cold open. Swap at least two of them for something only Matt or Zac would say.

Fix those and it ships.

**Genre shelf it could be mistaken for:** the two-host paranormal and unsolved-case show. The video shelf is BuzzFeed Unsolved: Supernatural, which also did an alien-abduction episode ("We Investigated Three Creepy Cases Of Alien Abductions"; I could not confirm Walton is one of the three). On audio, the Walton case alone has been done by Dread: The Unsolved, Paranormal Witness (Syfy, Season 2 "The Abduction"), Monster Fuzz, They Disappeared, Up and Vanished's "UNEXPLAINED: Travis Walton" and a podcast episode literally titled "Five Days Gone". A viewer scrolling Shorts will file this under "another Walton abduction explainer" unless the crew angle and the film-versus-account angle stay up front.

## Pass 1: Premise

Generic logline: "A man vanishes after a UFO sighting, and the witnesses are suspected of lying."
Works that fit: Fire in the Sky (1993) itself, every Walton documentary and podcast listed above, Unsolved Mysteries style UFO segments, The X-Files abduction episodes as a fictional cousin. The list is long, so the premise does no work by itself. That is expected for a true-story show: the case is the case.

What makes it not that list:
- **The crew as the subject, not Walton.** Line 10 opens on the six men, line 48 ("With the sheriff looking at those six guys. Not the sky.") and line 37 (they go back for him) frame it as a story about loyalty and suspicion. Most Walton teasers open on the light or the ship.
- **The film-versus-account hook, lines 71 to 82.** "The thing you picture when somebody says Travis Walton probably isn't what he says happened" is the Den Files format doing its job (story behind the story). This is the piece's real reason to exist and it is not the default Walton angle.

Finding: the premise passes on these two choices. Do not cut either in later drafts.

## Pass 2: Structure

Sequence: cold hook line (10), setting stamp (16), incident in three short beats (26, 33), the return (37), five days (44), suspicion (48), polygraph tease withheld (55 to 68), the film rewrote it (71 to 82), verdict withheld (86 to 95), title and callback (100).

This is the standard true-crime teaser skeleton (hook, date and place, incident, twist, withheld result, CTA). That skeleton is universal and fine. No known film's specific order of reveals is being copied. Fire in the Sky (1993) opens after the event with the crew under suspicion, then flashes back. The teaser runs straight through in time, so it does not mirror the film's structure.

One flag: line 44, "Not that night. Not the next morning. Five days." The countdown tricolon that lands on a number is a stock trailer move, and "five days" is the hinge every Walton telling uses (see the "Five Days Gone" episode title). Not a fail. But this is the one beat where the teaser sounds like the existing coverage instead of like Den Files.

## Pass 3: Character

- **Matt (the one who has read everything and withholds):** a type. Lines 62, 68 and 89 make him the knowing host who won't give the answer. That role is a stock two-host device (the BuzzFeed Unsolved pairing, and most podcast "I'll tell you after the break" hosts). It is saved by the fact that Matt is not cast as the skeptic. Line 93 ("That's a not yet") refuses the believer-versus-skeptic split that the shelf runs on. That refusal is the point of difference. Keep it.
- **Zac (the stand-in for the audience who asks):** also a type, saved by specific behavior. Lines 23 ("Let me get them in the truck first"), 30 ("Of course he does") and 65 ("He does this") are real, specific banter between two friends. They read like people.
- Overall: both are roles you'd find in any two-host show, but they are played by real hosts with a real friendship (since 1992). The banter lines carry that. The verdict lines (89, 95) are where they slide back into type.

## Pass 4: Texture

Lifted or stock lines, by line number:

- **Line 95**, "Pick the one that scares you." This is the Clarke-shape problem above. **Must change.**
- **Line 37**, "this is the part I can't shake." Stock true-crime narration phrase. Change it.
- **Line 89**, "Here's what I can't get past." Stock podcast verdict setup, and it sits close to line 37 (two "can't" lines in one 90 s). Change at least one of these two.
- **Line 52**, "the only story you've got sounds crazy." "Sounds crazy" is the default word for this idea. Weak, but it is the shelf's word.
- **Line 44**, "Not that night. Not the next morning. Five days." Stock countdown cadence (see Structure). Optional.
- **Line 68**, "You want the results, you watch the episode." A generic teaser withhold. Fine as banter and allowed under the teaser-not-verdict gate. Optional.
- **Line 71**, "Hollywood makes the movie." A stock phrase, but harmless and quick. Leave it.

Checked and clear:
- **Line 10**, "Six men drove out of that forest without their friend." Specific to the case. No source found. Strong hook. Keep.
- **Line 48**, "Not the sky." Light wordplay, specific to the case, reads as Den Files. Keep.
- **Line 100**, "Get in the truck." A callback to line 23. I considered "Get in, loser" (Mean Girls) and rejected it: the wording and the context are too far apart, and you can't put a viewer's recognition on it. Keep.
- No dialogue from Fire in the Sky (1993) appears (checked against the writer note at line 107; the film's script was not opened, so this rests on the draft plus my reading).

Pair check (lesson from 2026-11-20): lines 79 and 82 together ("So the thing you picture... Probably isn't what he says happened") make a clean logline for the episode and are not a stock pairing. Lines 93 and 95 together are where the Clarke shape forms. "That's a not yet" alone is fine.

## The counter: the strongest case for originality

This is a true case, so the facts can't be original, and the shelf being crowded is not the script's fault. Inside that limit, the draft makes three choices the existing coverage mostly does not. It tells the story from the crew's side: the six men, the sheriff watching them, the fact that they went back. It points at the gap between the 1993 film and Walton's own account instead of re-telling the film. And it builds the teaser out of two friends' real banter ("Let me get them in the truck first", "He does this") instead of a narrator voice. A cold viewer would most likely place this as "a podcast clip about Fire in the Sky", not as a copy of a named show. The only line a viewer could actually name a source for is line 95. Everything else is shelf texture, not lifting. If the closer is rebuilt and two of the stock phrases go, this is a fresh piece on a familiar case.
