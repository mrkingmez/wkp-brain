# THE WRENCH TURNER trailer: Originality pass 1 on script Draft 01
Judged: Post-Production\reports\ian-trailer\work\draft-01.md (Concept A "The Ball", GENERATE mode). Prior verdict checked: scratch\ian\originality-01.md (2026-10-23).
Date: 2026-11-04. Mode: report only. No lines rewritten, fixes described in the abstract.

## VERDICT: SHIPS WITH CHANGES

BLUF
- All seven required changes from the concept round were made, or are correctly parked as pending Ian. The middle of the trailer no longer reads as veteran-tribute stock. That problem is solved.
- The draft has a new problem, and a cold viewer can name it. The present-day tap (beat 9, 0:44.0 to 0:47.0) now reads as a horror beat: a ball rolls in from off frame, with no source, to a man in a dim room, and he freezes. That is the ghost-ball scene from **The Shining** (1980: a yellow tennis ball rolls to Danny down an empty hallway) and **The Changeling** (1980: a grieving father, his dead daughter's red rubber ball bouncing down the stairs). The draft's default ball spec is "about tennis-ball size" (draft line 24). The father is dead by beat 8a. So a cold viewer's most likely reading is that the father's ghost sent it. That is the wrong genre, and it points away from the book's real secret.
- The tap was clean at concept stage. What made it nameable is the draft's packaging: the no-source entry, the dark framing, the freeze, and the cello cutting out into silence (0:45.0). Change the register around the tap and it goes back to being the best asset in the package.
- The visitor log (beat 7a, 0:27.0 to 0:30.0) is still original as an image. But its staging, a page turn revealing the same handwriting filling the next page, is the reveal shape of the "All work and no play" pages in **The Shining**. Same film twice in one trailer. That is enough to push it toward haunted.
- The ratchet spine, the cello note and the ratchet-to-monitor bridge are clean. Nothing nameable.

## Pass 1: Premise

Generic logline is unchanged: a soldier comes home broken, loses his father, and finds his way back under the old car. The list is unchanged too: Gran Torino, the Chevrolet holiday ads ("Holiday Ride" 2021, "A Holiday to Remember" 2023, "The Sanctuary" 2024), Field of Dreams.

What gets this script off that list is the same thing as before: the book's bookend ball, plus withholding who rolled it. Draft 01 adds a second premise-level risk. The way it withholds now opens a different list: **grief plus an object that arrives from nowhere equals a haunting.** The Changeling is the closest single match there, because its protagonist is also a grieving man and its ball belonged to a dead family member. The script has to withhold the source without suggesting the supernatural.

## Pass 2: Structure

- The middle order is now boyhood, party, barracks, Germany, sand, hospital, home, present (beats 2 to 9). It is still a chronological life montage, but the stock tribute stations are gone (bag, rotor, flag). Each station is now a book-specific object passed along by the ball. No nameable skeleton. Clean.
- The bookend, ball under the car at 0:00 and ball taps the man under the car at 0:45.5, is the book's own structure. No other work owns it. Clean.
- The ending sequence has a familiar shape: a drone or low note, then a cut to silence half a second before the impact (beat 9, 0:45.0 to 0:45.5). That is standard trailer "suck-out" grammar, so no single source. Its job here is the problem: in horror trailers the suck-out sets up the scare, so here it tells the viewer the tap is a scare. See T1.

## Pass 3: Character

- **Grown Brandon (beat 9).** He is no longer Gran Torino, so change 5 holds. But the reaction direction now risks a new type, the man in a horror film who senses a presence behind him. The direction is "His wrench stops in the air. He goes still. He does not turn his head." (line 38), with "his face half in shadow." Each choice is fine alone. Together they play dread, not recognition.
- **The father (beats 2, 7b, 8a).** Still a type in SAFE (see change 6). The visitor log makes him specific. The gray hands folded in the visitor chair (7b) are a type image, but it is brief.
- **The mother (beat 8b).** Still a person. This is the best character beat in the draft.
- **The girlfriend (beat 4) and the woman in Germany (beat 5b).** Both are brief types. They are acceptable at this length and true to the book.

## Pass 4: Texture

**T1. The tap staged as a ghost ball. Beat 9, 0:44.0 to 0:47.0, plus line 24 and line 44. REQUIRED CHANGE.**
- Nameable matches. **The Shining** (1980): a yellow tennis ball rolls to Danny from off screen, down an empty hallway, and he looks up at nothing. It is one of the most-discussed shots in the film. **The Changeling** (1980): a grieving father, and a dead child's red rubber ball that keeps bouncing down the stairs with nobody there. It is often called one of the scariest scenes in horror. "A ball rolls to someone from nowhere" is a ghost-film convention, and these are its two famous instances.
- What in Draft 01 creates the match:
  - The tennis-ball default spec (line 24). That is literally The Shining's ball.
  - The rule list (line 44): no footstep, no shadow, the door kept out of frame. The rule exists to protect the secret, but on screen it reads as "no living source."
  - The dim garage with his face half in shadow.
  - He freezes.
  - The cello cuts out into near silence for the tap (0:45.0).
  - The dusty dead-father ratchet two beats earlier (8a), which gives the viewer a ghost candidate.
- Why it matters beyond embarrassment: the trailer promises a haunting, and the book's secret is a living grandson. A viewer who buys it for a ghost story is disappointed. A viewer who wants a veteran's family novel scrolls past a horror trailer.
- Fix, in the abstract. Keep every fence: no child, no second person, the secret never shown. Change the register, not the event.
  - (a) Do not default to a tennis ball or a red rubber ball. Get Ian's book spec (Depends item 4). If you have to pick, pick a kind of ball that carries neither association.
  - (b) Give the present-day frame signs of ordinary living daytime just outside the frame, through light and ambient sound, so that "someone is out there" reads as life, not as a presence. This can be done without any silhouette, footstep or voice tied to the ball.
  - (c) Do not let the score do a horror suck-out into dead silence under the tap. The near-silence idea can stay if what surrounds it is warm rather than empty.
  - (d) The reaction should read as recognition, the boyhood memory landing, rather than alarm. The concept's "he does not turn" can survive. What has to change is what the stillness signals.

**T2. Visitor log page turn. Beat 7a, 0:27.0 to 0:30.0. REQUIRED CHANGE (staging only).**
- The image itself, one signature on every line of a hospital visitor log, is still original. I can find no source, which confirms the concept-round finding.
- The staging is the problem: "A page turns; the same signature fills the next page." That reveal shape, turn the page and the same handwriting repeats again, is the "All work and no play makes Jack a dull boy" pages reveal in **The Shining**. In a calm trailer it might pass. Here it sits two beats after a hard silence (0:23.0 to 0:25.0) and seven seconds before T1, so it reads as obsessive or uncanny, not devoted.
- Fix, in the abstract: let the image say "time passing, he kept coming" through progression on a single page, such as dates or wear, rather than through a flip that reveals repetition.

**T3. Party heads turning. Beat 4, 0:11.0 to 0:16.0. NOT a finding on its own. Contributing cue.**
- Silhouetted heads turning toward the lens one after another while the sound sucks out. It is not one nameable work. The comedy version (the room goes quiet, everyone stares) and the horror version (a crowd turns in unison toward the protagonist, Get Out's garden party being the nearest recent example) are both trope-level. I am not calling it a match.
- Note only: it is one more beat in the same uncanny register as T1 and T2. If the turn reads as humiliation (the viewer has clearly seen the hand slide into another hand first), it is fine. If it reads as a staggered wave aimed at the lens, it adds to the haunted read.

**T4. Ratchet-stops sound spine. Beats 1, 2, 6, 9, 11. CLEAN.**
- The ratchet stops mid-turn in the sand (0:23.0), two seconds of silence, then a monitor comes in at the ratchet's tempo (0:25.0). This is a sound bridge, which is common grammar. The famous named instance is Apocalypse Now's rotor-to-ceiling-fan, and this draft uses neither the rotor sound nor that transition. I can find no ad, trailer or film that uses a working tool's rhythm as the father's heartbeat and then hands it to a hospital monitor. Silence followed by a monitor starting is the reverse of the stock flatline, which helps.
- The ratchet resuming slower in grown Brandon's hand (0:39 onward) and the single click on the title (beat 11) are clean. This is the strongest new texture in the draft.

**T5. Cello note. Line 9, beats 9 to 11. CLEAN as an instrument choice.**
- It meets change 3. A single sustained low cello note is not Jaws, which is a two-note ostinato, and not Americana. A low drone under the climax is generic trailer grammar, not one owned source. The only issue is how it cuts out before the tap, covered under T1(c).

**T6. Watch items, not findings.**
- Beat 6, 0:21.0 to 0:27.0, the motionless boot heel. Keep it a heel at the edge of frame. If the shot ever reads as an empty upright boot in the sand, it becomes the battlefield cross, which belongs on the stock-iconography list with the flag.
- Beat 8a, the dusty ratchet on the bench. A dead relative's untouched tools in a garage is the grammar of the Chevrolet holiday ads ("Holiday Ride", "The Sanctuary"). With the acoustic guitar gone, the risk is low. Just do not pair it with warm golden-hour light on a classic car body.
- Beat 5b, line 34. The staging calls for "wet cobblestones," and the sound note says "Keep it dry: no rain." That is an internal contradiction. Wet cobblestones are also the stock set for a European farewell. Dry stone is the less familiar choice.
- Narration L1, line 109 (and L1a, line 121). The "Before X, he was Y" opening is a stock biopic-trailer construction. It is a trope, not one work, and it is mild. The SLOT version softens it, because Ian's book line follows immediately. Flagged so it is a conscious choice. L2's opening fragment, "Two years.", is mildly trailer-ish. Not a finding.
- Card A, line 149, "even something broken can be fixed". This is Ian's verbatim back cover, so it is not ours to change. As a phrase it is common in self-help and repair-metaphor copy, so it does no originality work. The script around it has to carry that load.

## The counter (strongest case FOR originality)

Everything that carries the weight of this trailer comes from the book, and nobody else has it. The ball under the car at the start and at the end is Ian's own bookend. The floor-level camera height, about the height of a creeper, still has no source I can find. The ratchet as the father's heartbeat, stopping mid-turn in the sand and handed to a hospital monitor at the same tempo, is a genuinely new sound idea. It replaces the most generic ten seconds of the concept round with the most specific ten seconds of the script. The visitor log, as an image, is still unique. The mother's look in 8b is a person, not a type. Every stock station flagged in October is gone, and gone cleanly, not swapped for a near cousin.

The ghost-ball read is real, but it comes from execution choices: a spec default, the lighting, the score's cut to silence, the direction for his reaction. It is not in the idea. In the book this beat is a reunion, not a haunting, and both 1980 films use the ball to announce a dead child. Once the frame around the tap reads as daylight and life, The Shining and The Changeling drop away, and what is left is a trailer no other book could have.
