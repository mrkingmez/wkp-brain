## Part 1. 01-script-and-beat-sheet.md

### 1.1 L2 "Two years. His dad was there." (Section 3 base cut table; 2a beat 7)

**Holds up.** Read aloud in the author register it sounds like a man saying it, not writing it. The clipped fragment "Two years." then the count is how people actually state a hard fact. Dropping "for all of it" helps: the old tail explained the feeling, the short form leaves it unsaid and lets the hands carry it.

Timing checks out against the file's own method. "Two years." is 2 words, about 0.9 s, ends about 0:29.3; the count runs to about 0:30.3; "His dad was there." is 4 words, about 1.7 s, ends 0:32.0. That matches the stated start of about 0:30.3 and the window end exactly. Zero slack at 2.3 words per second (see 2.2 for what that means for a slower read).

One thing working, not a flag: "His dad was there." followed 1.2 s later by "He came home to his dad's tools." repeats "his dad" back to back. That repetition is right. Present, then only his tools. Do not let a later polish pass "vary" it to "his father" in one place (Section 3 already requires one word throughout).

Watch item, not a flag: with "for all of it" gone, "there" leans fully on picture 7a and 7b to mean the hospital. In the sound version that works because the line lands as 7b cuts in. In the silent version it depends on 1.2 below.

### 1.3 C4 "He came home to his dad's tools." 0:33.5 to 0:36.0 (Section 2d; change log item 11)

## Part 2. 07-voiceover-east-texas.md

### 2.1 Words: unchanged (Section 2a table, 2c, 4)

Checked every line against 01 Section 3 and 2b, letter for letter: L1a, L2, L3, L4, L4-cut all match. The recording order (L1a, L2, L3, L4, L4-cut) matches 01's recording spec. No dialect spelling, no filler, no added "ain't" or "-in'". The cues stay in sound only. Pass.

### 2.2 Window-fit numbers (Section 2a and BLUF)

The arithmetic is right. I re-ran every row at both speeds and got the same ends: L1a about 0:06.6, L2 about 0:32.4, L3 about 0:40.6 (first sentence about 0:36.7, breath to about 0:37.1), L4 about 0:44.3, L4-cut about 0:09.8. Where I disagree is in how the results are filed and framed:

- **BLUF misfiles L4-cut.** BLUF says "At a slower 2.0 words per second, L1a, L3 and L4-cut do not fit their windows" and puts L2 and L4 in the "past the window but inside the picture" group. The line-by-line notes say L4-cut "fits the cut, at a cost", and 01 2b explicitly accepts an end at 0:09.8. At 2.0 words per second all five lines overrun their windows; what separates them is the hard edge. L4-cut belongs with L2 and L4. Only L1a (in SLOT) and L3 hit a hard edge.
- **L1a "HARD" is true for SLOT, firm for the base cut.** In SLOT, 0:06.3 to 0:06.8 is the speaker-change cue and an overrun breaks it. In the base cut the hand-off "plays on sound alone" and the ratchet resumes at 0:07.5, so a 0.3 s overrun muddies the clink but does not break a speaker change. The sheet should say which version makes it hard.
- **C3 is not a constraint on the voice.** The L2 row lists "Caption C3 ends 0:32.3" as part of the hard edge. C1 to C6 are the silent cut only. A long L2 take does not collide with C3. If anything, the .srt for sound versions has to be timed to the real take, not to C3.
- **2.0 words per second as "the drawl pace" is an assumption.** UNVERIFIED: Southern speakers are often measured as somewhat slower on average, but by how much varies widely by speaker. Note also that 2.3 words per second (about 138 words a minute) is already an unhurried read. The sheet labels its numbers as estimates, which is honest, but the BLUF line "A drawl slows you down" plus the 2.0 column reads as if the size of the slowdown were known.
- **Word counts hide the real tightest line, and the sheet still ranks it right.** L1a and L4 both count as 7 words, about 3.0 s, but L1a has 9 syllables and L4 has 7. L1a is tighter than the table shows and L4 is roomier. The sheet calls L1a "the tightest line" and L4 "the roomiest", which is correct, just for a reason beyond its own math. Worth one sentence so the performer trusts it.

### 2.3 Does the delivery guidance read like a real person or a performance?

**The worst offender: the sheet assumes the accent is Zac's own.** Section 4 Take 1: "Read every line in your natural drawl." Section 3 pen/pin row: "If your natural speech does it on 'went', that is fine." Nothing in the project files says Zac's natural speech is East Texas, or a drawl at all. If it is his own speech (Georgia or wherever he grew up), the honest instruction is "talk the way you talk", and East Texas specifics are beside the point. If he has to reach for East Texas, then every row of Section 3 is a list of features to put on, and the sheet's own test ("If you can hear yourself 'doing the voice', back off by half") will fail by design. The sheet should name this fork plainly before any cue, and tell him that if it is not his own voice, Take 1 is his own voice with no accent work at all. This sits next to WRENCH-2026-11-05-01 (who narrates) and does not replace it.

**L2 is told to be flat and then given three stretches.** 2c L2: "plain, almost flat. This line is a fact, not a feeling." Then: "'years' can take two beats... 'dad' can stretch the 'a' a touch. 'there' keeps its r and lands." Three stretched words in a six-word line is not flat. On this line in particular, the flatness is the feeling. A two-beat "years" plus a broken "dad" is where a listener first hears an accent being done.
PROPOSAL: on L2, one stretch at most ("years", lightly), and "His dad was there." with no stretch at all.

**Cues that tip toward caricature if pushed:**
- Section 3, "a" breaking row: "On 'dad' in L2 and 'back' in L4 it can carry the whole accent by itself." That invites loading one word with the whole accent. A broken "dad" is one of the most imitated Southern sounds. PROPOSAL: drop "carry the whole accent by itself"; keep it at "lightly".
- 2c L3 and Section 3: the flat "i" on "polite". "polite to him" is the line that "should hurt a little". A flattened "po-laht" on the most tender word in the script is where an accent turns into comedy. The sheet does warn "Do not push it into 'po-lot'". PROPOSAL: on "polite", natural speech only, no added flattening.
- 2c L1a and L4: "Stretch 'car'... keep the r" next to "Stop dead after 'car.'" and "Do not trail off". A stretched, r-colored "car" tends to trail. The cue should say stretch the vowel, then close the r clean and stop.

**Cues that are good and should stay:** the "Against caricature" paragraph; "Calm and plain beats thick and colorful every time"; "Never fix a long take by speeding up"; letting small words run together; "to him" going soft with the h nearly gone (that is ordinary English, not an accent effect, and it sounds like a person). The three-take plan (natural, window fit, calm and clean) with a hard stop is sensible.

**Practical note:** Take 2 asks him to time each line "with a stopwatch or phone timer" while reading it. Nobody can read a 3 s line and run a stopwatch at once without the stopwatch shaping the read. Timing from the voice memo playback afterward gets the same number without it.

### 2.4 Dialect claims (Section 3 table)

All of the following are my general knowledge, UNVERIFIED, not checked against a source in this run.

| Row | Claim | My read |
|---|---|---|
| Long, flat "i" | Flattened even before t in rural East Texas | Plausible. Texas and inland Southern speech is commonly described as flattening this vowel before voiceless consonants more than other Southern areas. The sheet's hedge is right. See 2.3 on not using it on "polite". |
| Stretched stressed vowels | The drawl | Plausible as described. |
| "a" breaking | On dad, back, Brandon | Plausible as a Southern feature. Caricature risk noted in 2.3. |
| Fronted "oo" | On Two, grew; not before l in "tools" | Plausible, and the "not before l" exception is a real pattern. Note this fronting is now widespread across American English, so it adds little regional color. |
| "home" and "mom" | "mom" opens to a long "ah" | **Overclaimed.** For most American speakers "mom" already has the "ah" vowel. It is not an East Texas marker. Listing it as one invites him to exaggerate an ordinary word. "home" gliding is plausible, by ear, as the sheet says. |
| Rhotic r | "Texas speech keeps its r's... Dropping the r reads as a different region." | **Slightly overclaimed.** Safe advice for a modern speaker, but older rural East Texas speech was historically described as partly r-less, in line with the Lower South. The instruction (keep the r) is fine; the absolute wording is not. |
| Unstressed second syllable of "Brandon" | Listed as a feature | **Not a dialect feature.** Every English speaker reduces it. Fine as a timing cue, wrong as a regional feature in this table. Move it to 2c. |
| Pen/pin merger | "went" may sound like "win" | Plausible and correctly hedged. Correct that no word pair in the script can be misheard. |
| "ain't" | Not in the script | True. The row adds nothing a performer needs; it reads as padding. Harmless. |
| Softened "-ing" | No -ing word in the script | Correct, checked all five lines. |
