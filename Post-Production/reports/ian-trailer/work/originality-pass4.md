## 3. Character

No character changed. The narrator is now possibly Zac in an East Texas voice (07, Section 1). That raises one type question:

- **The wise plainspoken heartland narrator** is a type, and a commercial one. Nameable instance: Ram Trucks "So God Made a Farmer" (2013), a dead radio man's slow, warm, preaching cadence over stills of hands at work. Also widely associated, not searched this run: Matthew McConaughey's Lincoln campaign (from 2014), a slow Texas voice paired with a car. A man under a classic car plus a drawl is close enough to both that an overdone read would make a viewer say "truck commercial" or "car ad", which is the wrong genre for a book trailer.
- What keeps it a person: third person, short flat facts ("His dad was there." read "almost flat", 07 Section 2c), no sermonizing, no second-person address, no "you", and 07 Section 3's rule "if you can hear yourself doing the voice, back off by half." Harvey's piece is a list built on anaphora ("So God made..."); these lines have no repetition device at all. McConaughey's spots are first-person musing at the wheel; this is a narrator who never says "I".

Grown Brandon, mom, dad: unchanged, still people by the pass 3 read.

## 4. Texture

**L2 "Two years. His dad was there." (01, Section 3).** Two-word time fragment, a beat, a short plain sentence. That shape is common trailer grammar with no single owner. No known tagline or audiobook line matched. The short form actually reads less like trailer copy than the old "was there for all of it", which leaned toward a stock tribute phrase. Not a finding.

**Does the shorter L2 make the tap read as a ghost or sign?** No. L2 sits at 0:28.4 in the master, 14 s and three beats before the tap, and L2 is not in the 15 s cut at all. It cannot drive the tap's read. The shorter line slightly weakens "dead" at the hospital (it says he was present, not for how long he lasted), but the dead-or-away read is carried by the broken-off log and dust (8a), which is unchanged, and question 2 of the cold-viewer test checks it. That is a clarity issue, not originality.

**Narration register (01 Section 3; 07 Sections 2 to 4).** "Author reading his own book", plain, third person, no announcer voice. The famous trailer voice cliche (the late Don LaFontaine "In a world" register) is explicitly ruled out. Audiobook cliche check: the known audiobook tics are hushed breathy intimacy and over-acted character voices; the sheet bans whispering and trailing off (L4 note) and does not voice the dad. Not a match.

**East Texas as Southern Gothic (07, Section 3).** Southern Gothic markers in film are decay, heat, cicadas, menace, a philosophical low drawl (True Detective season 1, Louisiana, is the most nameable recent example of the drawl-as-dread register). This picture is flat daylight, birds, a garage, a kettle, no menace (01, Section 2a beats 8 and 9). The drawl as written (stretch stressed vowels, keep the r, light on "polite", no "ain't", no added words) is regional color, not Gothic. It would only tip if the read went low, slow and ominous, which the sheet already forbids. Not a finding.

**Known narrator imitation.** Nothing in 07 points the performer at any real person's voice, and it states it is "not a transcription of any person." Good. Keep it that way: no "do it like Sam Elliott" or "like McConaughey" direction anywhere in notes to the performer or in ElevenLabs or any voice-tool prompt if a temp voice is ever made.

## 5. The counter (strongest case for originality)

The trailer's spine is a single object filmed at floor height across one man's whole life, a ratchet tempo that becomes a hospital monitor, and an ending that withholds both a person and an answer. None of the comps found across four passes (Here, the Chevrolet holiday ads, Gran Torino, The Shining, The Changeling, Field of Dreams) shares more than one element, and every one of those single shared elements has been staged away from its source: plastic not tennis or red ball, daylight not a dark hallway, a neutral pause not a fright or a smile, an interrupted log not a sheet over a car. The third-read changes push further from the comps, not closer. The voiceover sheet's insistence on plain spelling, no added words and "a man reading a book, not a character in it" is exactly what separates a real regional voice from a commercial one. I could not build a case that a cold viewer names a source for this cut.
