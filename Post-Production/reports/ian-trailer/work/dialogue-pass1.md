## BLUF
The narration mostly holds. It is plain, it is short, and "polite to him" (line 111) is the best line in the draft because it leaves the important thing unsaid. Three problems need fixing before Ian records.

1. **Worst offender: L4, line 112, "So he went back under the car. Wrench in hand. Same as before."** The last two pieces are a three-beat trailer cadence, the "same as before" part sums up the theme, and the line sits on the climax where the picture is already doing that job. "So he went back under the car." is a real thing a man says. The other two are the room writing a trailer line.
2. **The timings do not fit a calm, slow read.** L1, L1a, L2 and L3 are each given less time than the read notes ask for (lines 109 to 111, 121). Ian is told to read "slower than feels natural" (line 134) and then given windows that need a brisk read. He will rush, or the edit will clip him.
3. **Card B, line 39 and 150, "when a surprising secret changes everything."** This is Ian's back-cover text, so no rewrite from me. It is the most worn phrase in book marketing, and it names the secret, which goes against the draft's own promise at line 8 ("Nothing in any line, caption or card points at the secret").

The SLOT is in the right place (over the wrench hand-off, line 31), but the window is too short and nothing tells the listener the voice has changed from narrator to father. Details below.

## Narration, line by line

### L1, line 109 (SAFE). "Before the Army, Brandon Turner was a boy under a car, handing his dad wrenches."
- **Unsayable in the window.** That is about 22 syllables in 4.5 s (0:03.5 to 0:08.0). At the calm, slower-than-talking read the draft asks for, it needs about 5.5 to 6 s. He will have to hurry the one line that is supposed to be warm.
- **Two registers in one sentence.** "Brandon Turner" is the full name, which is blurb voice. "His dad" is kitchen-table voice. The read note (line 103) asks for the kitchen table. A man telling one person about someone doesn't usually say the full name and then "his dad" in the same breath.
- **The narration repeats the picture.** Beat 2 already shows the boy handing his father a wrench (line 31). "Handing his dad wrenches" says what the viewer is watching.
- **Point in its favor:** saying "Turner" aloud sets up the title pun with THE WRENCH TURNER on the end card. That is a real reason to keep the surname if the line can be shortened somewhere else.
- **Reaching for:** who he was before the Army, in one warm breath.
- SUGGESTION (keeps the Army, drops the name): "Before the Army, he was the kid handing his dad wrenches."
- SUGGESTION (keeps the name and the pun, drops the Army): "Brandon Turner grew up under a car, handing his dad wrenches." The barracks and sand beats show the Army without anyone saying it.

### L2, line 110. "Two years. His father was there for all of it."
- **Doesn't fit the window.** The read note asks for a full stop and a silent count of one after "Two years." That is about 3.5 s of read in a 2.5 s window (0:29.5 to 0:32.0).
- **"Two years" of what?** A cold viewer has just watched a ball stop in the sand. Two years could be the deployment, the war or the hospital. Being a little elliptical is human, but this one may simply not register. The visitor-log page turn (line 36) is what tells us it was a long time, so "Two years" can lean on it only if the line starts while the pages are turning.
- **The narration repeats the picture again.** The log with the same signature on every line (line 36) already says "his father was there for all of it." The line and the image make the same point at the same moment.
- **Dad in L1, father in L2 and L3.** This could be a deliberate shift from boy to man. Read aloud it sounds like two different writers. Ian should pick the word he would actually use. An old soldier talking at a kitchen table says "his dad" or "his old man" far more often than "his father."
- **Reaching for:** the father's devotion, stated flat so the image carries the feeling.
- SUGGESTION: start L2 at about 0:28.0, over the page turn, so the pause after "Two years" fits and the page turn answers "of what." The words themselves are plain enough to keep.

### L3, line 111. "He came home to his father's tools, and a mother who was polite to him."
- **A comma nobody performs.** "Came home to X, and Y" is a written construction, a zeugma. Read aloud, it needs a held comma and a lift that a calm reader won't give it, so it comes out as one long run.
- **"A mother."** The indefinite article is a writer's distancing trick. Nobody says "a mother" about a particular man's own mom. On the page it is pointed. Read aloud, it sounds like a slip.
- **"Polite to him" is the best thing in the draft.** It is the feeling itself rather than a description of it, and it does not name the illness. Keep it, and protect it by cleaning up the clause in front of it.
- **"His father's tools" is doing a plot job it may not manage.** It is the only spoken hint that the father has died (see the pass-2 note at line 187). As spoken, it sounds like ordinary tools. That belongs to the structure lane, see HANDOFF.
- **Timing:** about 17 syllables in 4.0 s, under the note "Slower here." It needs about 5 s.
- SUGGESTION: "He came home to his dad's tools. And his mom was polite to him." Two sentences, no comma to perform, and the "And" carries the hurt.

### L4, line 112. "So he went back under the car. Wrench in hand. Same as before."
- **Slop tell: the rhythmic three-part close.** Short sentence, fragment, fragment. "Wrench in hand" is written phrasing nobody says out loud. "Same as before" sums up the theme for the viewer when the next shot (the same pose as the boyhood shot, the ball rolling in) is built to make them feel it without being told.
- **"So he went back under the car." on its own is right.** "So" is how people really start the last part of a story. It also gives Ian the quiet ending the read note asks for, without making him whisper three fragments.
- **15 s cutdown problem (line 58).** In the cutdown, L4 is the only line, so "So" follows nothing. It also runs from 0:09.2 for about 3.5 s, which takes it to 0:12.7, but the tap is at 0:11.0 and the table says the line "ends just before the tap." Those two numbers can't both be true.
- **Reaching for:** coming back to where he started, and the stillness before the tap.
- SUGGESTION (master): "So he went back under the car." Then stop and let the slower ratchet carry the rest.
- SUGGESTION (15 s cut): "He went back under the car." No "So", and it fits in about 2 s before the 0:11.0 tap.

### Pattern across all four lines
Every line is a complete, past-tense summary sentence, and each one lands on a neat ending. Each also says what its beat is already showing (L1 the hand-off, L2 the log, L3 the bench and kitchen, L4 the creeper). That is the narration explaining the picture to the audience. The fix is subtraction, not new words. Let one line in two lean on the picture rather than describe it. The read note wants "one person across a kitchen table" (line 103), but third-person "Brandon Turner was" is blurb voice. Ian should choose which register he is in, the author reading his own book or the man at the table, and the lines should all follow that choice. Either works. Mixing them is what sounds false.

Small point on a read note: line 109 says "like you are smiling at the memory." The memory belongs to Brandon, not Ian, unless the book is drawn from his own life (open item 16, line 180). The note may land oddly with him.

## SLOT placement, lines 119 to 124

- **Position is right.** The father's line plays over beat 2, at the moment the father takes the wrench (line 31). It is the only real human speech in the trailer and it pays off the title on the end card. On dialogue grounds, the SLOT version is the stronger cut.
- **L1a doesn't fit before it.** "Before the Army, Brandon Turner was a boy under a car." is about 15 syllables in 2.5 s (0:03.3 to 0:05.8). A calm read needs about 3.5 to 4 s, which pushes the slot to about 0:07.0.
- **Not enough room behind it.** The slot window is 3 s (0:06.0 to 0:09.0). Line 122 says that if Ian's line runs past about 3.5 s, beat 3 shrinks to make room. Beat 3 is only 2 s (0:09.0 to 0:11.0), so it can give up about 1 s before the ball roll stops reading as a roll. Realistically, Ian's line has to be about 2.5 to 3 s at his calm pace, or something else must give.
- **Nothing signals the change of speaker.** The narrator says "a boy under a car" and then, in the same voice, the father's words follow. A listener may hear the father's line as more narration. The slot needs an audible handoff. The draft already has one available: line 31 says the ratchet stops for the hand-off. Put the father's line inside that ratchet silence and let the ratchet start again after it. A small shift in Ian's read (line 122 leaves this to him) also helps.
- **Silent cut:** set the slot caption in quotation marks so it reads as the father speaking, not as narration (affects line 148).
- SUGGESTION: L1a starts at 0:03.0. The ratchet stops at about 0:06.5. The slot runs from about 0:06.8 inside the silence. The ratchet restarts right after his line. Beat 3 starts no later than about 0:10.0.

### Card B, lines 39 and 150. "when a surprising secret changes everything."
- "Changes everything" is the most worn closer in book and trailer copy. "Surprising secret" is close to redundant, since a secret is surprising.
- It names the secret, which contradicts line 8 and line 126.
- Read as two cards, two seconds each, Card B is a sentence fragment that starts with "when". It only works if the viewer joins it to Card A as one sentence. Two seconds for seven words with a break in between is tight.
- Because these are Ian's words, the options are his: use Card A alone, use neither, or ask whether he has another line from the book. I'm not supplying a replacement.
