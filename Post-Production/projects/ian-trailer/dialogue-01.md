## Captions

**C2, "Back then, he was / the "Wrench Turner.""** (lines 26, 83)
- Problem: quote marks burned into picture. On screen, quotes around a nickname read as scare quotes, the way you would write a word you are making fun of. Out loud nobody performs the quotes, so the caption says something the voice would not.
- Reaching for: marking "Wrench Turner" as a name somebody gave him, Ian's phrase.
- Proposal: drop the quote marks and let capitals carry the name (Back then, he was / the Wrench Turner). If the nickname needs more weight, set it on its own line in the same type, not in quotes.
- Second, smaller: the line break leaves "he was" dangling at the end of line one, so the eye stalls on a verb with no object for a beat. Proposal: break after "Back then," (Back then, / he was the Wrench Turner). Check fit at 64 pt vertical.

**C4, "The car was the easy part."** (line 28, 70)
- Problem, borderline: "X was the easy part" is a stock tagline shape. It earns its place better than most because the last shot is him back under the car, but it is the line most likely to read as built-for-the-trailer. It is the trailer line by design, so the risk is only that it sounds borrowed.
- Reaching for: the car gets fixed, the man is the open question.
- Proposal: keep it as the pick, and have no-ai-slop's judgment pass look at this one line specifically. If it gets flagged as generic, the fallback in the draft (Ian's own back-cover line) is the honest swap, not a new aphorism.

**"Home was harder." used as the optional spare in the 45 s** (line 63)
- Problem: if the spare is ever switched on, the 45 s carries two comparison lines ("Home was harder," then "The car was the easy part") inside about 15 seconds. Two tidy comparatives in a row is the rhythmic pattern that makes trailer copy sound written.
- Proposal: keep the spare off in the 45 s, as the draft defaults. If it goes on, C4 should change.

**C1, "He came home from the war."** and **C3, "Home was harder."**: hold. Plain, no editorializing, readable in the time given. C1 is a common opener for veteran stories, but it leans on the picture, which is the right move here. No flag beyond that.

## Ask-Ian text (Zac to Ian)

Overall: the open ("Hey Ian, trailer's coming along... whenever you get a sec") and the close ("That's it. Thanks man.") sound like Zac. Items 3 and 4 sound like a guy asking a buddy. The problem is the middle: it drifts toward a project checklist, and two items say things a friend who already talked to Ian would not say.

**Item 8, "Heads up, the pictures and clips in it are AI-made, not filmed... Your accounts, your call. I'd turn it on, but up to you."** (line 116)
- Problem 1, nobody would say that here: Ian already said no label was needed (TASKS.md). Raising it as if new reads like Zac did not hear him. A friend circling back says he remembers, then says why he is bringing it up again.
- Problem 2, says it twice: "Your accounts, your call" and "but up to you" are the same thought. "Your accounts, your call" is also the tidy, quotable kind of line people write, not text.
- Reaching for: making sure Ian knows the toggle exists without pushing.
- Proposal: open the item by acknowledging Ian's earlier answer (along the lines of: I know you said no label, but...), give the one reason, keep one "up to you," cut the other.

**Item 1, three questions stacked in one item** (line 109)
- Problem, too clean and too much: three quoted lines, a question, a second offer with a fourth quoted line, then a second question. On a phone this is a wall, and Ian will answer one half of it. "Anything there wrong for the book?" is also slightly off as speech; people ask whether something fits, not whether it is wrong for a book.
- Reaching for: getting Ian's OK on the screen words and the back-cover swap in one go.
- Proposal: split it into 1 (the three lines, "any of that not fit the book?") and 2 (want your back-cover line at the end instead?), and renumber.

**Item 5, "How does the book handle that? Want a quick glimpse of it in the trailer?"** (line 113)
- Problem: "How does the book handle that?" is editor-speak to an author about his own book. "Glimpse" is a writer's word; out loud a guy says "a quick shot of it." The rest of the item ("two guys sitting quiet on a tailgate... nobody breaking down") is the best voice in the whole text.
- Reaching for: asking how heavy the scene is in the book so the trailer does not misrepresent it.
- Proposal: ask it the way a friend would (how heavy does that get in the book?) and swap "glimpse" for "shot."

**Items 6 and 7, colon-led openings** ("Coming home: I've got him...", "The girlfriend at the pasture party: a quick shot...") (lines 114, 115)
- Problem, pattern: two items in a row start with a label and a colon, like form headers. That is how a document reads, not a text. Item 6 also has "Does that match the book, anything I'd get wrong?" which runs two questions together with a comma and the tense slips ("I'd get" for something already made).
- Reaching for: telling Ian which scene each question is about.
- Proposal: lead with the scene in plain words, no colon (For coming home I've got him in uniform hugging his folks out front...), and end item 6 on one question ("anything I got wrong?"). "Folks" over "parents" is Zac's register and still clear of the fence words.

**Length, the whole message**
- Eight numbered items in one text is a lot to send a friend in one shot. The numbering is useful so he can answer by number, so keep it. But if item 1 splits, it becomes nine. Worth asking whether item 4 (who the people on the cover are) can fold into item 3 (who made the cover), since the same person will know both.
