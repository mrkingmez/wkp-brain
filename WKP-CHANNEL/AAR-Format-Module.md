# THE AFTER ACTION REVIEW
### Format Module — Warrior King Productions
### Companion to the Military Research Master Prompt v1.0

---

## WHAT THIS FORMAT IS

A battle run through a standard military After Action Review, facilitated by someone who has actually sat in that room. Not a retelling — a debrief. The participants are present, but only through what they genuinely said or wrote.

**The differentiator:** What If and Hidden Hinge are formats a strong historian could execute. This one runs on operational experience. The value isn't the history — it's the framework being applied correctly by someone who knows what a real AAR sounds like and, more importantly, what a dishonest one sounds like.

---

## FORMAT LAW

**1. No invented dialogue. Ever.**
Participants speak only through the documented record: after-action reports, dispatches, war diaries, letters, testimony, depositions, post-war interviews, memoirs (flagged as self-presenting). If a commander's reasoning isn't preserved, the honest line is *"he never explained this, and we don't know."* One fabricated quote costs more credibility than the entire episode earns.

**2. Improvements must be period-legal.**
Every improve item is tested against the technology, doctrine, communications, training base, and institutional culture actually available. "Should have coordinated better" is worthless if there was no means to coordinate. If the fix didn't exist yet, that's not a lesson — that's the limit of the era, and saying so is the more interesting observation.

**3. Judgment against contemporary standards only.**
A commander is measured against what a competent peer, in that army, in that decade, would have done. Not against modern doctrine.

**4. The unit of analysis is the command relationship.**
Whoever actually owned the decisions, at whatever echelon that sat. Ancient armies: the commander and his host. Fort Necessity: Washington across a force he only partly controlled. The question is constant — who made the calls, what did they take away, did the next fight prove it.

**5. "They didn't learn" is a legitimate — and often better — episode.**
The lesson was correct, it was written down, and the institution refused it. That failure mode is real, common, and almost never covered.

## THE FOUR QUESTIONS

### Q1 — WHAT WAS SUPPOSED TO HAPPEN
*The plan.*

- Stated objective, in the commander's own words where preserved
- Higher intent — what the theater or political leadership actually wanted
- Scheme of maneuver as designed
- Assumptions the plan rested on, stated and unstated
- Intelligence picture at the time of planning: what was known, believed, and absent
- Force, logistics, and time available versus what the plan required
- Was this plan achievable with these means? Judged against contemporary standards.

Tag every element per the master prompt evidence bands. Where the plan wasn't written down, say so rather than reconstructing it from the outcome — **reverse-engineering intent from results is the single most common error in this format.**

### Q2 — WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED
*The breakdown. No plan survives first contact.*

Chronological, phase by phase. For each phase:

- What occurred
- Where reality diverged from the plan
- **When the commander became aware of the divergence** — usually much later than the audience assumes
- What decision was made in response, and what options were genuinely open at that moment
- Second- and third-order effects rippling into the next phase

Track four failure classes explicitly:
- **Friction** — weather, terrain, exhaustion, breakdown. Nobody's fault.
- **Enemy action** — the opponent did something well. Give them credit by name.
- **Command failure** — a decision that was wrong on information available at the time.
- **Institutional failure** — the system, training, doctrine, or command structure set them up to fail.

That last category is what most battle content misses entirely.

Close with: **the point of no return** — the moment the outcome became unrecoverable, and whether the commander recognized it.

### Q3 — TAKEAWAYS

#### 3A — SUSTAIN
*What was done right and should be repeated.*

There is always something. A losing commander who found it is more instructive than a winner who got lucky. Cover:
- Decisions correct on the information available, regardless of outcome
- Things that worked as designed
- Individual or unit performance that held under pressure
- What prevented the defeat from being worse

**Separate good decisions from good outcomes.** A sound call that failed still sustains. A reckless call that paid off does not.

#### 3B — IMPROVE
*What went wrong and how to fix it — with the tools of the time.*

For each item:
1. What went wrong
2. Root cause — not the symptom
3. The period-legal fix: what a competent contemporary would have done differently, using only what existed
4. Whether the commander had the authority to implement it, or whether the fix sat above his level

That fourth point matters. A lieutenant colonel cannot fix a doctrine problem. Naming the correct echelon for each fix is what separates analysis from armchair quarterbacking.

Rank the improve items. Which one, fixed alone, changes the most?

### Q4 — RETRAIN, RE-FIGHT, VERIFY
*Did they learn it?*

Run the three tests in order:

**Test 1 — Was the lesson available?**
Could it have been identified with what they knew then? Or is it only visible now? If unavailable, they cannot be faulted for missing it.

**Test 2 — Was it identified?**
Does the record show them naming it? Orders, correspondence, revised drill, doctrinal changes, personnel decisions. Cite the document.

**Test 3 — Was it institutionalized?**
Did it survive contact with the organization, or die with the individual who learned it?

Then verify against the next engagement:
- Next battle under the same commander, and what changed in his conduct
- Whether the specific improve items appear as corrected behavior
- Whether new failures replaced the old ones
- Whether the parent institution absorbed it or the individual carried it alone
- Long arc: does this lesson show up ten or twenty years later in the same person's command?

**Verdict, stated plainly:**
`LEARNED` — identified, applied, and demonstrated in subsequent action
`LEARNED — NOT INSTITUTIONALIZED` — the individual adapted; the system didn't
`PARTIALLY LEARNED` — some items corrected, others repeated
`NOT LEARNED` — the same failure recurs
`UNTESTED` — no comparable subsequent engagement

`LEARNED — NOT INSTITUTIONALIZED` is the most common real-world result and the most interesting one to build an episode around.

## RESEARCH PROMPT — COPY BELOW

You are conducting an After Action Review of a historical engagement, facilitated to the standard of a professional military debrief.

**ENGAGEMENT:** [___]
**COMMANDER / COMMAND ELEMENT:** [___]
**SUBSEQUENT ENGAGEMENT FOR VERIFICATION (if known):** [___]

Apply all governing rules from the Military Research Master Prompt v1.0: four-band evidence tagging ([ESTABLISHED] / [SUPPORTED] / [CONTESTED] / [SPECULATIVE]), claim-type source matching, mandatory citation discipline with "attribution uncertain" flagging, no modern moral framework, multi-national historiography, no manufactured balance, and "unknown" as a valid answer.

Additional rules specific to this format:

- **No invented dialogue or interior thought.** Participants are represented only through documented statements. Where reasoning isn't preserved, state that it isn't.
- **All improve items must be period-legal** — achievable with the technology, doctrine, communications, and training actually available. State explicitly what was available.
- **Reverse-engineering the plan from the outcome is forbidden.** Where the plan wasn't documented, say so.
- **Name the echelon** that owned each identified fix.

Produce the report in four sections: What Was Supposed To Happen, What Actually Happened, Takeaways (Sustain / Improve), and Retrain–Refight–Verify. Structure each per the module above.

Close with the three claims in your own report you consider weakest, and why.

## WORKED EXAMPLE — FORT NECESSITY, 3 JULY 1754

**Why this is the pilot episode:**

The failure is unambiguous. It's documented from both sides. And the verification beat is unusually clean — Washington is back in the same theater thirteen months later at the Monongahela, under Braddock, against a comparable force using comparable tactics.

It also delivers the format's best result *inside a single case*. Washington appears to have taken real lessons from 1754. The British regular establishment, by most accounts, did not — and the Monongahela is where that gap gets tested. Individual learning against institutional refusal, in one episode, with a documented outcome.

**What each section has to work with:**

- **Q1 (Plan):** A defensive position at Great Meadows, built while awaiting reinforcement, following the Jumonville Glen action in May. Worth examining hard: was holding position the plan, or the absence of one? And the compromised command arrangement — Washington's colonial commission versus Captain Mackay's royal commission, which meant Mackay's independent company was not clearly under his orders. That's a Q1 institutional problem that shapes everything downstream.

- **Q2 (Reality):** Position siting is the core of this section. High ground and treeline within musket range, a meadow that flooded, trenches filling with water, powder soaked. Whether that siting was forced by circumstance or was a judgment error is the question the section turns on — and it's genuinely contested, which makes it good material. Ends with a surrender document signed in French, translated in the field, containing language about the Jumonville affair that Washington later disputed understanding.

- **Q3 (Sustain/Improve):** Improve items are unusually concrete — position selection relative to covering terrain, engineering under time pressure, powder security in wet conditions, translation and terms verification before signature. Every one of those has a period-legal fix; European siege engineering of the era had well-established answers to defensive siting. The command-arrangement problem, by contrast, sat above his authority — a clean example of naming the right echelon.

- **Q4 (Verify):** Monongahela, 9 July 1755. Test each improve item against his conduct there and afterward. Then the harder question: what did the British establishment take from Fort Necessity, and did that shape the 1755 campaign? Washington's own later reflections on the engagement are documented but should be treated as memoir-class — self-presenting, and written by a man managing his reputation.

**Research caution:** Washington's post-war account of his early career is a curated document. Weight his contemporary 1754 correspondence over later reflection, and flag the difference where they diverge.
