# How to check what AI tells you

About 10 minutes to read, plus about 15 to try it

## What you will be able to do

- Explain, in your own words, why an AI can sound completely sure and still be wrong.
- Run a three-step check on any answer before you rely on it.
- Know which kinds of details deserve the hardest look.

## Why AI sounds sure when it is wrong

An AI chat tool does one thing very well. It writes the words that most likely come next, based on the huge amount of writing it learned from.

That is different from looking something up. It does not open a book in its head and find the page. It builds a sentence that fits the pattern of a good answer. Often the pattern and the truth line up. Sometimes they do not, and the sentence comes out smooth and wrong.

Here is the part that catches people. A wrong answer sounds exactly like a right one. Same calm tone, same tidy paragraphs, same helpful manner. How confident it sounds tells you nothing about whether it is true.

So the job of checking falls to you. It is not hard, and it takes a few minutes once you have the habit.

## The three-step check

### Step 1: Ask what the answer rests on

Right after it answers, type something like this into the same chat.

> What is that answer based on? And which parts are you least sure of?

You will often get a more careful reply. It may point out the shaky parts on its own. Treat that as a map of where to look first. It is not proof either. The AI is describing its own answer, and it can be wrong about that too.

### Step 2: Open the source yourself

If the AI gives you a link, a book title, an article, or a quote, go and open it. Do not just trust that it exists.

Links, titles, and quotes can be made up. A link that looks real is not proof. It can have a believable web address and lead nowhere. A quote can sound like something a real person said and never have been said. Click it, read it, and see whether it actually says what the AI claims.

If you cannot find it, treat the claim as unconfirmed.

### Step 3: Check a second way

Find the same fact from a source that is not the AI. Search for it yourself. Look on the official website of the place, company, or office the fact is about. Ask a person who would know. A printed book or a trusted reference works too.

If two separate sources agree, you can lean on it. If they disagree, or you only find the AI saying it, hold off.

### What to check hardest

Slow down and verify these every time.

- **Numbers.** Prices, amounts, measurements, quantities.
- **Dates.** When something happened, or when something is due.
- **Names.** People, places, businesses, titles of books and shows.
- **Quotes.** Anything in quotation marks, and who is said to have said it.
- **Anything medical, legal, or about money.** A mistake here can cost you your health, your rights, or your savings. Use the AI to understand the topic and to prepare good questions. Then confirm with a doctor, a pharmacist, a lawyer, your bank, or the official source.

## Try it

Open your AI chat in another tab. Keep this page open beside it. If you do not have one set up yet, ask Zac to help you open one before you start.

### Part A: Ask about something you know well

Pick a subject where you are the expert. Your own town. A hobby. A family recipe. Something about your own life that is not private or sensitive.

Ask the AI a few specific questions about it. For example, ask about a local landmark, or how a dish is made, or the details of something you do. Keep the questions specific, because vague questions get vague answers that are hard to catch out.

Now read the answers like a teacher grading homework. Hunt for a mistake: a wrong name, a detail that is a little off, or something stated as fact that you know is not.

**If you find nothing wrong, that is fine.** It happens. Move on to Part B anyway, because that is where the habit gets built.

### Part B: Run the three steps

Ask the AI a factual question that you can look up afterward. Something with a clear answer, such as when a particular building opened, who wrote a certain book, or how far apart two places are.

Then do all three steps.

1. Ask it what its answer rests on and what it is least sure of.
2. If it names a source, open that source yourself and see whether it says what the AI says.
3. Check the answer a second way, with a search or a website that is not the AI.

When you finish, ask yourself two things. Did the answer hold up? And how did checking feel, compared to just believing it?

## Keep this in your pocket

1. A confident tone proves nothing. AI writes likely words, it does not look facts up.
2. Ask what it rests on, open the source yourself, then check a second way.
3. Check hardest on numbers, dates, names, quotes, and anything medical, legal, or about money.

## Practice

Three everyday jobs. Each one takes about 10 minutes. Do them in any order. You need an AI chat open in another tab. Where the tasks mention a message or a list, I wrote the examples for you, so nobody's real private details are involved.

Each task has the same parts: what to do, a prompt you can copy and paste, a short checklist, and a picture of what a good result looks like. Each prompt box has a copy button, so you do not have to retype it.

### Task 1: Check a claim about something you own

**Why it matters:** a warranty length or a part number is a detail an AI can get wrong without any sign of doubt, and trusting a wrong one costs you money.

**Do this:** pick one thing in your house with a brand name on it, such as the vacuum, the coffee maker, or the car. Paste the prompt below with the brand and model filled in. Then confirm each answer from a source that is not the AI, such as the manual, the sticker on the item, or the maker's own website.

```text
I own a [brand and model]. Tell me three things: how long the warranty
usually lasts, one common problem owners report, and one maintenance tip.
For each one, say how sure you are and where I could confirm it.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I wrote down all three answers before I checked anything.
- [ ] I opened the manual or the maker's site myself. I did not just ask the AI if it was right.
- [ ] I marked each answer: matched, did not match, or could not find.
- [ ] I looked hardest at the number, the warranty length.

**What good looks like:** you end with a short list, each item marked matched, did not match, or could not find. A "could not find" is a good result. It means you caught a claim you cannot lean on. If everything matched, you still learned something, because now you know that tool was right on this one. Next time you will still check.

### Task 2: Spot a scam message, then check the AI's help

**Why it matters:** an AI can help you read a suspicious message, but it cannot see who really sent it. It can sound sure and still miss something.

**Do this:** here is a made-up text message. It is not real. Paste it into the prompt below.

> Your package could not be delivered. Pay a $1.99 redelivery fee at the link below within 24 hours or it will be returned. [link]

```text
I got this text message: "Your package could not be delivered. Pay a $1.99
redelivery fee at the link below within 24 hours or it will be returned. [link]"
List the warning signs that it could be a scam. Then tell me what you cannot
tell from the text alone.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] The AI listed warning signs, and I can say in my own words why each one is a warning.
- [ ] The AI said what it cannot tell from the text alone. If it did not, I asked it again.
- [ ] I know the safe way to check a real delivery: go to the carrier's website by typing the address myself, never through the link in the message.
- [ ] I did not click anything.

**What good looks like:** a good answer points to pressure to hurry, a small payment request, and a link you did not ask for. A good answer also admits it cannot confirm who sent the message. You are the one who decides, and you decide by going to the real company yourself. Module 3 goes deeper on scams.

### Task 3: Decide what is safe to paste into a chat

**Why it matters:** once you paste something into a chat, you cannot take it back. The safe habit is to decide before you paste, and to assume the chat is not private.

**Do this:** read the list below. Before you ask the AI anything, sort each item yourself into three piles: fine to paste, fine after I remove the personal parts, never paste. Write your sorting down. Then paste the prompt and compare.

- A recipe you want shortened
- A photo of your driver's license
- An email from your landlord with your name and address in it
- The password to your bank account
- A paragraph you wrote for a school paper, with no names
- A letter from your doctor with test results
- A list of your friends' phone numbers

```text
Here is a list of things I might paste into an AI chat. Sort each one into:
fine to paste, fine after I remove personal details, or never paste. Explain
each choice in one sentence.
[paste the list above]
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I sorted the list myself before I saw the AI's answer.
- [ ] I found at least one place where the AI and I disagreed, or I can explain why we agreed.
- [ ] For each "never paste" item, I can say what could go wrong.
- [ ] I did not paste anything real from my own life into the chat for this task.

**What good looks like:** passwords, ID photos, and other people's private details land in "never paste". Items with a name or address in them land in "remove the personal parts first". A plain recipe or an anonymous paragraph is fine. Some items could go either way, like the doctor's letter. For those, leave it out or strip every personal detail first. Where you and the AI disagree, the stricter answer is the safer one. Module 2 covers this properly.
