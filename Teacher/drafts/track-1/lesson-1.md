# What AI is, in plain words

About 10 minutes to read, plus about 30 to do the practice

## What you will be able to do

- Say in your own words how an AI chat tool differs from a search engine and from a person.
- Tell which jobs an AI chat tool is good at and which ones to double-check.
- Test an AI yourself and sort what it tells you into right, wrong, and not sure.

## What you will make

A one-page "right, wrong, not sure" sheet. You ask an AI questions you already know the answers to, then grade it like a teacher. It is your first real look at what these tools can and cannot do.

## What AI is

When people say AI today, they usually mean a chat tool. You type a message, and it types back. Claude and ChatGPT are two of these. You have both, so you already know the feel of it.

Here is what is going on inside, in plain words. The tool learned from an enormous amount of writing. From that, it got very good at one skill: given the words so far, write the words that should come next. Chain enough of those together and you get a paragraph, an email, a recipe, a poem.

That is a different thing from a search engine and a different thing from a person.

### AI, a search engine, and a person

| | What it does | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **A search engine** (Google, Bing) | Finds pages that already exist and shows you a list | The page may be wrong or old, but it is a real page you can open |
| **An AI chat tool** | Writes a brand new answer to your message, in sentences | The answer can sound sure and be wrong |
| **A person** who knows the topic | Knows things from living, and can say "I have no idea" | Can be wrong too, but knows when they are guessing |

The middle row is the one to remember. A search engine hands you something that exists. An AI writes something that sounds right. Most of the time those match. Sometimes they do not, and the answer comes out smooth and wrong.

One more thing, so you are not surprised. Some AI tools can also search the web as part of an answer, and some cannot. It differs by tool and by plan, and the companies change it. We will not guess here. Later in this practice you will test it yourself.

## What AI is good at

Think of a quick, well-read helper who never gets tired and is happy to rewrite things ten times.

- **Writing and rewriting.** A first draft of a note, an email that sounds warmer, a shorter version of something long.
- **Explaining.** A hard idea in plain words, or the same idea explained again a different way.
- **Brainstorming.** Twenty ideas for a birthday party, a gift, a name, a dinner. You pick the ones you like.
- **Sorting and cleaning up.** You did this once already with your email. Lists, messy notes, and piles of text are a good fit.

## Where to be careful

- **Facts.** Names, dates, numbers, quotes, and anything about health, law, or money. Check these somewhere else before you rely on them.
- **Very recent or very local things.** Whether it knows about last week, or about your town, depends on the tool. Test it before you trust it.
- **Anything private.** Treat a chat as not private. Do not paste passwords or other people's personal details. The Stay Safe track is where this gets its own lessons.

## Keep this in your pocket

1. A search engine finds pages that exist. An AI writes a new answer that sounds right.
2. AI is strong at writing, rewriting, explaining, brainstorming, and sorting.
3. Check its facts, especially names, dates, numbers, and quotes.

## Practice

Three jobs, about 10 minutes each. You need two browser tabs open. In one, go to claude.ai and sign in. In the other, go to chatgpt.com and sign in. Both free versions are all you need. Type into the box at the bottom of each page and press Enter to send.

Everything below uses made-up or public topics. Do not paste anything private.

### Task 1: Grade the AI on what you already know

**Why it matters:** the best way to learn how far to trust a tool is to quiz it on a subject where you are the expert.

**Do this:** pick a topic you know well, such as a hobby, a favorite show, a recipe you make, or a place you have lived. Write down three questions you already know the answers to. Make one of them easy, one a bit tricky, and one with a specific detail, like a year or a number. Ask Claude all three. For each answer, write down whether it was right, wrong, or not sure.

```text
I know a lot about [your topic]. I am going to ask you three questions.
Answer each one in two or three sentences, and tell me if you are unsure.

1. [your easy question]
2. [your tricky question]
3. [your question with a year or a number in it]
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I wrote my own answers down before I read the AI's.
- [ ] I marked each AI answer right, wrong, or not sure.
- [ ] I noticed how sure the AI sounded on each answer, and whether that matched how right it was.
- [ ] If it said it was unsure on anything, I wrote that down too.

**What good looks like:** a sheet with three rows, each marked right, wrong, or not sure. Getting all three right is a fine result. It tells you the tool is good on this topic, not that it will always be. Getting one wrong is a better lesson. Look at how it sounded when it was wrong. That is the thing to remember.

### Task 2: Ask the same questions in both tools

**Why it matters:** two tools can give different answers to the same question. Seeing it once makes you a careful reader for good.

**Do this:** open ChatGPT and paste the same prompt with the same three questions from Task 1. Compare the two sets of answers, line by line. Did they agree? Did one sound surer than the other? Did either add something the other left out? Check any difference against a source that is not an AI, such as a book, an official website, or a person who knows.

```text
Same three questions again. Answer each one in two or three sentences,
and tell me if you are unsure.

1. [your easy question]
2. [your tricky question]
3. [your question with a year or a number in it]
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I put the two answers side by side and compared them one question at a time.
- [ ] Where they disagreed, I checked a source that is not an AI.
- [ ] I can say which tool was better on this topic. I also know that one test proves little.
- [ ] I added a "Claude" and "ChatGPT" mark to each row on my sheet.

**What good looks like:** your sheet now has two marks per row. Often the answers agree on the easy question and drift apart on the detail. When two tools disagree, at least one is wrong, and now you know which question to look up. When they agree, they can still both be wrong, so a check is still smart on anything that matters.

### Task 3: Search engine or AI, you decide

**Why it matters:** picking the right tool for the job saves time and keeps you from trusting the wrong thing.

**Do this:** read this list of jobs. Before you ask anyone, write down for each one whether you would use a search engine, an AI chat tool, or a person. Then try the store-hours job in Claude and in ChatGPT, and on a search engine, and note what happens.

- Find the phone number of a real business near you
- Rewrite a stiff email so it sounds friendly
- Get five ideas for a surprise party
- Learn what time a store closes today
- Understand a confusing paragraph from a letter, in simpler words

```text
What time does a pharmacy near [your town] close today? Tell me how you
know, and tell me if you cannot know.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I made my own choices before I tried any of them.
- [ ] I tried the pharmacy question on an AI tool, then looked it up on a search engine or the pharmacy's own website.
- [ ] I noted whether each AI tool said it could not know, or gave an answer anyway.
- [ ] I can explain why a live, local fact is a risky thing to take from an AI without checking.

**What good looks like:** writing, rewriting, and brainstorming go to the AI. Phone numbers and store hours go to a search engine or the business itself. Some tools may say they cannot look up live information. Others may search the web and show you where the answer came from. Others may guess. Whichever you see, the real hours on the pharmacy's own page are the answer to trust.
