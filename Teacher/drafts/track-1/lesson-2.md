# Meet Claude

About 10 minutes to read, plus about 35 to do the practice

## What you will be able to do

- Say what Claude is and who makes it.
- Name what Claude is good at, and what it cannot do on its own.
- Find out the current free-plan rules yourself, from Claude's own pages.

## What you will make

A one-page "What Claude can do for me" list, with ten real uses you picked after testing some of them yourself. You will build it from your own life, so it is a list you will actually use.

## What Claude is

Claude is an AI chat assistant made by a company called Anthropic. You type a message, and it writes a reply. You can reach it in a web browser at claude.ai. You have already used it once, when you ran Zac's prompt to clean out your email. That was a real job, and it is a good example of where Claude does well.

ChatGPT, the other tool you have, does similar work and is made by a different company, OpenAI. Most of what you learn about Claude in this lesson carries over to ChatGPT. Where the two differ, you will find out by trying both.

## What Claude can do for you

Here are the kinds of jobs to try, in plain words.

- **Write.** A first draft of a message, a thank-you note, a short speech, a product description.
- **Rewrite.** Make something shorter, friendlier, more formal, or clearer. You can ask again and again.
- **Explain.** Take a confusing letter, a recipe step, or a big idea and say it in simpler words.
- **Summarize.** Pull the main points out of text you paste in.
- **Brainstorm.** Names, gift ideas, meal plans, party themes, ways to say no politely.
- **Organize.** Turn a messy list or a pile of notes into something tidy, the way it helped with your email.
- **Plan.** A weekly schedule, a packing list, the steps of a project.

## What Claude cannot do on its own

- **It cannot promise to be right.** It writes answers that sound right. You still check names, dates, numbers, and quotes, as the Stay Safe lesson shows.
- **It cannot see anything you do not give it.** It does not look inside your computer, your email, or your photos. Whatever it works with is what you type or attach in the chat. If a chat ever offers to attach a file, treat that as sharing the file.
- **It does not act in the real world for you.** In the plain chat you are using, it writes, and you act. You copy the email and send it. You buy the groceries.
- **It may not know about today.** Whether it can look up fresh news depends on the tool and the plan. Test it, as you did in the last lesson.

This changes over time, so we are not going to guess. Anthropic lists what the free plan includes, and what the paid plans add, on its own pages. Read them yourself and write down what you find. You will do that in Task 1 below.

## Practice

Three jobs, about 10 to 15 minutes each. Keep claude.ai and chatgpt.com open in two tabs. The free version of each is all you need. Use made-up or public details only.

### Task 1: Read the free-plan rules yourself

**Why it matters:** the rules of a free plan are written by the company and can change. The company's own page beats anything you hear second hand, including from this course.

**Do this:** open the Claude pricing page from the link above. Find the free plan. Write down, in your own words, what it says you get and what it says you do not. Write the date. Then do the same on OpenAI's side by going to chatgpt.com/pricing. If a page has no plain answer to something, write "not stated".

```text
Page: ______________________   Date I looked: ____________
What the free plan says I get:
What it says is only on a paid plan:
Anything the page does not say clearly:
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I read the company's own page, not a news article or a video.
- [ ] I wrote what it says in my own words and I put the date on it.
- [ ] I wrote "not stated" for anything I could not find.
- [ ] I did not sign up for or pay for anything.

**What good looks like:** two short notes, one for each tool, with a date. Some questions will have no clear answer on the page, and "not stated" is a fine result. It is a better note than a guess.

---

### Task 2: Ask Claude what it can do, then test it

**Why it matters:** a tool will happily describe itself, and the description may not match what it really does. Testing one claim at a time is how you learn what you can count on.

**Do this:** paste the prompt below into Claude. From its answer, pick three things you would like it to do for you. Try each one with a small made-up example, for instance, "make this short note friendlier" with a note you write on the spot. Mark each: worked well, worked partly, or did not work.

```text
I am new to AI. In plain words, list ten things you could help me with
in everyday life. Then list five things you cannot do or might get wrong.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I picked three things from Claude's list and tried each one.
- [ ] I used made-up text, not anything private.
- [ ] I marked each one worked well, worked partly, or did not work.
- [ ] I noticed whether any "cannot do" item surprised me.

**What good looks like:** a short list with three marks. A "worked partly" is useful. It tells you how much to edit its work. If something did not work, try saying it a different way once before you give up.

---

### Task 3: Make your "What Claude can do for me" page

**Why it matters:** a tool you cannot name a use for goes unused. A short list of your own uses turns this into something you reach for.

**Do this:** take a sheet of paper, or open a new Google Doc (go to docs.google.com and click Blank document). Think about the past week. Write down five small things that took you time or annoyed you, such as a message you put off, a meal plan, a messy list, or a letter you did not understand. Run this prompt in both Claude and ChatGPT. Pick the five best ideas from the two answers, and add them to your own five. That gives you ten uses. Put your name and the date at the top. This is your page.

```text
Here are five things from my week that took time or annoyed me:
1. [your first thing]
2. [your second thing]
3. [your third thing]
4. [your fourth thing]
5. [your fifth thing]
For each one, tell me whether you can help, how, and what I should still
do myself or double-check.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] My list came from my own week, not from a generic list.
- [ ] I ran the same prompt in both tools and compared the two answers.
- [ ] Next to each use, I wrote what I should still check myself.
- [ ] I did not paste anything private. I described each thing in general words.

**What good looks like:** one page you could stick on the fridge. Ten uses, each with a "still check" note. Some will be great and some will be duds, and learning the difference is the whole point. Keep it handy. It gives you a list of jobs to practice on in the next lessons.
