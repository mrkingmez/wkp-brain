# Build a prompt library

About 10 minutes to read, plus about 50 to do the practice

## What you will be able to do

- Save a prompt that works so you never write it from scratch again.
- Turn a one-time prompt into a reusable one with fill-in blanks.
- Keep a small notebook of your own prompts, tested and ready to use.

## What you will make

Your own ten-prompt notebook in a Google Doc. Each entry has a name, what it is for, the prompt itself with blanks to fill, and the date you last tested it. You already have the first three or four sitting in your work from lessons 3 to 7. Now you collect them, clean them up, and add the rest.

## Why a library

Think about a recipe box. You do not work out your best banana bread from nothing each time. You wrote it down once, with notes, and now you reach for the card.

A prompt that works is a recipe. If you wrote a good letter prompt on Tuesday and cannot find it on Friday, you start over, and the new one may come out worse. A library fixes that. It also does something quieter: it shows you what you actually use AI for. After a few weeks, the entries tell you where it earns its place in your life and where it does not.

## What goes in a library entry

Keep each entry small and the same shape, so you can find things fast. Here is a good shape.

- **Name.** A short label in plain words. "Thank-you note, my voice." "Weekly dinner plan." Pick a name you would search for.
- **Use it when.** One line on the job it does.
- **The prompt.** The full text, with square brackets where the details change, like [name of the person] or [the date].
- **Watch for.** One line on what to check or fix. "Check dates." "It adds a company name I never gave."
- **Last tested.** A date. Tools change, so a prompt that worked in November may need a tweak by spring.

## Blanks are the secret

The most useful thing you can do to a prompt is to find which parts change and which parts stay. The parts that stay are the real work, such as the role, the tone, the shape, and the rules you always want. The parts that change become blanks.

Compare.

> Write a thank-you note to my neighbor Rosa for watering my plants.

> You are a warm, brief writer. Write a thank-you note in under 50 words to [who], for [what they did]. Keep my voice: friendly, a little funny, no big words. Give only the note.

The second one will do a hundred different thank-you notes. You only fill in the two blanks.

## Where to keep it

A Google Doc is plenty. It is free with your account, searchable, and you can reach it from your phone and your computer. You do not need an app. A plain list in one document beats a fancy system you never open.

Two rules for what goes in it:

- **Blanks, not secrets.** Do not save passwords, account numbers, or other people's private details in your library. Use brackets and fill them in fresh each time.
- **Your own account only.** Do not share the Doc with anyone until you decide to, and when you do, you choose who.

## Keep it alive

A library gets better when you use it. After each time you run a prompt, if you changed something to make it work, put that change back into the saved entry. A one-minute fix today saves ten minutes next month.

Do not worry about getting ten perfect entries on day one. Start with what you have. Add as you go.

## Keep this in your pocket

1. A prompt that works is a recipe. Save it with a name, a use, the text, a watch-for note, and a test date.
2. Find the parts that change and make them blanks in square brackets.
3. Put your fixes back into the saved entry, and keep private details out.

## Practice

Three jobs, about 15 to 20 minutes each. You will use Google Docs at docs.google.com and claude.ai. You will test in ChatGPT too. Made-up details only.

### Task 1: Set up the notebook and your first three entries

**Why it matters:** a notebook with a clear shape is easy to add to. A messy one stops getting used.

**Do this:** go to docs.google.com and open a blank document. Name it "My Prompt Library" by clicking the title at the top left. Type "Prompt Library" on the first line. Then paste the entry shape below, once, and fill it in for a prompt you already like from lessons 3 to 7, such as the grocery list, the thank-you template, or the handoff note. Make it blanks-ready by replacing anything that changes with a [bracket]. Repeat for two more. To make each entry easy to find, click on the entry's name line, then use the style box near the top of the page (it usually says "Normal text") and pick Heading 2.

```text
NAME:
USE IT WHEN:
THE PROMPT:
WATCH FOR:
LAST TESTED:
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I have three entries, each with all five parts.
- [ ] The parts that change are in [square brackets].
- [ ] There are no real names, passwords, or private details in any entry.
- [ ] I can find each one in under ten seconds by its name.

**What good looks like:** a Doc with three clear entries that look the same. If one entry feels too long, you probably have two prompts in one. Split them. If you cannot decide what the blanks are, run the prompt once and notice what you had to change.

---

### Task 2: Add seven more from your own life

**Why it matters:** the library is only worth having if it holds jobs you actually do.

**Do this:** think about the last two weeks. Write down seven small jobs where a good prompt could have helped. If you are stuck, pick from this list. For each, write the prompt in Claude, run it once with made-up details, tune it until the result is good, then paste the final version into your notebook with all five parts.

- Reply to a message in a certain tone
- A week of dinners from what is in the house
- Explain a confusing letter in plain words
- A packing list for a trip
- A short product or listing description for something you make
- A polite way to say no
- Plan a week of small chores
- Summarize a long email thread
- Turn rough notes into a clean list
- Ideas for a gift under a set budget

If you get stuck writing the first version, you can ask Claude to help.

```text
I do this job often: [name the job]. Help me write a reusable prompt for it. Put square brackets around the parts that change each time. Ask me up to three questions first if you need to know my voice, my reader, or the shape I want.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I now have ten entries in total.
- [ ] I ran each new prompt at least once before saving it.
- [ ] Every entry has a name I would think to search for.
- [ ] At least three entries use a role, an example, or a shape from earlier lessons.

**What good looks like:** ten entries, each one tested once. If a job did not work well, save it anyway, and write what went wrong in "Watch for." A prompt that taught you its limit is still worth a line. Do not worry if a few entries are rough. You are going to improve them in Task 3.

---

### Task 3: Test and polish three entries

**Why it matters:** an untested prompt is a guess. This is where the library becomes dependable.

**Do this:** pick three entries from your notebook. For each one, open a new chat in Claude (click New chat at the top left of the page, or the plus sign if you see one), paste the saved prompt, fill the blanks with brand new made-up details, and run it. Then run the same filled-in prompt in ChatGPT in a new chat. Compare. If the result is off, fix the saved entry, and run it again. Update LAST TESTED with today's date. If a result is off and you cannot see why, this follow-up helps.

```text
Here is a prompt I use and the result it gave me. The result is not what I wanted because [what is wrong]. Tell me what in the prompt could have caused that, and suggest one change. Do not rewrite the whole prompt.

Prompt: [paste the prompt]
Result: [paste the result]
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I used new details each time, not the ones I tested with before.
- [ ] I compared Claude and ChatGPT on at least one entry.
- [ ] I changed at least one saved prompt because of what I saw.
- [ ] I updated the test date on all three.

**What good looks like:** three entries with a fresh date and, where needed, a better "Watch for" line. The best sign is one where you made a small change, such as adding "Give only the result," and the output got noticeably better. Note which tool each prompt works better in. A line like "Claude is tidier on this one" is what makes a library yours.
