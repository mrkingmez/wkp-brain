# Write with AI, 1: a partner, not a ghostwriter

About 10 minutes to read, plus about 40 to do the practice

## What you will be able to do

- Say which writing jobs to hand to the AI and which to keep for yourself.
- Pick a short piece you care about, and say who it is for and why you are writing it.
- Spot the sentences that do not sound like you, and write down what makes your voice yours.

## What you will make

This is the first of five lessons, and across all five you make one real thing: a short piece, 400 to 700 words, that you care about. A letter to the family. An item for a neighborhood or school newsletter. A short story. By the last lesson it has been brainstormed, outlined, drafted, revised and fact-checked, and you will have a one-page "my writing method" checklist you can reuse on anything. Today you choose the piece and set up your voice card.

## Ghostwriter or partner

A ghostwriter writes the thing and someone else signs it. If you type "write me a letter to my family" and send the result, that is what happened. It will be smooth, a little bland, and not quite yours. Your aunt will read it and feel a stranger wrote it.

A partner works beside you. It asks questions, offers ten angles, points at a sagging paragraph and says "this repeats." You decide, and your hands stay on the words that matter.

"What AI is, in plain words" showed that the AI writes what sounds right. Sounding right is not the same as sounding like you. The warm, specific parts have to come from you.

## What to hand over, what to keep

**Good to hand over:**

- Lists of ideas, angles and possible openings
- A first skeleton for the order of your points
- Questions a reader could ask
- Spotting repetition and long sentences in what you wrote
- Tidying spelling and grammar when you ask

**Keep for yourself:**

- What you actually think and feel
- The true details only you know: the burnt pancakes, the song your dad hummed
- The sentences that carry the heart of the piece
- Every decision to keep, change or cut
- Your name on it

A quick test. Could anyone else have written this sentence? If no one else could, it is yours to write.

## Your voice card

Your voice is the sum of small habits: short or long sentences, jokes or none, the words you would say out loud and the ones you never would. The AI does not know yours until you show it. A voice card is a short note you keep and paste into chats, so the AI starts from you instead of from "average writer." You build one in Task 3 and use it again when you draft and revise.

## Keep this in your pocket

1. The AI is a partner: ideas, structure, questions and edits. You write the sentences that carry the heart.
2. The true details only you know are what make a piece worth reading.
3. Keep a voice card, and show it to the AI before it writes anything.

## Practice

Three jobs, about 10 to 15 minutes each. Use claude.ai on the free version, plus Google Docs. Go to docs.google.com and open a blank document. Click the title at the top left and name it "My Writing Folder." You will keep all five lessons' work in it. Keep private details out. Use a nickname or initials instead of real names, and leave out addresses and health details. If a detail would hurt you if it leaked, change it. The Stay Safe track will cover privacy in full.

### Task 1: Choose your piece

**Why it matters:** a piece you care about keeps you going for five lessons. One you picked just to have something will be hard to finish.

**Do this:** pick one: a family letter, a newsletter item, or a short story, 400 to 700 words. In your Doc, type "My piece" as a heading and answer three lines in your own words, no AI: who is it for, what do I want them to feel or do, and what is one true detail only I know. Then paste the prompt below into a new Claude chat (click New chat at the top left of the page, or the plus sign if you see one) with your three answers filled in.

```text
I am writing a short piece. Here is what I know so far.
Reader: [who it is for]
What I want: [what I want them to feel or do]
One true detail: [your detail]
Do not write anything. Ask me five questions that would help me find what to say. Wait for my answers.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I wrote my three answers before I asked the AI anything.
- [ ] I chose a piece I would be glad to finish.
- [ ] I answered the five questions in my own words.
- [ ] I kept private details out, or changed them.

**What good looks like:** a heading and three clear lines in your Doc, plus a few of your answers to the questions. If the AI wrote a draft anyway, tell it "No draft yet, questions only" and carry on.

---

### Task 2: Sort the jobs

**Why it matters:** deciding what to hand over before you start is how you keep the piece yours.

**Do this:** in your Doc, make two headings, "I hand over" and "I keep." Sort the jobs below into them for your piece. There is no single right answer, but be ready to say why. Then open a new Claude chat, paste the prompt with your two lists filled in, and compare its sort to yours.

- Finding a better word for one I overuse
- Choosing which memory to open with
- Listing ten possible angles
- Writing the closing line
- Checking spelling
- Deciding what to cut
- Suggesting an order for my points
- Describing how the room looked

```text
Here are eight writing jobs and how I sorted them between "the AI can do this" and "I should do this myself." Tell me where you disagree and why. Do not change my mind just to be agreeable. Jobs and my sort: [paste your two lists]
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] Every job is in exactly one list.
- [ ] I can give a reason for each of my "I keep" choices.
- [ ] I noticed where the AI disagreed, and decided for myself.
- [ ] I changed a job's list only if I agreed with the reason.

**What good looks like:** a short sort you stand behind. The true details and the closing line usually land in "I keep." If the AI pushed you to hand over a job you care about, you may say no.

---

### Task 3: Build your voice card

**Why it matters:** the card lets you spot, in a second, a sentence that is not yours.

**Do this:** set a timer for five minutes. Write about your piece's subject in your own words, fast, without fixing anything. Then open a new Claude chat, send the prompt below with your writing pasted in, and read both versions. Mark every sentence in the AI's version that is not you. Last, in your Doc, under the heading "My voice card," write four lines: how long my sentences usually are, words I use, words I never use, and one habit, such as jokes or short punchy endings.

```text
Here is a paragraph I wrote about [subject]. Write a smooth, polished version of it, the way a generic writer would. Keep the same facts.
[paste your five minutes of writing]
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I wrote the fast version before reading the AI's.
- [ ] I marked at least two phrases in the AI's version that are not me.
- [ ] My voice card has all four lines.
- [ ] I did not paste anything private.

**What good looks like:** a card that would help a stranger sound like you. If your first card is vague, such as "friendly," go back to your fast writing and count the real habits.
