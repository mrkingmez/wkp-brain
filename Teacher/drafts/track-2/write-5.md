# Write with AI, 5: long projects and fact-checking

About 12 minutes to read, plus about 55 to do the practice

## What you will be able to do

- Keep a bigger project on track by feeding the AI summaries and outlines, not whole manuscripts.
- Say what a hallucination is and catch one in a paragraph.
- Check every claim in your own piece and finish with a one-page "my writing method" checklist.

## What you will make

A project card for a bigger writing job, a fact-check of an AI paragraph with a record of what held up, a fact-check of your own piece, and your one-page "my writing method" checklist. That page is the thing you take away.

## When the piece gets long

Your piece is short. One day you may write something longer: a family history booklet, a year of newsletters, a story with twelve chapters. The habits are the same, but one trap is new.

It is tempting to paste the whole thing into a chat and say "make it better." "Long tasks: files, chats and memory" covered why long chats drift and where to look up how much a chat can hold. There is a writing problem too. Ask about everything at once and the notes may be broad and hard to act on. Ask about one section and each note has something specific to point at. Try the same question both ways and see.

A better way is a **project card**. It is a short page you keep, with these parts.

- The goal and the reader, in two lines
- A one-line summary of each chapter or section
- The names, places, dates and facts that must stay consistent
- Your voice card and style note

Paste the card and only the section you are working on. Because the card holds the whole project in a page, any new chat can start from it, much like the handoff note.

## What a hallucination is

"What AI is, in plain words" showed that the AI writes what sounds right, and sometimes the answer comes out smooth and wrong. A made-up date, a book that does not exist, a quote nobody said, a street that is not on the map. People call these hallucinations.

In writing, watch the spots that want detail most: a number to make a point, a name to make it vivid, a quote to give it weight. A wrong claim can sound just as sure as a right one, so tone will not tell you.

## Verify every claim

"How to check what AI tells you" in the Stay Safe track has the full method. Here it is in short, for writing. Do it before anything leaves your hands.

1. **Underline every claim** that could be wrong: a date, a name, a number, a place, a quote, "always" and "never."
2. **Find a source that is not the AI.** The town's own website, the school, a document you hold, a family member who was there. Use an official page when you can.
3. **Mark it.** Matched, did not match, or could not find. "Could not find" means cut it or say it is your memory.
4. **Do not ask the AI to confirm its own claim.** The AI is not a source, so asking it again checks nothing.

For a family letter, the best sources are the people in it. Ask them.

## Keep this in your pocket

1. For long work, keep a project card and share summaries and the one section you need, not everything.
2. Smooth and sure does not mean true. Underline every claim and check it somewhere that is not the AI.
3. "Could not find" means cut it, or say plainly it is your memory.

## Practice

Three jobs, about 15 to 20 minutes each. Use claude.ai and your "My Writing Folder" Doc. Use made-up details for Task 1, and keep private details out everywhere.

### Task 1: Build a project card

**Why it matters:** the card is what makes a long project manageable.

**Do this:** imagine a family cookbook with eight chapters. Invent a one-line summary for each, such as "Chapter 3: Sunday dinners, mostly about the long table." Add three facts that must stay consistent, such as the family name, the year the recipes began, and who tells the stories. Put it all in your Doc under the heading "Project card." Then open a new Claude chat (click New chat at the top left of the page, or the plus sign if you see one) and send the prompt with your card pasted in.

```text
Here is a project card for a long writing project. Check it only. Which chapters overlap? What is missing? What facts could clash? Do not write any chapters. Card: [paste]
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] My card has a goal, a reader, eight summaries and three facts.
- [ ] I gave the AI the card, not a pile of text.
- [ ] I picked one overlap or gap and fixed it.
- [ ] I kept it made-up.

**What good looks like:** a one-page card with at least one change after the check. If the AI said everything was fine, ask "What would a reader find confusing?"

---

### Task 2: Catch a hallucination

**Why it matters:** you learn to spot it by finding one yourself.

**Do this:** think of something in your town you could check on its own official website, such as the library, a park or the school. Open a new Claude chat and send the prompt below. Underline every claim in the answer. Check each on the official page and mark matched, did not match, or could not find. Record the results in your Doc.

```text
Write a short paragraph about [a place or organization in my town] with at least four specific facts such as dates, names and numbers. After each fact, tell me where I can verify it.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I underlined every claim, not just the ones that looked odd.
- [ ] I checked each on a page that is not the AI.
- [ ] I marked each one matched, did not match, or could not find.
- [ ] I looked up any source it named, and noted if it did not exist.

**What good looks like:** a list with each claim marked. Wrong or missing results are normal and teach the most. If everything checks out, try the same prompt on a smaller place and see whether the answers get shakier.

---

### Task 3: Check your piece and write your method

**Why it matters:** this finishes your piece, and the checklist gives you the same quality next time.

**Do this:** underline every claim in your final piece: dates, names, numbers, places and quotes. Check each against a person or document that is not the AI. Cut or fix anything you cannot confirm. Then, in your Doc, write your one-page method. Start from the template below and change every line into your own words.

```text
MY WRITING METHOD
1. Choose: who is it for, what do I want, what true detail is mine?
2. Hand over: ideas, angles, questions. Keep: what I feel and the heart sentences.
3. Brainstorm: ask for many, mark two.
4. Outline: ask for three, borrow, challenge.
5. Draft: I write it. Context and my style note for any stuck paragraph.
6. Revise: "Do not rewrite. List and quote." Keep a log.
7. Check: underline every claim and verify it somewhere that is not the AI.
8. Read it aloud. Sign it.
```

**Self-check:**

- [ ] I checked every claim in my piece, not only the ones I doubted.
- [ ] I cut or fixed what I could not confirm.
- [ ] My method is in my own words and fits one page.
- [ ] I saved it where I will find it, and I know who gets the piece.

**What good looks like:** a checked piece ready to send, and a page you would hand a friend. If you find a wrong claim in your own writing, good. That is the check working.
