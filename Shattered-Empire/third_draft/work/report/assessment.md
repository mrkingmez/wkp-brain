# Whole-Story Assessment

## Part 1. Score and sub-ratings
Overall score: 8 out of 10 for the craft of the manuscript as it stands after Draft 2. It is a long, confident, team-driven fantasy with a real engine in the back half. The alpha reader's 7 out of 10 came from a reader who stopped near Chapter 9, which is the stretch where this draft is at its stiffest, so the number rises the further a reader goes. That is the main risk. A reader who stays past Chapter 9 gets a better book than the one who judged it there.

Sub-ratings (my own judgment, backed by the 55 chapter wraps and the Grain Table in the appendix):
- Cadence variety 9. The plan, brief, execute and adapt rhythm is intentional and it works. Chapter lengths vary on purpose.
- Voice differentiation 8. Harmon, Taerel, Ash, Garran, Aymir and Rouric are distinct on the page. Several others lean on word choice alone because dialect spelling is nearly absent (see Part 6).
- Stress escalation 9. The pass battle, the climb, the Stormhaven chapters and the dock all escalate cleanly.
- World texture 9. Cobblestones swept twice, the filtered helms in Forgehallow, the wet-wool smell of the stable. It is the book's best asset.
- Sensory grounding 9. Almost every scene starts in a body and a place.
- Emotional subtext 8. Strong in Nyssa, Dain, Aymir, Harmon and Garran. Weaker where a character explains a feeling the scene has already shown.
- Prose control 7. The recurring simile habit, the not-X-but-Y turn and a steady run of continuity slips hold this score down. All of them are fixable at the line level.
- Market fit is not scored. See Part 4.

## Part 2. Fluidity and sagging ranges
Fluid, in the order a reader meets them:
- Chapters 4, 6 and 9. The market, the balcony, the first town.
- Chapters 16 and 19 to 23. The first Pass set piece, the argument chapter, Luca's origin, the heist, the breach and the prison payoff.

- Chapters 37 to 46 and 49 to 53. The cavern camp, the poisoning, the Pass battle, Riven and Nyssa's ledge, the heist at Merrick's, the tower, the dock and the debrief.

Slower, with the reason:
- The Prologue to Chapter 3. Setup friction. Harmon and Adin's banter is good, but these chapters carry more backstory than motion, and a cold reader hits them first.
- Chapters 11 to 15. A run of setup, debrief and lesson chapters (the lesson scene, the Magistrate's tower, the storm and campfire, the inn war room). Each is good, but four in a row without a set piece slows the middle.
- Chapters 17 and 18. A breath after the strike, then two scenes where the second is the better. Fine alone, soft in sequence.
- Chapters 25, 27, 30 and 33. Quiet chapters by design. Chapter 30 has to make the Luca and Lysana romance land before Chapter 40, and it nearly does.
- Chapters 35 and 36. The book's best images set against its most tangled bookkeeping (the sanctum scene, the Stormhaven day). The slowness here is clarity, not pace.
- Chapter 48. The densest continuity load of any chapter (the carts, the splits, the family assignments). A clarity problem, not a pacing one.

## Part 4. Comparable works
Genre-fit comps (no sales claims, picked for how a reader would shelve this):
- Glen Cook, The Black Company (1984). A small company of professionals carrying a war, with dry humor and weight. Closest for team and tone.
- Steven Erikson, Gardens of the Moon (1999). Soldiers and mages on the edge of a larger war, no lone hero.
- Brandon Sanderson, Mistborn: The Final Empire (2006). Heist structure and a magic system that rewards attention. The target reader you named.
- Scott Lynch, The Lies of Locke Lamora (2006). A crew, a long con and a street-level voice.
- Nicholas Eames, Kings of the Wyld (2017). A band of aging specialists reassembled for one more job.

Length. The manuscript is about 235,000 words. I searched for current guidance and found no authoritative 2025 agent statement, and the sources I found disagree. One gives 90,000 to 120,000 for science fiction and epic fantasy, another gives 120,000 to 180,000 for epic fantasy specifically, several say the usual debut ceiling loosens for epic fantasy, and older forum threads report 125,000 to 175,000 for debuts. See [Word count lengths, epic fantasy (SFF Chronicles)](https://sffchronicles.com/threads/567622), [Twitter thread word counts for epic fantasy debuts](https://colony.litopia.com/threads/twitter-thread-words-counts-for-epic-fantasy-debuts-125k-175k.8584) and [How Many Words in a Novel by Genre (Squibler)](https://www.squibler.io/learn/writing/novel-writing/how-many-words-in-a-novel/). The honest read is that 235,000 is above every range I could source, so check each agent's submission guidelines before you query. Whether to trim is yours to decide, and I recommend settling it before the query letter, not after.

On your fear that no one would be interested: the team-first structure, the heist chapters and the Pass thread sit close to books readers already seek out. Nothing I read suggests the premise is the problem.

## Part 5. AI heat map, top 10 tells and your voice signatures
Overall heat: moderate, falling in the back half. Of 2,111 items, 597 carry the AI TELL tag (about 2.5 per thousand words). The heaviest chapters were Chapter 12 (Moderate to High), Chapters 47 and 48 (Moderate in the middle of the chapter), and most of the 20s to 40s ran Light to Moderate. The per-chapter table is in the appendix.

Top 10 tells, with the number of AI TELL items whose stated cause matched (keyword counts, so these are floors):
1. Stock similes, 86 items. The simile habit the alpha reader named (84 hits) is real, and I found stragglers beyond it.
2. The not-X-but-Y turn, 46 items. "Not panic. The particular stillness of..." and "was not silence. It was..."
3. A feeling explained after it was shown, 35 items.
4. Heartbeat, breath and body reactions, 33 items.
5. Aphorisms that close a paragraph, 19 items.
6. The "a man who" or "the way a man" simile, 17 items flagged by cause, with many more inside the stock-simile count.
7. "Weight" as the all-purpose abstract noun, 16 items.
8. "Particular" as a tic ("the particular ease", "the particular stillness"), 7 items.
9. The "He filed it" tic. Three uses in Chapter 52 alone, and it also shows in Chapters 2 and 50.
10. Stacked negations and silence described several times in one scene (Chapter 20 has six negation turns, and Chapter 50 describes silence four times).

Your voice signatures, the things I would protect: the dry deadpan in Harmon's lines ("Try not to die."), the concrete sensory opener ("the cobblestones swept twice"), the short fragment that ends a beat ("The river carried them on."), team banter that sounds like people who like each other, and openers that start inside a fact ("Luca's found an orc raider camp."). Those are the book. The edits aim at the scaffolding around them.

## Part 6. Voice and dialect report
Census across all 55 chapters: "ye" 0, "yer" 0, "reckon" 1, "ain't" 9, "'em" 9, "aye" 8, "gonna" 4, "gotta" 1, "y'all" 1, dropped-g forms 16. Dialect spelling is almost absent. Even Neavin's anchor chapter has one "ain't". The Annex asks for audible grain ("spice, not soup") and the book mostly delivers standard spelling for every speaker, so differentiation rides on word choice and rhythm. That works for the formal speakers (Taerel, Aldrek, Harmon, Rouric, the mirror) and is thinner for the Common speakers (Ash, Garran, Neavin, the dockhands). I did not flatten any intentional dialect, including Neavin's "ain't".

Where the voices are strongest: Taerel's formal dryness ("the company is poor, the pay irregular, and the lodging a stable"), Aymir's clear structured report, Harmon's warm steel, Ash's armor of jokes, and Rouric's cold register. Where they blur: some Common-speaker dialogue is interchangeable between Ash, Garran and the dock crowd, and some narration drifts into one narrator voice (the "particular" and "kind of" phrasing). The plural "gods" lines are logged as questions, never changed, per the God stays rule. The Eight and the Nine, Wymark and Wyman, and the first-name uses (OD-2) are logged, not decided.

## Part 8. Method log
- Pass structure. Each chapter was prepared by a mechanical script (spelling, spacing, quotes, names, locked-spelling hits), then read line by line, then written as one md file, then verified by script, then logged in `progress.json`.
- Scripts, in `D:\WKP\scratch`. `se_extract`, `se_prep`, `se_notes`, `se_mech`, `se_dialect`, `se_verify`, `se_progress`, `se_stats`, `se_wraps`, `se_mf`, `se_assemble`.
- Totals. 55 sections and 2,111 items. Tags (an item can carry more than one) are MECHANICS 489, CONTINUITY 428, AI TELL 597, FORESHADOW 265, VOICE 228, ZAC NOTE 123, CUT ONLY 50, ADD 5. Severity is Must fix 79, Should fix 405, Polish 1,627.
- Verification. Every chapter passes. The verifier requires the flagged line to appear exactly once in the source, forbids em dashes in my output, forbids colons in options and fixes, and rejects the locked-spelling variants inside options.
- What I did not do. I did not edit any source file. I did not run a full-manuscript diff (see front matter). I did not call the writing-room agents (dialogue-doctor, originality, fact-research) at chapter-group breaks or for the comps. The comps are my own, and the word-count guidance is from a web search, not from fact-research. If you want those passes, they are the next job.
- Decisions I did not make. OD-1 (Stormhold in Chapter 4), OD-2 (Drake's first name, printed as "Meridian" in Chapters 1, 24, 36, 38 and 49), OD-3 (Lysa and Camryn), OD-4 (Dain's surname), OD-5 (the Chapter 16 questions), the Chapter 38 day count, and OD-7 (the Chapter 50 leader) are left as questions.
