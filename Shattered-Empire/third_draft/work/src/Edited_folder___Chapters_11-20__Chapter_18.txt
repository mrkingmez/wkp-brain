After breakfast, Aurelia and Weslin returned to their rooms, a small chamber tucked beside Harmon’s command space. The morning had a hush, the kind that settled in just before the weight of the day truly began. Aurelia moved with quiet purpose. Weslin, by contrast, seemed energized, his steps quicker, eyes alert, as if ready for another lesson before the first flame cooled.

The chamber was dim, touched by the soft spill of morning light slipping through slatted shutters. Aurelia sat cross-legged across from Weslin, her long sleeves rolled back to her elbows, a yellow shard resting gently in her palm like a piece of captured sunlight. Her eyes were closed, calm and composed. A single candle burned between them on the floor, its small flame flickering in tune with breath, alive in its own way. 

Weslin sat. The candle between them leaned slightly toward him and then thought better of it.  Weslin mirrored her posture, shoulders square, back straight, though a thin line of tension traced his brow.

She opened one eye, then the other. Something in her expression shifted as she looked at him, not quite settling, like a woman checking a face against a memory she wasn't sure was hers. "Did you practice what I showed you yesterday?" she asked, voice soft, the kind that invited honesty.

Weslin gave a small nod. “Yeah. Twice." He almost smiled. "Before bed, I kept losing count. But this morning it stayed.”

Aurelia's expression warmed, something close to pride. "Good," she said quietly. "That is the work. Most want the power and refuse the patience it takes to hold it." She leaned forward slightly. “And while you were in your meditative state… what did you hear?”

Weslin blinked, uncertain. “I… didn’t know I was supposed to be listening for anything,” he admitted, the words tripping over each other slightly.

A soft smile curved her lips. “Ah. That’s the trouble, then.” She closed her hand around the shard and tapped it once with her fingertip. “There is a difference between a door opening and a door being opened. One is weather. The other is will. ”

She sat back, spine straight as a drawn bow, her voice turning lyrical, almost like reciting an old mantra.

“Hearing is passive. It slips in through cracks. Wind at your window. Footsteps behind you. The hush before a lie.” Weslin’s eyes dropped, absorbing her words.

She continued,"Listening… listening is a choice." She said the word again, and for just a moment her voice went somewhere else, somewhere older. Then she pulled it back. "You lean into it. Your skin. Your bones. The silence between sounds. It is intentional. It is an invitation." 

“You can hear anything,” she continued, tapping her temple. “But are you listening? To truly hear, you have to stop speaking over what you want to believe and start listening. That’s what the shard teaches, too.”

She opened her hand. The shard glowed faintly in her palm, golden and pulsing like a heartbeat caught in crystal.

“This,” she said, “is older than any book, any tower, any old law. Once, magic flowed like underground rivers, fluid, wild, nourishing,” she said quietly, as if reminding herself as much as him. “The world drank from it like a spring. It wasn’t studied or summoned; it was simply felt. The ethereal plane wasn't some distant concept; it was stitched into the air itself.” She passed the shard to him gently. It pulsed warm in his hand.

“But then the breach was sealed. And the ether, cut off from its source, began to harden. Not like ice. Not like clay. Like stone. A deep, cosmic stillness took hold, and it flowed no more. And when the pressure became too much…” She tapped the shard. “It shattered. Fractured into the shards we now hold.”

Weslin stared at it, brows drawn. “But that happened a long time ago. So… how do we still have shards? Where are they all coming from?”

Aurelia gave a knowing smile, the kind that suggested a deeper truth had just been nudged into view.

“Good question. Most don’t think to ask it. The answer is… mine. The ether still leaks through, here and there, or so we believe.” Her fingers curled slightly, the certainty thinning at the edges. “Without it, the world would wither. But it doesn’t stay soft. As soon as it hits our plane, it begins to solidify again. Crystallize. Become what you hold in your hand.”

Her tone shifted, growing heavier and more political. “These places become rich veins,” she said. “Most of the time they are unstable and never where you want them. Few last long. Fewer are worth the blood it takes to hold them. Still, even one can elevate a nation.” She looked at him with a quiet intensity.

“Once, adepts didn’t need to be gifted; they were simply in tune. They knew the songs, the feel of current beneath skin. But now? The bloodline is thinning. The knowledge is fading. Fewer are born who can touch the current. And we call this the Debt now, like they owe the world an apology for being empty.”

She shook her head slightly, voice a little sad. “But the shards… they still remember. They still radiate.” She went quiet. Not for him. For something else, behind her eyes, something that had no business being in this room. Then she blinked, and it was gone, and she was simply Aurelia again, sitting across from a boy with a glowing stone in his hand. 

Weslin looked down at the crystal in his hand. It glowed faintly, the light licking at his palm.

She tilted her head. “Do you remember the feast at Opindale? The night of your first touch?”

He nodded slowly. “Yeah… Harmon gave me a mission and a gift.”

Her brow lifted slightly, inviting more.

“It was a pen,” Weslin said. “He handed it to me before I went off to the servants’ quarters. Told me to carry it, keep it close. He went as far as putting on my tunic. Said it might help me… hear things clearer. I didn’t know what he meant at first, but I was there to gather whispers, gossip, nothing official. He must’ve suspected I was adept… wanted to see if I could pick up anything passively.”

Aurelia nodded thoughtfully. “A test. That makes sense. He did tell me that.”

“I didn’t even realize there was a shard in it until later. I just… started hearing things. Voices that weren’t spoken. Thoughts, maybe. Like feelings turned into echoes. I thought I was imagining it. But then...”

His hands curled slightly around the crystal.

“Then Braden came at me, and everything just lit up. Not like fire, more like fury. Like lightning behind my eyes. I didn’t even know what I was doing, but he screamed. Said he felt like he was burning.”

Aurelia nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she already suspected. “That’s because you invoked the shard. There are two ways to use it. The first is passive, its presence alone fuels your gifts, strengthens your senses, sharpens what you already have.”

She raised one finger.

“But the second is to call it in. To pull its essence through you. That’s what you did when you were afraid. Or angry. Or both. It wasn’t training, it was instinct. We will go over the differences later and what it all means.”

She let the moment settle.

“But that path, while powerful, is dangerous if untamed. You can burn through your spirit faster than your body can follow.”

She reached out and gently pressed the shard in his hand back toward him.

“Today, we’re only learning the first path. Not invocation. Just… feeling.”

Weslin nodded, brow furrowed in focus.

“The shard radiates,” she continued, rising smoothly to her feet. “Like a sun under water. Its energy reaches, even if you’re not ready to reach back. I want you to sit. Breathe. And when you feel it, not think you feel it, but truly, raise your hand. Then, still with your eyes closed, point in the direction of the shard.”

He nodded again, slower this time.

She moved a few feet back, setting the shard in a small stone bowl, letting its light spill faintly against the walls.

“Now breathe. We’ll do this several times. Each time, I’ll place the shard further away. You’ll learn to feel its call from a distance. It’s like learning to see heat in winter, or smell rain before it falls.”

She stood, quiet and composed, and began to walk the circle around him. “You’re going to learn the language of the shard, Weslin.”

The morning had broken pale and cold over Forgehollow, the rising sun weak behind a swathe of silver clouds. A fine dusting of snow clung to the cobbled streets and iron-shod roofs, crunching lightly beneath boot and wheel. Smoke coiled from the chimneys in long, lazy trails, and the scent of coalfires and molten metal hung in the crisp air like a cloak.

Dain adjusted the silver clasp at his collar and gave his reflection a final glance in the warped mirror by the Iron Flask’s hearth. He wore a tailored charcoal tunic with blue satin lining and an embroidered half-cloak clasped with his family crest, subtle but unmistakably noble. Beside him, Brother Aldrek had exchanged his road-worn robes for ceremonial vestments: layered ivory and gray with a modest chain of prayer stones resting across his chest.

“Shall we?” Dain asked, sweeping back the curtain at the door as a gust of cold air kissed their faces.

The two departed the Iron Flask and made their way through Forgehollow’s bustling mid-morning traffic, their boots brushing snow from iron-grated steps and flagstones. They followed the direction of a name passed quietly by a merchant the evening prior: The Gilded Chamber.

“It’s where the quiet hands and loud purses meet,” the man had said over a mug of hot ale. “If you want whispers with weight, that's the place.”

Dain had heard the name again that morning from the blacksmith tending the horses. “Some call it a dining hall, others a council by candlelight. Either way, you’ll find the city's decision-makers elbow-deep in spiced wine and deals.”

They arrived at mid-bell, the Gilded Chamber nestled within the second level of the central tower's south arm, above the city's ancient forge archive. Its carved archway doors were flanked by wrought-iron sconces shaped like hammerheads, flickering with amber light. 
Two attendants in dark green livery opened the doors.
Warmth embraced them at once. The scent of cinnamon-spiced cider and roasting meats filled the air. Heavy velvet curtains framed a long hall whose black-steel columns were veined with gold and copper. Firelight flickered from chandelier bowls hung on chains, casting moving armor-shapes across the vaulted ceiling.
A steward approached, a wiry man with gray-streaked hair and eyes like frosted glass.
“Welcome to the Chamber,” he said with a shallow bow. “May I assist you, my lords?”
“You may. I’ve only just arrived in Forgehollow,” Dain said smoothly. “I am Lord Alric Rendsdale of House Emberlight. A minor line. I was sent by my father to explore new routes, particularly ones in Aldoria, now that the new treaty has been signed in Opindale between Opindale, Aldoria, and Moradaer. Naturally, we seek a favorable passage and exchange.”
He glanced subtly toward the gathering crowd. “House Emberlight is prepared to offer generous terms to those aligned with our interests.” He pressed a gold coin into the steward’s palm; the man’s demeanor brightened instantly.

“But of course,” the steward said, slipping the coin away and gesturing toward the room. “First, beneath the tapestry of the Old Forge, that’s Lord Veyric Bram, overseer of the mountain routes and riverways. If it flows, he taxes it. Rumor says he drinks more than he earns, yet his coffers never dry.”
“To your left,” the steward continued, “is Lady Elsin Corveira. Fortunes in horse breeding, half the northern stables are hers. She once gifted the Emperor a mare, believed to be faster than the wind. She’s… selective with company.”
He pointed again. “Count Hollus Fenwright there, carriages, freight lines, and those new iron wagons from the west. He’s pouring coin into something he calls an engine, a wheeled forge that moves on rails and never tires. Financed half the prototypes running the mountain routes.” Beside him, Lord Trellik Houn, a forge baron with a worker’s build and scarred hands, examined a crescent-bladed weapon with his apprentices.
“If you’ll excuse me,” the steward said as a noblewoman in violet beckoned.
“Thank you,” Dain replied, letting the polite smile fade once the man turned. The mask of Lord Rendsdale slipped; the hunter beneath watched the room instead.
“Circle the room,” he told Aldrek quietly. “See what words they offer a man of faith.”
Aldrek bowed and moved among the crowd.
While Dain traded easy conversation and gathered fragments, Bram’s debts, Corveira’s tourney plans, Fenwright’s rail contracts, Aldrek walked softly through the gathering, offering blessings and brief prayers. A mother of two asked for protection for a son sent to the front; an aging knight for strength to endure winter. One woman confessed guilt for poisoning her husband’s mistress; Aldrek answered only with a grave silence and a quiet prayer.
Dain eventually reached the long oak bar at the hall’s edge. The air thickened with spiced wine and the murmur of discreet deals. He rested one hand on the polished wood, then a voice cut cleanly through the noise behind him.

“Lord Rendsdale,” came a voice from behind, smooth, clipped, and pretending to be polite.

Dain turned slightly. Lord Iven Merrick stood there, dressed like old money, the kind that arrives in the right room and hopes no one checks the date on the coin. His ring was too big, his robe too new, his smile too practiced.
“My lord,” Dain said with a small bow. “Forgive me. I don’t believe we’ve been introduced. But then, introductions are so rarely honest in places like this, aren’t they?”
“Indeed,” Merrick smiled. “Masks are more useful,” he said lightly, “when you already know the face beneath them.”
“A charming philosophy,” Dain replied smoothly. “I prefer to let the mask do the work. It’s usually better dressed.”
A brief chuckle passed between them, polite and sharp. The music from the quartet faded to a single violin line as the space between them tightened.
“You caused quite the ripple,” Merrick murmured. “Poking about the Bastion. Asking after ghosts.”
“Did I?” Dain sipped his whiskey. “I have a fondness for forgotten things—old swords, lost names, the occasional man the world misplaces.”
“A romantic, then?”
“A collector.”
Merrick leaned in, quiet now, like a man who has stopped needing volume. "Tell me, Lord Rendsdale, what is it you're really looking for in Forgehollow? Or shall we keep dancing with riddles and pretty metaphors until one of us steps on the other's toes? ”

Dain turned fully now, facing him square. “My toes are rather resilient. But if we’re dancing, I’d like to lead.”

“Brave,” Merrick studied him a moment longer than courtesy allowed. “Curious,” he said at last. “Your name never crossed my desk.”

Dain chuckled. “Titles are like wine labels. Lovely to look at, but ultimately meaningless unless what’s inside holds weight.”

“I find bloodlines hold weight.”

“Yours or someone else’s?”

Merrick’s smile faltered, just a hair. Dain pressed, gently, like testing the edge of a blade. I admire the work, actually," Dain said, and he almost meant it. "Most men born to titles never had to decide if they deserved one. You did.”

“Easier to forge than inherit, wouldn’t you say?” said Merrick.

“Easier, yes. But rarely done without the smell of soot,” replied Dain.

A breath of silence. Their smiles had not changed. That was the problem. The laughter around them dulled, sensing pressure neither man acknowledged. 
The noise around them swelled, voices rising just enough that neither man needed to lower his own. They fenced in words, each smile fixed, neither man blinking.
At last, Dain said quietly, “Some of us carry our scars in the open. Others hide them in prisons.”

Merrick stilled. “You wound me, my lord.”

Dain gave a soft laugh. “Was that too plainspoken for polite company?” He paused to really take in Merrick, and how his attention had not left Dain for a second since this had begun, finding it almost enjoyable, he realized, before continuing, “Then let’s call it what it is, a conversation between two liars, pretending we still believe in truth.”

Merrick studied him for a moment, the humor never quite leaving his face, but something colder behind his eyes now.

“You’re looking for him,” he said, not a question, his grip tightening imperceptibly on the stem of his goblet. “And you think he’ll tell you something I won’t.”

“I think ghosts talk more freely than kings.”
“Do you believe in ghosts, Lord Rendsdale?”

“I believe in men who become myths because someone powerful needed them forgotten.”

Merrick’s voice lowered. “You come into my city wearing a borrowed name, sniffing after relics locked away before your crest ever existed. And all I see is a man afraid he’s already too late.”
Dain didn’t flinch. “And you,” he said, raising his glass, “are a man who thinks owning the key makes him the keeper of the vault.”
Merrick laughed. “Oh, gods, you think you’re the only one looking for the lock. Is that all this is?”
“No,” Dain said, smiling now. “This is just foreplay. The fun is still to come.”
He turned and left the words hanging like a drawn blade.
“Tell me one thing,” Merrick called softly after him. “Why him? Why Riven Duskwind?”
Without turning, Dain’s reply came low and razor-edged. “Because sometimes the most broken blade still remembers how to cut.”
Merrick turned on his heel with a flourish of his dark emerald cloak, clearly intending to make a show of retreat. His boots clicked smartly across the marble, once, twice, thrice, but then he paused.

Stillness, except the quartet in the far corner, easing into something older than either man would admit knowing.

Merrick turned.

Not slowly, not dramatically, but like a man snapping shut a well-oiled dagger.

He strolled back toward Dain, not a trace of hurry in him, only that dangerous kind of ease worn by men who have survived rooms that wanted them dead.

“Forgive me,” he said. “It’s unlike me to walk away from an unfinished riddle. Especially when the answer’s standing right in front of me, wearing Estmere silk and smugness like a tailored sin.”

Dain grinned around the rim of his glass and took a small, thoughtful sip. “You flatter me. Though I suspect your compliments come with a receipt.”

“I don’t give receipts,” Merrick said. “I write ledgers. And I never forget a debt.”

From across the hall, a man-servant in crimson livery hurried toward them, sweating beneath his silver tray. Merrick extended a hand without even glancing. The goblet arrived too late for his liking.

He took the drink, tasted it, and scowled.

“Warm,” he muttered, then turned a sideways look at the man. “You had one task, Albin.”

Albin mumbled an apology and disappeared faster than he’d arrived.

Merrick watched him go, then turned back to Dain. “Forgive the staff. Good help is rarer than truth these days.”

Dain’s grin sharpened. “You might try treating them like people. I hear it’s all the rage in civilized circles.”

Merrick chuckled, but there was no warmth in it. “I didn’t claw my way to a title by chasing fads.”

“No,” Dain said. “You bought it. I imagine it came wrapped in velvet and tied with the blood of better men.”

Merrick stepped closer, close enough that the flickering candlelight danced in both their eyes. “Wealth may not birth rights,” he said, “but it buries them just fine.”

“You speak like a man who’s buried a few himself.”

“I speak like a man who knows where they’re buried.” Merrick’s voice dropped. “And where is the next plot waiting?”

That drew a genuine smile from Dain, not the smirk of the moment before, but something darker. “Careful,” he said. “That almost sounded like poetry.”

Merrick looked mock-wounded. “My apologies. I forgot myself.”

“You always remember yourself,” Dain said, circling him now, slow, unhurried. “You remember your name, your holdings, your forged title, your silver tongue. But what I wonder, Lord Merrick, is whether you remember what it was like to be small.”

A flicker of something in Merrick’s gaze, pride, maybe, or shame poorly buried.

“I remember enough,” Merrick replied. “Enough to know that small men don’t get to ask big questions.”

Dain's reply came low and silk-slick. “Ah, but small men, they ask the best ones. Because they know the cost of not knowing.” He stepped closer now, and the air between them sharpened. “You want to know why I’m here.”

Merrick didn’t answer.

“I am here because someone is meddling with things he does not understand. I want our shadow back, and I want to know what you want with him,” Dain continued. “Not the ghost. Not the myth. The man you’ve shackled somewhere below your Iron Halls.”

Merrick’s brow arched. “Careful, Lord Rendsdale. You’re bleeding through your lie.”

“So are you. It is one thing to build a lie for your life, it's all something else when you try to make others play along.”

Silence. The kind that draws curious eyes from across the room.

Merrick sipped his wine again, thoughtfully this time, and turned slightly as if admiring the golden chandelier above them. “Yet I see a man so desperate to be relevant again, you have to swing for the bottom.”

Dain didn’t flinch. He smiled and gave a half-bow, courteous and mocking all at once. Speaking so only Merrick could hear, “At least you are willing to see you are the bottom, just another beggar at the feast.” Their eyes met. “Good evening, Lord Merrick.”

He turned, this time for real, letting the weight of their final words hang like a guillotine. Just as fast as it started, he was gone, leaving only the taste of smoke and secrets behind.

The crowd filled the silence. Soon after he left, laughter rushed back like air into lungs when a heavier presence joined Dain at the bar.

Brother Aldrek. Taller than most, robed in deep burgundy trimmed with silver, the sigil of the Creator’s Flame etched at the collar. His mace hung peace-bound at his side, though his gaze never needed binding.
He lifted a hand to his brow, then chest, a gesture of faith and signal both. “Hold a breath, Brother. Eyes are on us. Let them think we pray, not plot.” They spoke in fragments after that. Names without detail. Conclusions without paths.
Aldrek stepped beside him, placing his right hand palm down on the table. Dain mirrored the gesture. Heads bowed, they formed the posture of a ritual blessing used before meals or decisions. The murmur around them softened automatically.
“I talked to the miners’ guild,” Aldrek said, tracing a slow circle over his heart before laying his hand atop the first. Dain followed suit.
“Lord Tannith owns the lease north of Forgehollow’s old silver mine. Long abandoned. But Merrick bought it six months ago.”
“Merrick owns Tannith’s old mine, but why?”
“Looks that way. The miners haven’t worked since,” Aldrek said, then paused. “They were pushed off. Quietly. Yet with pay, my guess is to stay silent.”
“Paying a crew to do nothing on land no one’s allowed near…” Dain murmured.
“Vault site,” Aldrek said flatly. “Has to be.”
He touched two fingers to his brow and lips, a silent invocation, then took a swig from his tin flask. Dain mirrored the motion with his wineglass; to onlookers, it seemed like a prayer.
“Two dockhands told me they saw a group leaving past midnight three nights ago,” Aldrek continued. “No lights. Cart tracks deep as trenches. Thought smugglers, until they heard chains.”
“Chains?”
“Not on cargo. On people.”
The silence that followed was heavy.
“Maybe he brought in free labor to move something. I don’t want to think about what else it could be. The stories about these so-called vaults, and what it takes to open them.” 
Brother Aldrek paused to clean his mind of the images of that before picking back up, “One off Ironrun’s men, a guard, heard Merrick drunk-bragging that he was hiring a lockbreaker. Needed someone to open ‘etched warding’ on a private vault.”
Dain’s hand tightened on his glass before he lifted it and held the cup aloft, three seconds, then down again. A sign of restraint.
“Put it all together,” Aldrek muttered. “Doesn’t sing a song, but it hums the tune.”
They sat quietly for a moment. Then Aldrek’s eyes swept the room once more. “Figured you’d want to know.”
He rose, closing the benediction. Dain stood with him. Just as he reached for his glass, a small messenger boy, no older than eleven, came right up to him. Dain glanced at Aldrek. Aldrek gave the smallest shake of his head. Neither of them knew the boy. 
The boy didn’t look at him at all, only slipped the note into Dain’s palm as if following practiced instructions. The boy just vanished. Dain read it once. He folded it with the same care a man uses when he does not want to look like something has shaken him. He passed it to Aldrek. He took it, read it, and handed it back without speaking. Neither of them looked at the other. 
The two men turned back to the counter in silence, lifting their drinks once more. Beyond them, the laughter of Forgehollow rolled on, unaware of the storm it had just birthed.
