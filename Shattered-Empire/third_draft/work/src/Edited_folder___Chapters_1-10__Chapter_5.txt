Harmon Diem stepped through the great carved oak doors of the Grand Hall, and sound struck him first. By the time he entered the Grand Hall, night had fallen over Opindale. The torches blazed, as a tide of voices rolled across the vast chamber, laughter colliding with the ring of goblets, the scrape of chairs, the low murmur of a hundred side conversations. The vaulted ceiling soared above, beams of blackened oak rising into shadow. From them hung the banners of the three great realms: Opindale’s blue and black edged in gold, Aldoria’s crimson and silver, Moradaer’s dark green and onyx. Firelight from iron sconces made the colors shimmer across the stone, turning fabric into flame.
The air itself seemed alive. Spiced wine steamed from golden flagons, roasted boar glistened with a honey glaze, and the scent of fresh-baked bread wafted from the servers weaving between the long trestle tables. Harmon breathed it in as he walked, though the feast meant little to him. He measured people, not platters.
The envoys from Aldoria sat with a practiced, brittle stiffness, their whispers shielded by silver goblets while their gazes flickered toward every speaker like cornered prey. Opposite them, the Moradaer delegation maintained a veneer of unshakable composure, a calculated display of power that stood in silent defiance of the hall's cacophony. Between these factions sat the High Families of Opindale, a sea of rustling silks and jewels that flashed like signal fires in the torchlight. Here, elegance and steel were inseparable. Every gentle smile was sharpened by a hidden intent. Harmon read it the way he read a battlefield. Every position chosen. Every alliance is visible in who leaned toward whom. The feast was real enough. The warmth was not. 
At the far end, the King’s table dominated the dais, raised high above the floor. King Edric Wymark sat with practiced ease, every gesture carefully measured to strike a balance between warmth and authority. At his side, Queen Mirelle shone like a jewel set against the court’s splendor. Her gown of deep violet was threaded with silver that caught the firelight, and though her smile was gentle, her eyes missed nothing. Harmon noted how she leaned in when Edric spoke, the two moving as one, their presence steadying the dais as if the whole realm drew strength from their bond. Nobles closest to them shifted, eager to bask in the couple’s notice.
Harmon’s gaze shifted beyond them, and there his breath caught. High Seer Rhanis Tal-Kor sat swathed in layered robes heavy enough for a winter storm. The hall was warm with fire and bodies, yet the Seer’s garb seemed to swallow him whole. A prickling sensation crept up Harmon’s neck, the same instinct that had stirred before battlefield ambushes. He kept his expression still, but unease lingered. He had learned long ago not to dismiss such feelings too quickly.
He pressed forward, each step drawing him nearer the dais. Courtiers bowed as he passed, his presence too commanding to ignore even when he wished it otherwise. He inclined his head only slightly, eyes never straying long from the King.
At last, he reached the dais and bent in a formal bow. Edric rose, smiling as he raised his goblet, and the hall echoed with cheer at the King’s casual toast to “old friends and loyal hearts.” All eyes turned to Harmon, waiting for the famed knight to raise a cup in return. But Harmon’s hands stayed empty. He did not reach for the wine a servant pressed toward him, nor join the wave of lifted goblets. Instead, he touched a finger lightly to his nose, the smallest of signals, but one that carried more weight between him and Edric than a dozen shouted toasts. The King caught it at once, his smile never faltering, though the lines at the corner of his eyes tightened.
The cheer rolled on, but for Harmon it seemed dull, as if stone walls had muffled the sound. He saw only the King, and the King saw him. When Edric leaned close, his voice slipped beneath the noise of the hall.
“What have you learned, Harmon?” he whispered.
“Not enough. This requires privacy,” Harmon said, elevating his register without ornament.
He gave the smallest nod. The joy of the feast no longer touched him. Whatever lightness lingered in the hall, the weight in the King’s whisper told him this night would not end in merriment. Behind the pageantry, something dangerous was already moving, and he would soon be at its center.
The King placed both hands on Harmon’s shoulders, his grip firm beneath the guise of greeting, his voice carrying easily to those nearby, but his eyes spoke of urgency. When he returned to his seat, his gaze slid across the dais to the Governor’s chair.
It was empty. 
For a moment, the sight of the vacant chair seemed to swallow the hall. Murmurs stirred among the tables, rippling outward like a restless tide. Delegates from Aldoria stiffened visibly, their governor’s absence a shadow over their honor. The Moradaer envoys leaned together in hushed calculation, while the High Families of Opindale whispered behind jeweled hands. Even the musicians faltered before resuming their tune, the false brightness only sharpening the unease.
Harmon caught the shift instantly. A noble missing might pass unnoticed. But the Governor of Aldoria was gone. He kept his face composed, but inwardly he felt the weight settle.
The King's hand moved and a page appeared, bowing close at his shoulder. Harmon could not hear what passed between them. He did not need to. He watched Edric's face the way a man watches weather on an open plain, looking for the signs before the storm arrives. The page spoke three words. Edric's jaw tightened once, barely, the kind of movement that only meant something if you already knew what to look for. Then the mask was back, seamless, and the page withdrew. 
The boy withdrew only a step, then moved toward Harmon, bending close. “My lord,” he murmured, “the King requests you join him at once.” His eyes flicked nervously to the empty chair before lowering again.
Harmon gave a single, sharp nod. He rose smoothly. With a bow, the boy escorted Harmon through the side passage toward the smaller council chamber.
Inside, the council chamber, the air was quieter, heavy with the scent of wax and cedar. The chamber lay just off the Grand Hall, a place reserved for councils too delicate for the ears of courtiers. Shelves of leather-bound volumes lined the walls, beside tall windows that overlooked the torchlit courtyards. A broad oak table dominated the center, its surface scattered with rolled maps, sealed letters, and the gleam of an astrolabe half-buried in parchment. A pair of iron braziers cast steady light across the room, lending warmth against the chill that seemed to seep from the stone.
Governor Drake was already there, seated stiffly at the table, his face pale from worry. Chancellor Ayhmeri stood nearby, his thin hands clasped before him, gaze fixed on the maps as though he had been studying them while waiting. Both men looked up as the side door opened and King Edric entered with Harmon at his side.
The door shut softly behind them, sealing away the noise of the Grand Hall. For a moment, no one spoke, the crackle of the braziers filling the silence. Then Edric moved to the head of the table, his expression carved with the weight of fresh reports.
“You’ve heard what I have,” Edric said, his voice low yet authoritative. “Stormhaven struck. The pass is aflame. Beyond that… rumor and smoke.” His gaze shifted to Harmon, yet his words were steady, grave. Edric folded his hands. “Do you have a plan?”
“Yes, I do,” Harmon replied, his voice steady. “And it’s not an army.”
The King’s eyes narrowed, studying him with new intrigue. “Go on, then.”
"A strike team. No more than thirteen," Harmon said, resting both hands on the table. "Fast. Unseen. In, learn, out before they know." 
Edric leaned back, considering. When he spoke again, he came with the weight of the crown, “Very well,” Edric said. “Take whom you deem fit from the garrison.”
Harmon shook his head. “Not this time. Soldiers win battles. This requires something else. It would take too long to train anyone the skills this would require. I will use alternative means to acquire the talents needed. Men and women often looked past.”
"You would trust hired loyalties with this?" "Harmon." The King said it with the weight of a man who had heard many requests. "You ask more than most men would dare. From anyone else, this conversation would already be finished." s?”
"Because an army goes in blind. It makes noise. It draws attention. It commits before it knows what it is committing to. And then it dies." He looked at the King. "I have written enough of those letters. This will leave us with a bigger force when the fighting begins.” Harmon looked over the room, “It will limit those who will die.”
“I sense another request,” Edric said, steepling his fingers. “Go on.” 
“Yes, my grace,” Harmon said. “If this succeeds, I will step down. The team becomes mine.” The words came out steadier than he felt. He had carried them long enough that they should have been easy by now. They were not. He paused, clearing his throat. “For those who stay, I will need full pardons.”
The King considered him. "You know, Harmon, you do ask a lot. If anyone else asked, the answer would be no.”
Harmon did not move. He had heard kings consider things before. It was rarely the same as doing them. Ayhmeri's voice broke his reverie.“That is something to consider, Your Sire, it could prove to be a significant asset.”
"My King," Harmon said. "The pardons must be granted. Gold alone will not hold these people."
Edric was quiet for a moment. His eyes moved to the maps, then back. "Pardons are not a small thing, Harmon."
"No," Harmon agreed. "They are not."
The braziers crackled. Drake was watching the door. Ayhmeri had gone still in the way that chancellors go still when they are listening to everything.
"What you are asking," Edric said carefully, "is for the crown to extend its grace to people it has never seen, for a mission it cannot acknowledge, led by a man who will no longer carry its name when this is finished."
There it was. Said plainly enough for the room to follow. Harmon met his eyes.
"That is what I am asking."
Edric looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes that had nothing to do with strategy.
"If I release you from the title," Edric said, his voice measured, his words chosen for the room, "what does the crown receive in return?"
"The mission completed," Harmon said. "Stormhaven answered. The northern threat assessed." He paused. "And something the crown does not currently have. A capability that carries no name and leaves no record. One that will still be there when you need it."
Ayhmeri's hands unclasped and reclasped slowly. He understood the shape of it.
Drake understood nothing except that no one had said no yet.
Edric turned his goblet once on the table. It was a small gesture. Harmon had seen it before. He had seen it when Edric was seven years old, sitting in a chair too large for him, turning a cup he was not supposed to have, working through something he did not yet have the words for.
"And if I need to call on that capability," Edric said quietly. "In a moment that truly requires it."
"Then you call," Harmon said. "And I will answer."
Not because you command it. The words were not spoken. They did not need to be.
"As a friend," Edric said.
"As a friend," Harmon said.
The room was still. Ayhmeri looked at the maps. Drake pressed both hands flat on the table and breathed.
Then Edric looked away. Just for a moment. Just long enough. His jaw worked once, the way a man's jaw works when he is deciding whether to say something he will not be able to unsay. He looked back.
The mask was nearly all the way back when he spoke. "The pardons are granted." His voice was the king's voice. Steady. Final. "The title passes when the mission is complete." He straightened his robes. "See that it is."
Harmon inclined his head. "It will be."
Edric nodded once. Then he looked at Harmon the way you look at someone you have known your entire life and are not ready to stop knowing, and said nothing, because there was nothing left that the room could hear.
Harmon held his gaze for one breath longer than necessary.
That was enough.
Governor Drake took this moment to speak up. “Stormhaven?” Drake asked. His voice cracked. “My family?” 
Harmon's attention shifted to the Governor, “I will find them,” Harmon said. “That is my oath.”
The governor’s shoulders shook, and for a moment, he pressed both hands to his eyes. When he lowered them, his face was wet. “Then… then you will have my gold. Every coin I can muster. Only bring them back to me.”
The King exchanged a long look with Harmon, then with his Chancellor. Ayhmeri gave a single grave nod.
Edric exhaled and rose from his chair. He crossed to Harmon without hurry. “Assemble your team. You have my blessing. May God, the Creator, bless it as well.” 
Harmon clasped back, feeling the weight of the moment. “I will have a package delivered early tomorrow. It will be from my head steward, our means of communication while I am gone. We plan to ride before dawn.”
“Very well,” said the King. He released Harmon and crossed to Governor Drake, gripping the man’s arm with quiet strength. “We will do what we can. I promise.”
Drake’s throat closed with emotion. “Thank you, sire,” he managed, voice trembling.
Harmon stepped forward, took the governor’s hand in his own, and held his gaze. Drake nodded at him, lips shaping the words: thank you.
For a moment, the chamber was still, the weight of war, of family, of loyalty binding them together. Then Edric cleared his throat, straightening his robes. “Come. The feast will not wait forever.”

Harmon gave a single nod. They moved back toward the Grand Hall, not as individuals, but as men bound to a single course.
As the side doors opened, the hum of conversation faltered. Dozens of eyes turned toward them, nobles weighing gestures, envoys calculating meaning, servants pretending not to stare. Harmon felt the weight of those stares, each one searching for weakness or advantage. He kept his expression even, shoulders square, as though nothing more had passed in that chamber than a polite word of welcome.
King Edric strode ahead with the ease of a man long accustomed to scrutiny. He mounted the dais, raised his goblet once more, and the hall quieted. A warm smile spread across his face, practiced yet genuine enough to settle the room. “Friends,” he began, his voice carrying easily across the hall.
Harmon followed and took his seat at the King’s side, his movements deliberate, calm, as though all that remained was to dine. The weight of their secret lingered like a stone in his chest, but outwardly he was every inch the loyal Sword of the Realm, ready to share bread and meat as if nothing had changed.
The feast had begun.
