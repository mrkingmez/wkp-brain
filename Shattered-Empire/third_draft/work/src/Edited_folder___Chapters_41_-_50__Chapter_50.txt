Snow drifted over the body at Aymir’s knees, catching in her hair, settling in the blood that still glistened dark against the frost. Lysana lay as she had fallen, her bow at her side, one hand still curled as if reaching for an arrow that would never be loosed.

Aymir didn’t move. Didn’t blink. The wind cut at his face, but he barely felt it. His rifle rested across his lap, his fingers loose on the grip, every part of him fixed on the ground before him.

Taerel stood a few paces away, his left arm hanging low at his side, shoulder turned slightly where the ache had settled deep. The air between them was silent but heavy, the kind of silence that only follows the worst kind of noise.

The other survivors moved with quiet purpose. Five soldiers, still on their feet and able to fight, kept a loose perimeter, their eyes fixed on the tree line and the broken ridges beyond. Three more knelt in the churned snow, tending to the wounded with what supplies they had left, bandages torn from cloaks, water from dented flasks. The rest… were gone. Their bodies were being gathered in a single line just inside the kill zone, armor dented, blades still in hand.

No one spoke above a murmur.

The massive gate that barred the pass loomed above, iron-banded timbers scarred by claw and steel. It had been sealed since the attack began, its great chains still. Now, with a groan that rolled through the cliff walls, the lock pins began to drop. Metal slammed against metal, each blow echoing like a hammer on a coffin lid.

Neither brother turned toward the sound. The locks gave with a heavy, reluctant thud, and the massive timbers began to lower.

When the gate thudded into place, a column of soldiers poured out from the fort, armor dulled, helms shadowing their faces. They slowed as they crossed into the killing ground, the smell of it rolling over them. None spoke.

At their head strode Captain Vance, flanked by two of his guards. His gaze moved over the carnage, over the huddled survivors, before finding a familiar face. “Sergeant Rockwen,” he called.

The sergeant straightened from where he’d been crouched beside a wounded man, his helm under one arm, his armor streaked with soot and blood. He stepped forward with a limp and saluted.

“Report,” Vance said quietly.

“Sir, Sergeant Rockwen, Ford City platoon.” His voice carried a gravel edge, the words worn by exhaustion. “Twenty-five in. Eight standing. Forty-five civilians were held in the chapel until these two and their team came through and led us out into the mountains. Without them, we wouldn’t have held." He let his gaze go to the figure at Aymir's feet. "Those are the only two left of their team. The field was chaos. The enemy pressed hard and didn’t stop. We won, sir… but it doesn’t feel like it.” He looked back at the rows of still forms. “Too many won’t be going home.”

Vance said nothing for a moment, his eyes on the still forms beyond Rockwen's shoulder. “You did what you could.”

Rockwen shook his head faintly. “We all did. These men fought well.” He hesitated. “They shouldn’t be left in the snow.”

“Your first real combat?” Vance asked.

The sergeant hesitated, then gave a single, deliberate nod. “Yes, sir.”

“You did well,” Vance said, voice low but firm. “You kept as many alive as you could. That’s more than most can claim. But in war, you can’t keep them all. See to your remainder, water, food, and rest. You’re relieved for now. We have fresh troops moving up from the fort.”

Rockwen saluted again, slower this time, then turned back toward his men.

Vance exhaled, the frost curling in the air. Beyond him, carts began to roll from the fort, drivers hunched against the wind, healers bundled in heavy cloaks. They skirted the bodies without comment, heading for the wounded.

The brothers didn’t move. The captain’s gaze lingered on the shrouded figure between them before he gave a short nod. “We’ll leave her be.”

Aymir straightened, voice low but clear. “Thank you. We’ll take care of her body and belongings when we return from getting our things. It’s time we go.”

More soldiers were filtering out now, fresh-faced and unbloodied, their armor gleaming in the cold light. The tide of movement was steady, purposeful. This fight was in the clean-up stage, and it was no longer theirs to shoulder.

“It seems,” Aymir said, “you’ll have everything in hand soon enough.”

The captain’s mouth thinned. “Is there anything I can do for you?”

“Food for the road,” Aymir said without hesitation. “Nothing fancy, something that’ll keep.” He paused, then lifted a gloved hand toward the west. “There’s something else. About three hours that way, in the shadow of the ridge, we found tunnels that pass under the mountains entirely. They were used. They bypass the pass altogether. That’s where the orcs and goblins came through. You’ll want men there. We saw enough to know it’s not the only time they’ll try it.”

The captain’s eyes sharpened. “We’ll see it secured. And… thank you.”

Aymir gave a short nod, then turned to his brother. Without another word, they left the gate and began the long walk back to the cavern.

The path narrowed quickly, winding between frost-slick rock faces. Their boots crunched on gravel and thin ice. The silence between them wasn’t empty. It was thick with unspoken things.

“I should’ve done more,” Aymir said at last, his voice carrying low in the cold air.

Taerel glanced over. “You did enough to be standing here. That’s more than most can say.”

Aymir's hand tightened on the rifle. "Not enough for her." 

Taerel looked back at the path ahead. “Then it’s my fault, too. I wasn’t at my best. You saw how slow I was. How I couldn’t keep the rifle steady?”

“Don’t.” Aymir’s tone was gentle but firm. “You were there. You fought until you couldn’t.”

Taerel huffed out a breath that might’ve been a laugh, but there was no humor in it. “Then maybe we both failed her.”

The words hung between them until the cavern mouth came into view, a dark slash in the rock.

Inside, the air was still and cold. Their makeshift camp felt hollow now, the echoes of its life already fading. They went about their work with quiet efficiency, rolling blankets, bundling supplies, packing, and checking saddles.

When they reached Luca’s sleeping area, it was empty save for a single bracelet lying in the dust. Taerel crouched, turning it over in his hand. It was his communication bracelet to talk to Weslin. The leather was scorched, the metal warped. Broken. He didn’t need to say it out loud. He slipped it into his pack with the others he’d constructed, the leather strap joining his growing tangle of salvaged gear.

Lysana’s things were next. Each piece was folded or wrapped with care, as if the neatness could grant some measure of respect to the loss. Neither spoke as they worked, tucking it all with care into her pack.

Before they headed for the horses, a healer approached Taerel. “Let me see that arm.”

“It’s fine,” he muttered, but the man was already unwrapping the blood-stained bandages. Taerel went rigid as the healer applied an ointment and fresh linen to his wound, wrapping it tightly. Before he would let him go, he fitted him a sling for that arm.

When Taerel finally got away, he met up with his brother, where they tied up the horses. They checked saddles and saddlebags, taking their time to make sure they were ready to ride out.

Mounting up was clumsy for Taerel with the sling; pain made his balance awkward, but he got there. Luca’s horse still stood tethered nearby, saddled and ready, but empty.

“He must’ve kept walking after he packed up,” Aymir said quietly. “If he’s heading south, maybe we’ll catch him on the road.”

“Maybe.” Taerel didn’t sound convinced.

They rode out, their third horse carrying the bundles of Lysana’s gear. Back at the gate, they returned to her body. Together, they wrapped her in a sheet, then a blanket, tying the layers snug with rope. She was lifted gently onto her horse and secured for the road ahead. Her weapons were packed away with her.

Before they left, they paused. The siege camp outside the fort was being dismantled in grim silence, tents pulled down, barricades toppled, the bodies of orcs, goblins, and joblins heaped and set to the torch. Smoke curled upward, carrying with it the stench of blood and burnt flesh. Soldiers moved through the ruins with practiced detachment, but the hollow in their eyes told the truth.

Neither brother spoke.

Aymir shifted in his saddle, the leather creaking under his weight. The wrapped form of Lysana lay draped across her horse between them, secured with rope and blanket, swaying faintly with each restless shift of its hooves. He glanced at her once, then fixed his eyes on the road ahead.

The weight in his chest wasn’t just grief; it was the words of the dying men still echoing in his head, heavy as stone. That shattered face Corren had described, the sickly-sweet stench of blood and smoke… Aymir doubted those would ever leave him.

They started forward, guiding their mounts past the wreckage of the field. The cold wind funneled through the pass, biting at exposed skin, carrying with it the smell of burned timber from the ruined siege camp they’d passed. Ahead, Sergeant Rockwen and the five soldiers still on their feet worked in silence, hauling the bodies of their fallen from the trampled earth. Frost clung to armor, the bodies stiff with cold.

When the brothers drew near, the sergeant spotted them and gave a firm salute, hand in fist over his heart. The others followed, their movements weary but precise, the gesture heavy with meaning.

Aymir reined in, returning the salute without hesitation. Taerel brought his horse in closer, holding out his good hand. “Names,” he said simply.

One by one, they clasped forearms, the cold biting through the leather of their gloves. Names were exchanged, hard, short syllables that carried the grit of men who had survived when most hadn’t. No speeches, no promises, just the quiet acknowledgment of shared ground and spilled blood.

Taerel gave a single nod, and Aymir added, “We’ll carry your part of this with us.”

They turned their mounts again. “The captain said he would like to see you before you leave,” said Sergeant Rockwen.

“Where is he then?” asked Aymir.

“He took a group of soldiers to that tunnel you told him about, starting to secure it from my understanding,” Rockwen replied.

Aymir drew a slow breath and glanced at his brother. "Guess we're headin' that way then."

“What do you think waits for us out that way?”

“It could be anything, but no sense in making it wait. Let's go.” The two of them headed off towards the captain and whatever was waiting for them.

The ground covered more quickly on the return. Neither of them spoke. The valley gave them that much, at least, the bare ridges and the pale sky and the frost-still air, before the clang of hammers and the bark of orders found them and pulled them the rest of the way back. Soldiers moved in purposeful lines along the ridge, setting stakes, stacking stone, and stringing rope, turning the mouth of the tunnel into something that might hold if the enemy returned.

Aymir and Taerel reined in just short of the work, eyes moving over the activity. The beginnings of a fortified post were taking shape, a narrow palisade already stretching across the slope, dirt piled behind it in a low wall, sentries posted with bows in hand.

Aymir studied it for a long moment. “They’ll hold it. It feels good that they took our warning to heart.”

"They didn't wait," Taerel murmured. There was approval in it.

Aymir gave a short nod. “Good. This needs to be sealed for good.”

They had barely shifted in their saddles when a figure broke from the organized chaos. The captain they’d spoken with earlier, his dark hair damp with sweat, jaw shadowed with stubble, strode toward them. His boots crunched over frost-hardened dirt, the lines of fatigue drawn deep into his face.

Aymir straightened in his saddle. “It’s looking good here. You’ll have this sealed and defended before long.”

“We will,” the captain said, glancing back toward the rising fortifications.

Taerel added, “We’ll pass along what we’ve seen when we make it back. The Crown needs to know about this passage.”

The captain’s gaze held theirs a moment, the weight of shared battle still in it. “See that you do. Every hour counts. But, before you go,” he said, voice low but urgent, “there’s someone who says he must speak with you. Says it can’t wait.” His gaze lingered, weighing them. “He insists it has to be someone from the Crown. I’m guessing that’s you?”

The brothers exchanged a glance. There was hesitation in the shared look, neither truly sure they counted as anything close to official, but Aymir inclined his head. “Yes,” he said after a beat. “That’s us.”

The captain’s hand lifted, signaling to the rear. Two soldiers stepped out from the shadows of the palisade, half-guiding, half-supporting a man between them. His sergeant’s coat hung in tatters, the once-crisp lines scorched and blackened, the crest on his shoulder charred and curling at the edges. A broad bandage wrapped his ribs beneath the coat, dark in places where blood had seeped through, and another strip of linen crossed his neck.

He walked under his own power, but each step was a stiff, measured thing, like he was bracing against a jolt of pain that might unseat him. His skin had the pallor of wax beneath the grime, and his lips were cracked from heat and thirst. Every so often, his breath caught, not quite a cough, but the kind of sharp inhale that betrayed broken ribs.

Even from a few paces away, they caught the copper tang of blood mixed with the bitter reek of scorched cloth and poultices. His eyes, though shadowed by fatigue, burned with stubborn focus, the look of a man who’d already been told to rest and refused. Whatever it had cost him to stand here, he’d decided it was worth it.

As they got closer, the sergeant’s eyes were cloudy with pain. His voice came raw, a whisper edged with desperation. “Messengers of the Crown?”

“We’re here,” Aymir said, standing beside him, taking the place of the soldier who helped him out here.

Taerel stood on the other side. “What’s your name?”

“Sergeant Corren Vey,” the man rasped, breath hitching. “Fort Dreknor garrison… Forgotten Lands side.” His fingers twitched weakly.

Aymir leaned in, the cold air biting at his cheeks. “Go on.”

“I was… Sergeant of the guard,” Corren began, eyes drifting toward the gray sky. “One of the men, quiet, kept to himself, looked me dead in the eye… and his face changed.” His breath shuddered, showing how each word was a challenge. “Not like shifting shadows. It was… like glass shattering. Pieces falling away until there was someone else looking at me. Someone… wrong.”

Taerel's breath slowed. He filed the word "wrong" somewhere cold and didn't take it back out.

Corren’s hand tightened into a trembling fist. “He smiled. I’ll never forget the smell, gods, I’ll smell it in my grave. Sweet, like overripe fruit left to rot in the sun… and underneath it, oil. Lantern oil, thick and sharp. It clung to him. Like he’d bathed in it.” He swallowed hard, eyes unfocused. “Then he was on me. Knife in the gut before I could draw breath.”

Aymir glanced at Taerel. His brother had gone still.

“While I was on the ground… I heard the horns.” Corren’s voice dropped, as if speaking too loudly might summon it all back in full. “Orcs. And a few Red Cloaks with them. Their leader, tall, broad, with scars down his jaw, wore the old Brotherhood coat. The crest was torn, frayed, but it was there. He moved like he knew every step before it happened.”

Something moved through Corren's face. Not fear. Recognition. Like a man who has not yet stopped living it. He breathed once, deliberately, and kept going. “On his left hand, three fingers were gone to the knuckle. He didn't seem to notice. He gave orders like a man reading from memory, his voice low and even, and the orcs listened. That was the part that stayed with me. Orcs listening. They hit us hard and fast… burned the stables, cut down anyone who stood, and left the rest screaming.”

He coughed, a wet sound, and one of the stretcher-bearers shifted uneasily.

“They weren’t here to take the fort,” Corren pressed on. “It was a raid. In and out. They knew exactly where to hurt us. Stores, barracks, command. And the changeling, he didn’t run. He moved with purpose. He went to the other gate to get to its mechanism. Jammed it, broke the gearing… whatever he did, it won’t open without a full rebuild.” His eyes locked on Aymir with sudden intensity. “They’ll be back. And next time… they won’t stop at the gate.”

For a moment, only the wind moved, carrying the faint bite of woodsmoke and the sound of hammers striking home.

“How do you know what he went to do?” asked Aymir.

“Because… He told me. They were going to cripple the Fort and make us pay, make us watch. He said I was to be spared the story, so they knew it was true and what was coming.” Corren took a moment to catch his breath before continuing, “There is one last thing he said before he left, ‘The time of man is over.’” Taerel filed it quietly. Seven words. Clean. The kind of thing that gets said the same way every time because someone decided it was the message. Not the changeling's words. Someone else's words, coming from the changeling's mouth, in a burning fort.

Corren’s head turned as he went into a coughing fit, trying to catch his breath between coughs. "Had to tell someone… before…" He lowered his head. His legs went uncertain beneath him, and one of the soldiers caught his arm. He didn't resist. They walked him away slowly, and he kept his eyes on the ground, like a man who had already spent everything he had.

"Is he going to be alright?" asked Taerel.

"He may never be the same. What he saw will stay with him," answered the soldier to Corren's left. "But he insisted. Said he had to do something." Taerel watched Corren being helped back to the fort, then made a decision. "Get him to Opindale. He needs a real healer, not a field kit." The soldiers hesitated. Taerel met their eyes. "I speak with the authority of Harmon Deim." The name landed. Both men came to attention. "Yes, sir." They moved before he'd finished. Taerel didn't move for a beat. He had borrowed that authority the way you borrowed a coat that wasn't yours, hoping it fit well enough to pass. It had. He filed that away for later.

"I hope I don't get in trouble for that," Taerel said. Aymir said nothing, the weight of Corren's words still settling in his gut like lead. He was already moving toward his horse, his hands finding the reins out of habit more than intention. Taerel mounted a moment later, working around the sling with the careful effort of a man who had learned what hurt and what didn't. Neither of them spoke again.

The captain’s men moved around them in brisk, purposeful lines, shouts carrying over the hammering of stakes and the scrape of shovels biting into frozen ground. Somewhere, an order was barked to double the watch. Taerel met the captain’s eye.
“We’ll pass this on when we link up with the other half of our company,” he said, his voice low but certain. “They’ll want to hear this.”

The captain gave a short nod, his jaw set. “Then ride safe. Words like that can change the shape of a whole campaign, or start one.”

They didn't need to discuss it. Their part here was done, and the burden they carried south would not be lighter for the distance. They turned their mounts south, the road to the port stretching ahead in the pale winter light. Somewhere out there, Luca walked alone, and the brothers carried what you carry when someone walks away instead of falling.

Snow thickened into a slow, relentless fall, softening the world until hills and road and sky blurred into a single color. The horses’ breath smoked in steady pulses. Leather creaked. Rope rasped where it was tied around the blanketed form draped over the third horse. Every few strides, the animal flicked an ear, adjusting to the unfamiliar weight.

They rode a long while without words. For the brothers, silence was unusual, unnatural even, but it fit the shape of the day. Grief had a way of swallowing cleverness.

Between them, the third horse carried its silent burden, Lysana wrapped and secured, the weight of her absence heavier than any gear they’d hauled before. Now and then, Aymir's gaze would drift toward her form. He always looked away before it cost him too much. Taerel kept his eyes forward, but in the corner of his vision, the shape was always there, a reminder that felt carved into him.

Memories pressed in with the silence. Aymir thought of home with the particular ache of someone who knows it is still there. Their father at his bench. Their mother calling them in. The smell of the sweet cakes she just made. The life that had not changed because they had.

Taerel thought of the old jobs, how they’d lift ledgers from lockboxes without a sound, or deliver a sealed parcel to a man who didn’t dare admit he’d paid for it. He remembered the long nights in smoke-hazed gambling halls, Aymir leaning back in his chair, eyes narrow and amused, as another mark overplayed a hand. And the other side of it, the quick bolt over rooftops when a deal soured, the hard laughter when they made it to a safehouse with the goods in hand.

And there were newer memories now, too. Taerel remembered Lysana laughing under her breath the night they sat up, telling stories. How her eyes had glinted when the flames caught them from the fire. Aymir remembered the way she’d stood her ground in the fight, quick and fierce and utterly without fear. The ache in his chest was the price of having known her.

Taerel cleared his throat at last, a small, human sound in the muffled white. “When we reach the port,” he said, voice rough, “then what?”

Aymir didn’t look over. “There’s ‘what,’ and there’s ‘after,’” he answered. “You mean after.”

Taerel nodded once.

Aymir watched the road unspool beneath the horses’ hooves. “Home is west,” he said. “Mother’s garden, Father’s workbench. The smell of oil and hot iron from the foundries. The clockmakers’ street with all those little brass hearts ticking in the windows.” A corner of his mouth twitched. “Ly used to say the West was loud because it was alive. She liked that.”

“She liked the pastry carts by the shipyards,” Taerel said. “Almond braids. She’d pretend she didn’t, and then steal half of mine.” His breath fogged and drifted. “We could go back. See them. See everyone. Let the workshop dust settle on our coats again and pretend we’re the kind of men who stand still.”

Aymir let the thought sit there, warming his chest and cutting it at the same time. “Or we sign on with a captain. There’s coin in keeping a deck honest. The West builds ships faster than it names them. Steel ribs, reinforced keels. I can shoot off a listing gunwale as easily as on a ridge.”

Taerel’s sling creaked as he shifted. “And I can reload one-handed now,” he said dryly. “A useful talent for impressing drunk sailors and getting myself killed in a storm.”

“Still,” Aymir said, and that one word carried storms and coin and far horizons.

They fell quiet again. Snow gathered at the brim of Aymir’s hood until he shook it free with a small, irritated motion.

“Or,” Taerel said after a time, “we lean back into what we were good at before anyone handed us a righteous errand.” His tone didn’t boast; it remembered. “Acquisitions. Doors that needed opening. Papers that needed walking. Rare things changing hands for the right price and the right client. Dice, cards, the long hustle when the purse was fat and the mark was mean.” He exhaled. “We were good, Aym. Better than most.”

Aymir didn’t answer at once.

“Or,” Taerel added, softer still, “we find Harmon again. If there’s even a ‘crew’ to go back to. This was told to us as a one-time thing.” He glanced at his brother. “Maybe it was. Maybe it should be.”

Aymir’s jaw worked as he chose his words. “This,” he gestured at the snow, the road, the blanketed shape between them, “was the worst of it. But it was also… the clearest. Every footstep meant something. I’ve stolen gold because it was gold and lied because lies are tools, and I’ve never lost sleep over either. But this… I knew which way was forward. I knew why.” He paused. “I didn’t like all of who I was in it. But I liked not guessing who I should be.”

Taerel’s eyes stayed on the line where white sky met white road. “Whatever we choose next, home, ship work, or finding another job like this, it could end the same way.” His glance flicked toward the third horse. “Maybe not tomorrow. Maybe not for years. But it’s always waiting somewhere.”
Aymir’s breath plumed in the cold. “It’s the cost, one way or another. You take the work, you take the risk. Doesn’t matter if the pay is coin, or cause, or both.” He nodded toward the wrapped form. “She knew it. We know it. Pretending we don’t is just lying to ourselves.”

Taerel nodded. “So maybe the choice isn’t about safety. Maybe it’s about what’s worth carrying the risk for.”

Aymir’s gaze shifted to the space where a fourth rider should have been. “Some people decide it’s not worth it anymore. They lay it down and keep walking.”

Taerel understood without needing the name. “And sometimes that hurts worse than the fight itself.”

“Sometimes people break, and he broke,” Aymir said simply. “We all did. He just… did it first. And loudest.” The snow made a soft hiss across the plain as the wind shifted. “Maybe he needed to not be near us to keep breathing. I don’t have to like it to understand it.”

Taerel kept his eyes on the road. "You're more generous than I am," he said, and it wasn't a compliment or a criticism. Just an accounting.

They rode through a stand of winter-bare birches, the white trunks like bones. The sea’s cold breath was faint on the wind now and then, a far-off promise that the world still moved even when they felt fixed in place.

“Home means faces we love and a table that’s ours,” Taerel said at last, more to himself than to Aymir. “It also means laying all this down and letting it rust. I don’t know if I can do that. I don’t know if I should.”

“Work on a ship,” Aymir answered, “means never sleeping in the same bed twice and letting the weather decide your days. We could do it. We’d be good at it. It would feel like movement.”

“And going back to acquisitions,” Taerel said, “means we choose the weight we carry and set it down when the coin changes hands. It means using the worst parts of us like tools. Those tools fit our hands. They always have.” He glanced over. “This… whatever Harmon started… asks for the best parts. I didn’t know I had so many to spend until someone asked me to spend them.”

A long silence. The kind brothers could share without it feeling like a wall.

“Maybe the West has room for both,” Aymir said eventually. “For people who fix broken locks and people who stand when the wind tries to blow a city down. Maybe the next thing we do tells us who we are.”

Taerel huffed, a small cloud. “You’re getting philosophical in your old age.”

“I’m only two years older than you,” Aymir said.

“And I’ll remind you of it until you’re dust.” Taerel’s mouth moved, almost a smile, and then didn’t. He looked at the wrapped shape again. “We bring her home first. Whatever ‘home’ can mean, this is far from where she wanted to be.”

Neither of them spoke after that. The honest answer was that they didn't know where home was for her. She had never said, not directly. What she'd offered were glimpses, the name of a city she sometimes pronounced differently when she thought no one noticed, the way her jaw had set when someone mentioned the eastern sea. They'd ask when they reached the port. Someone would know.

They rode until the early light began to thin from gray to slate. The world inhaled cold. Snow beaded along the horses’ manes and stuck to the brothers’ lashes. A raven paced them for a time, beating black wings against the pale sky, then veered off toward the coast.

“Parents will be glad to see us,” Aymir said quietly. “Mother will pretend she isn’t counting the days. Father will try not to ask what we did, then ask anyway and apologize in the same breath.”

“We’ll eat too much,” Taerel said. “Sleep too long. Wake up and realize staying put doesn’t fix anything here.” He tapped his chest with the knuckles of his good hand. “Too much to decide now.”

Aymir nodded. “In the city.”

“In the city,” Taerel echoed.

They let the words settle like a marker driven into frost. No oath. No promise. A place to stand when they reached it.

Snow thickened, falling in slow veils that softened the edges of the world until the road wound like a pale ribbon between the shadows of winter trees. The third horse plodded between them, burden swaying in a rhythm that seemed to beat in time with their thoughts.

They rode in silence, not the kind that feels like absence, but the kind that comes when two minds are busy turning over the same unspoken thing. The talk was done for now. What awaited would soon enough ask its questions.

Taerel shifted in the saddle, digging into the pocket of his coat. When his hand came out, it held a small, dulled coin on a twist of worn twine. He turned it between his fingers, rubbing at the edge with his thumb until a faint gleam caught the low light. “Made this in Father’s shop,” he murmured, half to himself. “Scrap brass from a clock gear. Said it was lucky.”

Aymir glanced over. “It’s still round,” he said dryly. “That’s luck enough for something made in our hands.”

Taerel smiled faintly, holding the coin up so it caught the pale gold of the rising sun. "Almost the same shine. Makes you think the sun's just a bigger coin, and every morning it decides to land the same way. Heads. We get another day."
Ahead, the road bent west, and the light came stronger, amber cutting through the gray, throwing long shadows across the snow. The glow spilled between the bare trees, tracing the frost on every branch. It carried the faint tang of salt from the unseen coast, and with it, the first warmth they’d felt in days.

The woods thickened, and the smell of pine and damp bark stirred memories: their father bent over his bench, the air sharp with sawdust and oil; the two of them hauling cut boards through the shop door while he grumbled about “apprentices with more muscle than sense”; long walks home after deliveries, boots caked with snow, stopping to skip stones over the frozen edge of the river.

For a long while, they didn’t speak. Hooves crunched in the ruts, breath plumed from the horses. Somewhere in the quiet, Taerel found himself turning the coin more slowly. The time of man is over. He didn't say it aloud. No point in giving it more air than it had already taken. But it had hitched a ride south with them, the same as everything else, and he suspected it would still be there when they arrived.

Finally, Aymir’s eyes slid toward his brother. “You know,” he said slowly, “this might not be the moment we decide what’s next. But—”

“We should schedule time to go see Dad,” Taerel said at the same time.

They both stopped, blinked at each other, and then, just for a heartbeat, laughed. Not loud, not long, but enough to warm the cold between them.

“Guess that’s settled, then,” Aymir said.

“Guess so,” Taerel answered, tucking the coin away.

The road ahead glowed in the morning light, the trees thinning to show the sky opening wide. Neither of them knew what the city held. They'd figure that out when they got there. Together.

The horses kept their steady pace toward the horizon, three shadows stretching long across the snow until the bend in the road swallowed them.
