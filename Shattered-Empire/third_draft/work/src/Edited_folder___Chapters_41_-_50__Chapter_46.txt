The loft above the stable was still and quiet. It was dimly lit by slanted sunlight through the warped wooden slats of the shutters. The air was stale with the scent of hay, sweat, and dust. Harmon stood over the war map still spread across the crate in the center of the room, fingers pressed against the parchment as if trying to will the plan into permanence. His jaw clenched. One more run. One more clean withdrawal. He could do that. He had to.

The sound of bootsteps behind him stirred him from thought.

“You’re late,” Harmon said without turning.

“I’m not late,” Weslin replied, breath just a touch quick as he pushed the door closed behind him. “You told me to wait an hour after Reau and Riven got back. I left on the dot.”

Harmon exhaled through his nose and nodded once. “And they’re safe?”

“Yeah. No injuries. Sword’s back. Manor didn’t burn down. As promised.” Weslin tightened a strap on his bag and crossed to the satchel he’d left behind. He knelt, rummaged, then froze. “Forgot my dagger today,” he muttered, drawing a dagger from its sheath at the bottom. “I’m not used to carrying it.”

Harmon turned, brow furrowing as he looked the boy over. “Weslin…”

He let the name hang there for a moment, then softened his voice, not angry, just firm. “You can’t keep rushing in with your head down. I know you want to prove yourself, but every mission, every life we try to save, depends on us thinking two steps ahead. Not one. Two.”

He stepped closer, the steel in his voice gentled by experience. "I know there's a clock running. So does every man in that tower. That's exactly why we cannot afford to add our own carelessness to the pile. Next time, check your kit before you leave. That's not training anymore. That's survival. Forgetting your dagger might not seem like much until the moment you need it. That could mean life or death, and not just for you.”

Weslin’s eyes dropped, ashamed, reflective.

But Harmon rested a hand on his shoulder. “This isn’t blame. It’s training. You’re still learning, and you’re learning fast. But we have to work on this. Because next time, there might not be a chance to come back for what you forgot. There's no shame in stopping to think before you move. Take it.”

Weslin gave a small nod, strapping the dagger at his side.

The two moved quickly through the loft, gathering the remaining packs and saddlebags, everything that hadn’t been taken during the mission prep. The space was nearly clear now. Below them in the stable, the remaining horses shifted softly, one letting out a snort and a low stamp of hooves. Somewhere outside, a wheel scraped stone and then stopped. Harmon moved to the stairwell door and unlatched it.

It slammed open with a thunderous crack, and a boot struck Harmon square in the chest, sending him sprawling back across the floorboards. He landed hard with a grunt, air driven from his lungs.

Weslin's eyes snapped to the doorway. “Torn?”

Framed in the light, armored in a mail shirt and dark leather trousers, stood the man they’d rescued from the prison. Just that familiar face, twisted now by something meaner. A slow, malevolent grin peeled across it.

Torn stepped forward and kicked Harmon in the ribs, hard.

“I’ve waited a long time for this. I have been following you since I saw you at your camp, thinking of all the things I would do to you. First time I have smiled in years.”

Weslin took a step forward, voice rising. “Torn, what are you doing? We rescued you!”

Torn didn’t even look at him.

He dropped down, driving a mailed fist into Harmon’s face with the pommel of his sword. Blood welled at the edge of Harmon’s mouth.

“I used to look up to you,” Torn hissed. “Back in the Academy. Back when I was your student. Now I get to look down on you.”

Harmon pushed himself up to his elbows, blinking blood from his eye.

Torn stepped back, sabers raised. “Say my name.”

Harmon’s breath rasped as he wiped his chin. “Teralon Odris. Shieldbearer to the Eighth Blade. Commander-in-training of the Skyguard.”

A flash of teeth. “So you do remember.”

His voice dropped, venomous. “I was Shieldbearer to the Eighth Blade. A rising commander of the Skyguard. All gone now. Burned away by your blind obsession with victory and your loyalty to the crown, no matter the cost.”

Torn’s eyes narrowed. “You remember the screams? The betrayal? Or do you block that out, too?”

Harmon grunted. “What betrayal? We were on a mission.”

“That’s all you ever cared about, the mission,” Torn snarled. “Not the men. Not the ones who died because of you. You climbed the ranks on the backs of their ashes. I didn’t forget. I hear them every night.”

He raised the sword again. “They tell me what to do. How to make you pay.” The word was still forming when Weslin lunged. Torn spun and backhanded him with brutal precision, sending the boy sprawling against a stack of gear.

“You were never the man you were supposed to be,” Harmon muttered, rising slowly. “There was always something in you. I saw it even then. I should’ve trusted my gut.”

As he was saying this, Torn, with incredible speed, moved to where Weslin was getting up and landed a solid kick to Weslin’s midsection, to a loud thud and a crack echoed throughout the loft. Weslin fell to his side, doubled over in pain. As fast as he was there, he was gone back to confront Harmon. 

He didn’t spare a glance back. Instead, he stalked toward Harmon, sabers dragging faint grooves in the wood.

“Look at you now,” he sneered. “Still wearing that cursed ring. Still pretending you stand for something.”

Harmon rose, breath low and steady, his jaw set beneath the shadows of his hood. The glow of the Ring pulsed faintly, no burst, no fanfare, just a low, harmonic hum like the promise of thunder.

Shadow-forged steel formed in his hand, edge first, the sword trailing wisps of black heat as it solidified. Enchanted armor rippled across his frame, piece by piece, locking into place with quiet finality.

The man he’d been, the commander, the knight, the protector, stepped forward in silence.

Teralon’s grin widened. A thin, wolfish thing stretched tight across blood-stained teeth. “Finally.” He raised both blades and dropped into a stance that spoke of years of repetition, of perfect form. The same stance Harmon had drilled into him back at the academy. Only now, it carried intent twisted by bitterness.

Harmon said nothing as he prepared. He knew what was coming.

Torn’s nostrils flared. "I used to imagine what I'd say when I got here." He looked at Harmon for a moment, something moving across his face that was not triumph. "I can't remember any of it now." Torn moved to attack. 

Harmon met Torn’s charge with a sweeping arc of his greatsword, testing for overreach, for that old mistake Teralon never corrected. Torn twisted beneath it, a blur of chain and leather, and his sabers flared in reply, crossing once to deflect the blow, then separating to strike. One carved across Harmon’s vambrace, the other clanged off his cuirass, leaving a spray of sparks and a gouge of metal.
Harmon grunted, footing staggered. “Still leads with the left. Still overcommits on the backstep.” He adjusted, pivoted hard off his rear leg, slashing his blade down like a hammer. The impact split an overhead beam behind Torn, dust and splinters raining down.
Torn laughed. "You fight the same way you commanded. Like if you hit hard enough, it stops being your fault." 
Their swords locked, grinding metal on metal, close enough to smell each other’s sweat. Harmon breathed through his nose once. “You call this justice?”
“No,” Torn sneered. “I call it repayment.”
He drove his knee into Harmon’s thigh, then disengaged with a flick of both sabers, slicing the strap of a pauldron. The armor piece hit the ground with a dull thud.
Across the room, Weslin stirred, groaning. One hand braced on a beam, the other gingerly at his ribs. Blood ran from his lip. He blinked through the haze, watching, helpless, for now.
Torn pressed forward, sabers dancing. Harmon caught the first with his blade, deflected the second with a gauntleted forearm, but the third stroke cut deep across his side, and Torn followed with a spinning backfist that sent Harmon stumbling into a stack of gear. Leather and cloth burst into the air.
“You remember what you said?” Torn spat. “Orders first. Feelings later. Sacrifices are just the cost of command.”
Harmon surged upright, parried two strikes in quick succession, then shoved Torn back with the flat of his blade. “I said that… to keep boys like you alive!”
Torn didn’t hesitate. He leapt back in, sabers spinning in tight arcs, then feinted low and came high. Harmon turned, caught part of the blow, but the rest tore his helm free. It clanged against the stone wall and rolled away.
Weslin’s breath caught.
Harmon’s voice came low, sharp as broken glass. “You want truth, Teralon? Fine. The men you mourn, those righteous brothers-in-arms? They died because they ignored direct orders. They disobeyed the fallback command and held the line long after we pulled out.”
Teralon’s face twisted in disbelief.
“We had to move on or we all would’ve died,” Harmon said. “I didn’t choose to leave them. They chose to stay. They made themselves martyrs. And it was their pride that buried them.”
“You’re lying!”
“I didn’t even know what they’d done until after you vanished. I read the full report weeks later, after you ran. After you abandoned the debrief. You were too much of a coward to face the truth of what happened.”
Teralon faltered for just a heartbeat. Harmon pressed. “You want to know what else I learned? After they stayed behind, they torched a village. Claimed it was a rebel safehouse. But there were no weapons, no threats. Just families. Children.”
Teralon’s hand trembled.
“They took everything I taught them and twisted it. And you, who were supposed to lead them, you vanished, before the truth ever reached you.”
“Shut up,” Torn whispered, voice cracking. “Shut your mouth.” With a scream of rage, Teralon charged, sabers flaring, footwork reckless. But Harmon was ready.
He caught the first strike, rolled his shoulder to deflect the second, then smashed the flat of his blade into Teralon’s side. A sharp elbow to the jaw followed, then a rising cut across the ribs that split chain and leather.
Teralon staggered, stunned. But not for long. “You don’t get to rewrite their memory!” Torn howled, surging forward, sabers spinning like teeth. “I know what I know. I lived it. You think I’d let you poison it now? You’re just trying to trick me. Twist it. Disarm me with guilt.”
He drove Harmon back with each word, every strike a punctuation. He took a breath. “No. I know what I know. I was there." 
Harmon barely kept his footing. One blade scraped his shoulder, another clipped his thigh. Blood ran freely.
“You ruined everything!” Torn screamed, eyes wild. “You took my loyalty, our purpose, and turned it to ash. You ruined the Skyguard. You ruined me.”
And then his voice dropped, low and venomous.
“You want to know how deep this runs? It was me, Harmon. From the beginning.”

“I was the one who warned the nobles about Dain’s true name. I was the one who left the note that drew the others away, so you’d be alone, and they are heading into a trap.”
Something shifted behind his eyes. He kept his feet. 
“Every setback since the prison? That was me. The missteps, the near-misses, you weren’t cursed, Harmon. You were hunted.”
He struck. Harmon couldn’t stop it. A saber cracked against his ribs, armor buckling. The second swept his legs. He fell hard, his sword skidding away. The ring-forged armor shimmered, fractured, and vanished in a whisper of smoke. Only his travel-worn plate remained, dented, split, bloodstained.
Torn stood over him.
Harmon’s face was bloodied. A cut across the brow, sweat in his eyes. 
“Look at you,” Torn hissed. “The mighty knight. The ringbearer. Reduced to groveling.”
Harmon rose again, slow but steady, letting the ring hum at his side, its pulse barely perceptible, power contained. “You wanted a monster. I tried not to be one.”
“You failed,” Torn growled. “You failed me. You failed them,” Motioning to the place where he last saw Weslin, “You failed all of us.”
Torn went for another blow, and Harmon summoned his blade again.
Their swords met again. This time, Harmon drove forward with force, his back foot skidding across the loft as he shoved Torn across the space. Dust billowed beneath them. Torn reversed, dropping low to kick Harmon’s knee and slash at his exposed flank. His side told him he'd taken a cut. He noted it and kept his feet. 
Weslin, unnoticed, had drawn his dagger. He kept to the shadows, each motion slow, purposeful, his breathing ragged.
Torn pivoted, sabers whirling. “You knew we weren’t ready for that raid. You knew we didn’t have the support. But the mission mattered more than the men, right?”
“It wasn’t that simple. Your hate has twisted what happened back then,” Harmon muttered.
Torn struck, one blade high, the other low. Harmon blocked one, took the second on his side with a grimace. Torn was not even listening to any of it. He pressed forward. “You ordered the push anyway,” Torn shouted, pressing the attack. “Six dead! My twin among them!”
Harmon staggered, barely keeping upright.
“I carried his body back myself,” Torn snarled, voice raw. “I buried him alone.”
“I…” Harmon faltered, then found his voice again, low and firm. “I lived with those choices. Every damned day.”
The sabers dropped an inch. Just an inch. And for a beat, Torn was not a man with weapons and a plan. He was a man on his knees in the dirt with his hands full of his brother and no one else around, and that look had lived in him for years without a single person to aim it at until this moment. "Not enough," he said. Not a shout. A verdict on everything that had come before it. Then he charged. Because grief has nowhere to go, and anger at least has a direction. 
The charge had no finesse. Just weight and velocity and years of accumulated damage aimed at one man. Harmon tucked and caught it on the blade, the impact driving him to one knee. 
Torn circled once, eyes flicking over Harmon’s battered form, then added, ”You forget, old man. I was by your side longer than most of them. I know your stance, your timing, every counter, every feint. You made me... and now I’ll unmake you.”
He disarmed Harmon again with a twin strike, blades shearing against the greatsword and flinging it across the loft in a crash. Harmon fell back, dazed, armor cracked, blood running freely now.
Torn loomed, sabers crossed at Harmon’s neck. “Now you kneel,” he breathed. “Good. Stay that way.”
Across the room, Weslin stood. Silent. Focused. A shimmer of light rippled across the floor. Torn didn’t see it. His gaze narrowed to Harmon alone. Torn raised his blade for the final stroke.
Then paused. Head turning.
A creak. Torn looked to where he had left Weslin, but he was not there. He looked around, but Weslin was nowhere to be seen. “And you called me a coward, as your squire ran away. Smarter than I gave him credit for. I’m looking forward to killing him next.”
Torn looked back at Harmon, but what he saw was Harmon still on his knees, head bowed in a silent prayer.
"Pray all you want," Torn sneered, lowering his sword. "Your gods won't save you."
He swung. The blade sliced through smoke. An illusion. Torn stumbled forward, eyes wide, suddenly exposed, and behind him, Weslin moved. One step. Two.
The dagger was in his hand. His whole body shook. But his grip was firm. Two steps ahead, Weslin thought. With determination driving him, he plunged the dagger forward. Steel punched through flesh, easily going from the back of the neck through the front, ripping open his throat.
The illusion collapsed the moment the dagger found its mark. Weslin barely noticed. There was a ringing behind his eyes from holding it, and something worse in front of them from letting go. 
Torn stiffened. His swords fell. He saw Harmon lying flat on the ground behind where the illusion was. Blood ran down the blade. Harmon rose quickly. Torn’s eyes widened. Blood burst from his mouth. His legs buckled. He collapsed forward into Harmon’s arms, dying on the man he hated most.
Silence followed. Thick. Absolute.
Weslin stood frozen, dagger still in his grip, the blade still in Torn's neck. The boy's chest rose and fell too fast, too shallow, eyes wide, face drained of all color. He stared not at Torn’s body, but through it. Through the blood on his knuckles. Through the ringing in his ears.
Harmon reached up slowly from where he knelt and curled his fingers around the hilt of the dagger. “It’s done,” he said gently.
Weslin didn’t move.
“Wes.”
At last, Weslin blinked. His grip loosened, and Harmon guided his hand away. The dagger came free with a sickening wet sound. Harmon set it aside, then eased Torn’s body down and let it rest. Once a student. Now a body on a loft floor. Harmon had made peace with worse things, but this one would sit differently. 
Harmon turned. Weslin hadn't moved. He was standing in the same spot, both hands loose at his sides now, but his face had gone somewhere the room couldn't reach.
“I didn’t…” Weslin’s voice cracked. “I didn’t know it would feel like that.”
“There’s no glory in it,” Harmon said. “And there’s no taking it back.”
Weslin’s jaw tightened. He shook his head, just slightly. “I thought I was ready. I thought… with the training, with everything… I thought I understood.”
“No one does. Not until the first one.” Harmon stood, slowly, painfully. Blood trickled down his side. “But you did what had to be done.”
“I didn’t even think,” Weslin whispered. “I just saw you kneeling, and I…”
“You acted. You saved my life.” Harmon rested a hand on the boy’s shoulder, firm but not heavy. “There's no clean version of this. None of us got one.” He kept his hand on Weslin's shoulder. "The weight you're feeling right now is what separates you from what we just put in the ground. Don't try to get rid of it. It's going to tell you things. Listen to it.” 
The silence that followed was thick with breath and blood and things neither of them could say. Then, quietly, Weslin dropped to one knee.
He looked at his hands first. The blood on his knuckles had dried to rust at the edges, and he stared at it until the room settled. Then he looked up at Harmon, and the words came. Not from anything he had planned. Not from training, not from anything his father had taught him, not from a book or a ceremony he had witnessed. From somewhere older than any of that, somewhere he could not point to. He heard himself beginning and kept going because stopping felt like the wrong thing to do, like breaking something that was being passed through him rather than something he was saying himself. 
Weslin’s voice was steady. “You are my knight. And I am your squire. I will carry your orders, guard your back, and rise when you fall. I serve not just you, but the mission and the team we are. I take this oath freely, without reservation, without regret. And if you’ll have me… then from this day forward, we face what comes together.”
He stopped speaking, surprised by the silence. He had heard himself saying the words and known each one was true even as he was surprised by them. He looked at his hands again. They were still shaking. But not from the same thing as before. 
Harmon’s jaw worked. He reached out, hand cupping the back of Weslin’s neck, and bowed his forehead to the boy’s. He summoned his sword and touched both shoulders of Weslin, "I accept." He held the boy for a moment, one hand at the back of his neck. When he stepped back, something in his face was different. "Those words have been waiting a long time for someone who meant them. You did." 
The loft, thick with the weight of what had transpired, seemed to exhale. Harmon blinked, then turned toward the open door. Down below, hooves pounded stone. Cart wheels clattered into the alley. Then voices, familiar, urgent. “Move!” Reau’s voice rang like a bell. “Now!”
Harmon moved first, his breathing shallow but steady. Torn’s body lay where it had fallen, blood staining the wooden floorboards beneath. No parting words. No rites. Just the echo of unfinished vengeance fading in the corners of the loft.
He and Weslin shouldered the last of their gear, bedrolls, satchels, and half-spent supplies. They descended the narrow stairs with haste, Weslin favoring one side, a hand pressed lightly to his ribs. 
The dagger was back in its sheath. His hand kept finding it the way a tongue finds a loose tooth, not to do anything, just to confirm it was there, and that the weight of it was now different from what it had been an hour ago. 
The world outside the loft had no mark on it. No record of what had happened above it. The slush, the shadows, the distant sound of a cart wheel, all of it exactly as they had left it. Weslin felt the gap between that and what his hands still remembered like something physical, like a second weight he was now carrying that had no shape yet. 
The air had a weight to it that the loft hadn't. Not weather. Something the city had been gathering while they were occupied elsewhere. The buildings hunched closer, shadows running deeper. Eyes followed them from windows and balconies, faces slack but eyes too bright, watching with a precision that had nothing to do with curiosity. The city was awake. And it knew them.
There were no children. Harmon had not noticed until now. Every occupied city he had ever moved through had children in the streets, regardless, they found ways in and out of anything. This one had none. Whatever the occupation had done to the residents, it had done it thoroughly enough that the children were gone from the street completely, and he did not want to think too carefully about where. 
Riven sat perched on the front of the cart, twin blades at his back and fury in his eyes. Reau was already off the bench, surveying Harmon’s battered armor and the state of his face.
“What the hell happened in there?” Reau asked, jerking a thumb toward the loft. He didn’t wait for the full answer. “Never mind. We’ve got a problem.”
Harmon reached into his coat, pulled the folded map from its oilskin pouch, and handed it to Weslin. “Take the rest of the horses to this point here, see it?” He tapped once. “Use the northern alleys. Stick to the shadow line and avoid the plazas.”
Weslin nodded, face still pale but determined. He tossed what he was carrying into the back of the nearest cart, then sprinted for the stable’s main door. 
Harmon watched him go, then hauled himself into the second cart with a wince, settling against the sideboards just as Reau climbed up front and took the reins. A moment later, Riven cracked the whip on the lead cart, and they rolled out.

The streets narrowed quickly, pressing shadows in on either side as the wheels rattled over cobbles slick with melt. Harmon stayed quiet for a while, letting the rhythm of motion dull the edge of his wounds.

Finally, he spoke. “Anything we need to worry about from that manor?”

Reau didn’t glance back. “There was a ledger in the study. Looked like it hadn’t been touched in months. We flipped through, marked deliveries, coordinates, artifact references.”

Riven called over his shoulder, “And one name. One we’ve never heard before.”

Harmon leaned forward slightly. “What name?”
Reau reached into his coat and pulled out a folded slip of parchment, offering it back without turning. “Written on its own page, different ink. Just one word.”
Harmon took it. A single name, scrawled in looping script, ‘Irothis’. The moment he read it, something pulled at his gut. A hollowness. Like breath drawn too deep in a cold room. The word landed the way old wounds do when the weather turns. Familiar. Wrong. Already making plans for how long it intended to stay. 
Riven’s voice was quieter now. “When I said it aloud, it felt like something… shifted. Like the walls leaned in.”
Reau nodded. “Didn’t feel right. Didn’t feel normal. Almost like someone was watching.”
Harmon folded the note again, slower this time. He tucked it into his belt pouch. The bolt of violet came from the tower's crown while they were still moving. Riven pulled on the reins. Reau's head came around. Harmon was already watching the tower. He did not flinch. He looked like a man confirming something he had not wanted confirmed, and when both of them looked at him, he said nothing, and that said enough. 
They rode in silence for another minute, wheels bumping over uneven stone. Somewhere behind them, a bell tolled from a distant tower, three slow chimes swallowed by the chill. Riven broke the quiet. “Weslin was covered in blood. He okay?”
Harmon didn’t look up. “He’ll live.”
Reau glanced over his shoulder. “That wasn’t just a scrap, was it?”
Harmon sat back, breaking his eye contact with the tower. “It wasn’t his, it was Torn’s.”
That landed hard. Riven exhaled slowly. “Last time I saw him was when he took that horse and rode off from the Forgehallow camp. Didn’t say a word. Just disappeared.”
Reau nodded. “He didn’t trust anyone. Anyone, he always stood a little apart. Quiet. Guarded. But he pulled his weight.”
“Yeah,” Riven said. “You knew he had demons, but he worked. Never saw this coming.”
Harmon’s voice dropped. “You didn’t see it because I wasn’t around yet.”
Neither of them responded at first. Then Riven asked, quieter now, “Is this gonna come back on us?”
Harmon didn’t look up. “No. He’s not anyone’s problem anymore.” No other words had to be said.
At the end of the street, Garr stood waiting beneath the jut of an overhang, one hand resting on the hilt of his blade. Neavin crouched nearby atop a flat section of rubble, falcon-eyes distant, tracking something overhead. Both were still as statues, save for Garr’s fingers drumming a slow, deliberate rhythm on his belt.
As the carts creaked to a halt, Garr stepped forward.
He raised a brow. “Didn’t expect backup.”
Harmon gave a grim nod, eyes scanning rooftops and windows. “Plans shifted.” He glanced toward the tower, where the tower cut the sky above the roofline. “No contact since they entered?”
Garr shook his head. “Nothing. They made entry a few hours ago, got in clean. We’ve seen no movement, no signals. Not a sound since. And the longer it's quiet, the louder it feels." Garr's eyes went to the tower and did not come back. Both Riven and Reau nodded. 
Harmon folded his arms. “Where’s Aurelia?”
Garr's fingers stopped drumming on his belt. He took a breath before he spoke.
"A few hours ago, she tried a mental sweep. Light probe, nothing she hadn't done a dozen times. Something pushed back, and she went down screaming. Then she stood up." He paused. "Wrong. She stood up wrong. Back too straight, chin up, hands flat at her sides like she'd been arranged. Then she opened her mouth, and it wasn't her."
He said the last part plainly, the way a man says something he has been trying to find a better explanation for and given up on.
"Cold. Not angry. Not afraid. The way something speaks when it doesn't have to feel anything about you to deal with you. Said to leave. Said there was nothing here worth what we were risking. Said it, looking directly at me, like it was waiting to see if I'd comply." He shook his head. "When I didn't move, it turned her head and looked at Neavin."
The silence that followed that was brief and not comfortable.
"She tore free a few seconds later. Clutched her skull and ran. Neavin tracked her to the pond off Eastveil Lane."
Harmon looked at Neavin.
Neavin hadn't moved from his piece of rubble. His eyes came back slowly, the way a man pulls his attention from somewhere both distant and close at the same time.
"Still there," he said. "Feet in the water up to the ankle. Been rockin' since I found her, same rhythm, ain't changed once. Lips movin' but I can't make out what." He paused. "Eyes are open. She's lookin' at somethin' in the water. Ain't sure it's the water she's seein'."
Something shifted in Harmon's face.
Not an alarm. Not the focused quiet of a commander triaging a problem. Something older than either of those things, something that moved through his expression the way a current moves beneath ice, there and then controlled, but Garr had spent enough time around Harmon to know what the normal register looked like, and for three full seconds it had not looked like that.
"What exactly did it say?" Harmon asked. The word "exactly" came out weighted in a way that had nothing to do with tactics.
Garr repeated it word for word.
Harmon was still for the duration of it.
"Did she fight it," he said, "or did it release her?"
"She tore free," Garr said again, slower this time, because the question itself had not been casual. "Neavin thinks she pulled herself out."
"Good." He said it the way a man says it when he is identifying the category of problem in front of him, not celebrating anything. His hand had found the cane pommel. He made himself let go.
The specific quality of what Garr described was not new to him. The voice was cold because it had no reason to be otherwise. The patience behind the words. The way it turned her head to check whether Neavin would comply. These were signatures. He had encountered them before, in a different city, under a different name, in a war whose records no longer existed anywhere except in the memory of the people who had survived it.
He had thought, in the years since, about what he would do if he came across it again.
"We retrieve her on the way out," he said. "We leave no one." He let that sit for a moment. "And if it is still using her when we find her, if she has not come back yet, you let me speak to it first."
Garr looked at him. "What?"
"If it is wearing her when we arrive. You let me speak to it before anyone else does." He met Garr's eyes and held them. "I will know the difference. And so will it."
Neavin said nothing. His head turned back toward whatever he had been watching in the middle distance, the place where Aurelia was sitting in the cold with her feet in a pond and her lips moving and her eyes on something below the surface that was not the water.
He turned to the carts and pointed to a new position deeper in the alley, away from the tower’s sightlines. “Shift the carts here. We’ll stage for recovery. If they’re being chased, this is where we break contact.”
Reau and Riven exchanged a glance as they paced in the slush-rutted alley beside the cart. Riven’s fingers were twitching against the hilt of his dagger. Reau leaned on the front rail, arms crossed, scanning the tower’s upper windows as if daring someone to show their face. They had the look of men who had been told to stay and were burning through the word "stay" one second at a time. “We need to help them out,” Reau said. 
Riven nodded. “A few well-placed blades, maybe a distraction or two. We’ll clear a path.”
Harmon, seated in the cart bed, shook his head. “If you go in without a way to communicate, then we will have two teams flying blind. If they come out and we don’t know where you are, then we are still in the same position. What if they have people on their tails? No, it's not good tactics for this.”
Reau frowned. “So we just wait?”
“No,” Harmon said. “If you want something to do, start scouting exit paths. Low-profile. Look for any signs they might be carving a new way out. If you hear anything or see anything that looks like an escape in progress, move to help. If they’ve got civilians with them, your priority is getting them out safe.”
Riven gave a sharp nod. “Stay low, move quietly. Eyes out, ears sharp.”
“Exactly,” Harmon said. “Be smoke until you need to be steel.”
Reau cracked a grin. “Now that sounds like the Harmon I remember.”
The tension eased, but only slightly. Reau vaulted down from the cart, Riven following. The two of them slid into the deeper shadow of the adjoining street, footsteps vanishing into the city’s noise.
Garr snorted faintly. "Better to aim their fire at something useful than let it burn a hole in their nerves.”
Harmon stayed seated, watching the tower. He didn't move. He was still enough that Garr stopped watching him after a while, which was the point. 
“Whatever came next, we’ll have to be ready for it.” His gaze stayed fixed on the skyline beyond, where the tower loomed like a half-buried spear in the ribs of the city. 
Wind tugged at the edges of his cloak. Somewhere below, a shutter creaked. The tower was dark except where it wasn't. That bothered him more than the noise would have. 
“Let’s be ready,” Harmon said, voice low, iron beneath it. “Whatever breaks loose next, we meet it standing.”
Then he turned to the carts. A sudden flash brightened the sky, then another.
Everyone looked up, from the crown of the tower, violet lightning, wrong in its color and wronger in its silence, lashing twice from the tower's crown and leaving nothing behind but the smell of scorched stone. 
Garr and Harmon asked in unison, “What the abyss was that?”
