Dain offered a half-bow. “Lady Liora, shall we inspect the bloodlines you so graciously outlined?”

“Delighted,” Nyssa said, rising.

The guards moved.

A flick of her wrist. A blade flew, striking a guard in the thigh. He collapsed with a bark of pain.

Dain grabbed a silver tray and flung it into another’s path, knocking him off balance.

“Move!” he snapped.

“Run, little lord. Run while you still remember your own name.” They ran, not knowing how to get away.

“Just think, by next week, you’ll be serving our tea,” one nobleman jeered, laughter curling sharp as wire.

Through the roses, past the fountain. Another guard gave chase. They dodged his attempt to grab them and turned around to go the other way. “They always run. It just makes the breaking sweeter,” said Baron Rusk.

Nyssa hurled a sugar dish. The glass exploded. Dain vaulted a low bench and pulled Nyssa over a stone wall.

They ran past two more guards as they made their way through the garden and back to the main road.

“They’re still coming,” Nyssa gasped, glancing back. “They’re not slowing.”

They kept dashing, rounded a corner, and they were yanked into the shadows.

A strong hand covered Nyssa’s mouth. Another pulled Dain down.

Garr. Harmon. Both already braced as if they’d been waiting.

Harmon raised a finger to his lips.

The guards passed.

Four hearts pounded.

And from the garden, laughter echoed, thin and distant.

The sun dipped behind Stormhaven's upper spires, painting the old quarter in copper light as Neavin, Aurelia, and Aldrek passed beneath the crumbling arch of what once might have been a prayer hall. The building was quiet now, but not abandoned. Someone had tried to tidy the debris, and faint incense still clung to the air.

Neavin crouched near the threshold. “The floorboards have been swept. Recently.”

Aurelia nodded, fingers brushing the edge of a wall tapestry. The fabric was freshly hung, depicting a radiant figure wrapped in crimson light. Too clean. Too new. “Someone's trying to resurrect this place. But it doesn’t follow any of the registered orders.”

Brother Aldrek frowned, gripping the hilt of his mace. “The imagery is all wrong. The scripture teaches light by sacrifice, not domination. This... this is distorted.”

They moved deeper. Makeshift pews, irregular and ill-formed, were set in uneven rows before a raised stone dais. Carvings lined the back wall, etched in unfamiliar patterns.

Neavin tilted his head. “That doesn’t match any formal script or liturgy I’ve ever seen. And these lines here, they curve too sharply.”

“It’s not a language,” Aurelia murmured. “It’s mimicry.”

A sudden scrape echoed above. They froze. A boy, thin and pale, descended the narrow stairs from the bell tower. His eyes were wide, unblinking.

“You shouldn’t be here,” he said flatly.

“Neither should you,” Aldrek countered.

The boy didn’t flinch. “They said you’d come, that you’d want to see. It’s almost ready.”

Neavin exchanged a glance with Aurelia. “What is?”

The boy pointed to the altar. “The Offering. He feeds on the willing. They say he makes the city clean.”

Before more could be asked, the boy turned and fled.

Aurelia stepped to the altar. Carved behind it, almost hidden beneath an overlaid drape, was a symbol, not of any known god or divine branch. It looked like a sun, twisted and spiraled inward, burning from the center out.

Neavin rubbed his thumb across the carving. “Looks like something old trying to be new again.”

“Or something false wearing the skin of the familiar,” Aldrek said.

They searched the rest of the space, finding half-burned ledgers, papers referencing a list of converts and purification dates. All pointed to a ceremony scheduled under the Harvest Moon. A name was signed beneath it: "High Minister Varin of the New Flame."

Aldrek scoffed. “That title doesn’t exist. It’s a fabrication.”

“Still,” Aurelia said, rolling the parchment, “it’s enough to mislead hundreds. Maybe thousands.”

They gathered the evidence and slipped out into the alley before the dusk bells. None of them noticed the boy watching them from the tower’s shadow.

They should’ve stopped after the false registry. After the woman in the glass shop recited the same too-perfect script, and the child in the alley said he “didn’t remember” his own parents’ names. The whole district stank of something scripted. But Aldrek insisted they press further, and Neavin found a path tucked behind a smithy’s ruined yard, an access shaft hidden by collapsed slagstone and old cart frames.

Aurelia hesitated at the opening. “This isn’t on the city plans.”

“Exactly,” Neavin said.

So they went lower.

The tunnel narrowed, then bent in a tight spiral that led them down past rusted ladder rungs and old binding stones crumbling with disuse. The air grew thick with dust and heat. Far above, the forge district’s silence lingered like a forgotten echo.

Their boots scraped metal once, then stone again.

They didn’t speak. Not until they reached the place where the air changed, the walls opening like lungs into something far older than any of them expected.

What they found wasn’t a tunnel.

It was a cradle.

The chamber was massive, vaulted stone with ancient supports carved in the old tongue. Stale magic hung in the air like dried blood, too thick to breathe properly. At its center was a platform of obsidian and fused bone. Upon it rested a figure, twenty feet tall, humanoid in shape, but forged of armor too black to reflect light and too intricate to be made by human hands.

Runes burned faintly across its shoulders, like the last embers of a long-dead forge.

Neavin stopped in his tracks. “That’s not a golem.”

“No,” Aurelia whispered. “That’s something older.”

Aldrek made the sign of the Circle. “I’ve read about these in the sealed annals. War-beasts. They are from the Second Magic War. Created using methods we’ve lost, alchemical forging, blood-script binding, soul-fixation. Most were dismantled. Some... vanished. One was said to have razed three keeps in the Eastmere Rebellion.”

The thing on the platform shifted, only slightly. Just enough to show it wasn’t dead.

Neavin took a step back. “That wasn’t sleep. That was waiting.”

Aurelia’s voice turned cold. “They’re waking it.”

Aldrek’s gaze swept the glyphwork circling the room, blood channels, resonance coils, and a broken altar at the far end. “This isn’t just a tomb. It’s a fuse chamber. They’re charging it.”

Neavin turned to the others, face grim. “We have to tell Harmon. This thing, with the Red Cloaks’ Adepts, it’s an army without match.”

None of them moved for a moment. The air in the chamber pressed down like a held breath. The thing on the platform didn't shift again. But none of them looked away from it. He looked back at the construct.

“With this... they could hold the walls against a kingdom. This isn't a deterrent. It’s a declaration.”

They lingered in the shadowed alcove for a long minute after the guards passed, four dark shapes swallowed by the fog-draped alleys. Their boot-steps echoed too rhythmically. Too perfectly.

Garr’s hand dropped from the hilt of the club he’d fashioned from a busted lantern post. He exhaled through his nose, tension bleeding off his shoulders like sweat.

“Not just any guards,” he muttered.

Harmon gave a slight nod, his gaze following the vanishing silhouettes. “No,” he said quietly. “No hesitation. No conversation. Just executioners in uniform.”

Beside them, Nyssa was steadying her breathing. Dain hadn’t said a word since they ducked into the alcove, but the look in his eyes was carved granite, controlled fury. He was still gripping the hilt of his blade-knife with white knuckles, blood from a shallow graze on his arm wetting the cuff of his stolen noble’s sleeve.

“We need to move,” Harmon said at last. “Sun’s dying. The longer we wait, the more eyes will start to see what they shouldn’t.”

Garr, Harmon, Dain, and Nyssa stepped into the alley, careful not to let their boots splash in the puddles of ashwater collecting at the corners. The light was copper-tinged now—dusk rolling in like a tide, casting long bars of shadow through the broken shutters and low eaves of the merchant quarter. A single tower bell rang in the distance, muffled, as if through cloth.

As they moved, Nyssa fell into step beside Garr. “I didn’t think they’d see us so quickly.”

“They didn’t,” Garr said. “They felt us, like wolves in the trees. They weren’t searching. They were hunting.”

Dain looked back once, then forward, saying nothing. “How much longer can this city pretend it’s fine?”

“As long as it takes,” Harmon answered. “Or until someone lights a match.”

They ducked behind a ruined wall that once bore a mural of the Empress’s Accord. Now it was a smear of red dust and faded symbols. Beyond it, the alley veered sharply into the back lanes near the quarter’s edge. Harmon paused there, resting momentarily on his cane.

“Safe house is three turns from here,” he said. “Let’s hope the others found better luck. Or at least fewer knives.”

Dain gave a grim smile. “Luck’s for gamblers.”

Nyssa brushed a fallen blossom from her shoulder and glanced back toward the noble district, where lanterns were starting to flicker on behind barred windows.

“This whole city’s playing cards with a demon,” she said. “And no one’s folded yet.”

The four of them moved as shadows, slipping through the soft skeleton of Stormhaven’s old bones. Whispers echoed from courtyards. A chant rose through the cobbles, low enough that they couldn't be sure they were hearing it, loud enough that it never left them. 

Above them, a silver banner caught the last light of the day. It hung limp, torn down the center, and bore no sigil.

They didn’t speak again until the safe house door closed behind them.
