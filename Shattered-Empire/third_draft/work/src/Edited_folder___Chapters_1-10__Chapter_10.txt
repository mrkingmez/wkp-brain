The wind rolled down the high cliffs of Forgehallow like a hammering breath, hot and dry even in the dying hours of the day. The city was built into the bones of the mountain, its foundations older than any kingdom’s claim. Long before the Empire had sent banners and laws into the highlands, the wildcat smiths of Forgehallow had carved out their halls with blood, fire, and invention. One of the few places where old laws bent under the weight of innovation. And in its deepest hold, a prison unlike any other clutched Riven Duskwind in silence and steel.
The main road curved along a shelf of blackstone, wide enough for a caravan, and watched by sentries in plated leather, their helms fitted with breathing filters for the slag-heavy lower tiers. Sparks and smoke rose from open forges built right into the cliff walls, giving the streets a hellish orange glow. Massive iron fans turned high above, circulating air through ducts like a city-sized bellows system.
They had been riding east of Rellisford for a day and a half when the road began to climb, and the air began to change. The green of the lowlands gave way to black rock and red stone, and the faint smell of something burned that never quite cleared.
The scent of scorched iron hit them before they saw the walls of Forgehallow. Ash rode ahead of the others, eyes narrowed against the rising heat that shimmered across the path like a mirage. Smoke belched from chimney stacks that jutted from the cliffside heart of the city, black mouths pumping soot that hung over Forgehallow like a storm that never broke.
“Smells like home,” Ash said with a quick, offhand delivery, a crooked half-smile tugging at his mouth. His memory flickered to his father’s forge.
Neavin replied in his short, grounded cadence, “Aye. Sweet mountain home. Fresh air, if you’re used to stone.”
Harmon and the others rode in quietly behind him, cloaks drawn tight. They all emerged from the mountain road leading into Forgehallow’s lower tier just as the sun dipped below the high ridge. The road dipped sharply, curling into a narrow pass between jagged redstone outcroppings until finally revealing the spiked gates of the Ember Gate, tall, reinforced steel with twin sigils of the Miner's Guild and Forgemaster’s Guard burned into the iron.
Dain pulled ahead, brushing dust from his dark doublet, the one with the silver trim he reserved for noble illusions. Nyssa rode beside him, eyes constantly scanning, the only one with any real memory of the town. Harmon rode rear guard, with Aurelia and Weslin between.
Wiping his nose, Garr muttered, “Feels like the mountain’s… alive. Listen.”
“It’s breathing,” Weslin said, pointing before he realized he was doing it. “The fans, the ducts. I mean, it’s made to, on purpose.
A guard in piecemeal metal armor, each plate clearly forged by a different smith, stepped forward, "State your business."
Dain flicked his reins and rode forward, wearing the face of smug nobility like a second skin. “We are here on contract,” Dain replied, polite as a blade. “Mining interests. We will not be staying long.”
The guard squinted, then nodded and waved them through. In Forgehallow, coin opened more doors than blood. As they were passing through, Harmon stopped at the guards and asked, “Do you know a good place to stable our horses?”
“Just around the corner. You’ve got the coin, they’ll take care of them. Coin has to be right.” He gave Harmon a wink. “Now, carry on. Clear the gate.”
Harmon nodded a thanks, then rode on. The streets inside were narrow and slick with soot. Sparks danced in the air like fireflies. From every other doorway came the sound of hammering, shouting, grinding, work that never stopped, day or night.
It didn’t take them long to find the stable. The Mercenary-Run stable sat like a broken tooth on the edge of Forgehallow’s outer district, half-swallowed by the stone cliffs surrounding the city’s eastern blast gate. Once part of the city’s original defensive wall, the blast gate had been built during an age when Forgehallow feared invasion more than collapse, thick steel doors twenty feet tall that now hung permanently open and rusted, bolted into disuse. Around the gate's gaping mouth, opportunists had erected a series of stone-and-metal lean-tos, scaffolds, and bolt-on structures until the whole thing resembled some monstrous, makeshift barn fused into the city's bones.
 The stable itself was a tangled marvel of industrial decay and rugged utility. Heavy iron beams, salvaged from collapsed mine shafts, formed the skeletal frame, bolted directly into the cliff’s face. The roof was patchworked from copper sheets and fire-treated oak, scarred with old burns from stray sparks. Heavy chains hung between beams, swaying in the draft like grim ornaments.
A large chalkboard mounted above the entry arch read “DUNFEATHER STABLES - PRIVATE MOUNT STORAGE - NO REFUNDS - NO LOITERING - NO QUESTIONS”, the last rule underlined twice.
Harmon led the group in, jaw set, eyes scanning. Inside, the stable smelled of sweat, soot, ash, leather, and horse piss. The floors were layered with gravel, straw, and broken rock, nothing fancy, just durable enough to keep hoof and boot from slipping. Instead of stalls, there were partitioned alcoves, some of stone, some of thick corrugated steel, where animals and odd mounts were kept. A mechanical hoist system ran along the back wall, clearly intended for heavier creatures or supplies. The place was lit by sputtering lanterns strung on wire, and the constant din of industry echoed in from the city beyond: hammers, steam valves, shouting.
As they stepped in, the stablemaster, a hulking, bald man wearing a leather apron stained with alchemical oil, looked up from repairing a bridle with a set of comically tiny pliers.
“Something I can keep alive for you?” the stablemaster grunted, not looking impressed.
“We’ve got nine horses. Good ones. We’ll need them stabled, fed, and untouched until we return,” Harmon said evenly, stepping forward and dropping a small leather pouch onto the nearby anvil with a heavy clink.
The Stablemaster opened the pouch with a practiced pinky claw, sniffed, then grunted. “That buys you four days. After that, I eat the loudest one.” His smile was a big, toothy grin.
“Deal,” Harmon replied without missing a beat. 
Nyssa chuckled under her breath. “He’s charming in the way danger is,” Nyssa said. “Careful, Harmon. You collect that sort.”
Ash smirked. “He’d drink us under the table,” Ash said, already moving on, “Then sell the table.”
As the stablemaster barked at two stablehands, one with a mechanical leg, to start leading the horses away, Aurelia laid a calming hand on her mount’s neck. “Do we trust them?” she asked softly.
“No.” Harmon didn’t hesitate. “They don’t trust us either. That makes it fair. Gold buys trust as much as anything.”
 Dain leaned near one of the stalls, eyeing a strange beast, part mule, part something reptilian, snorting softly in the corner. “Aggressively rustic,” Dain observed. He glanced at Nyssa. “You dealt with these people?”
 Nyssa shook her head. “Closest I came was getting chased out once,” she said, eyes flicking away. “Different reason. Different day.” 
The group took a last look at their mounts as they were guided away into the deeper recesses of the old gate-turned-stable. The shadows swallowed them slowly, clinking with chains and the scrape of hooves on stone. Harmon took the claim ticket from the half-ogre and slid it into a hidden pocket in his vest.
The group made its way out into the streets and left the cave that was the stable. The first full glimpse of Forgehallow opened before them like the lid of a ticking clockwork device. The air was warm, kissed with the bite of soot and the lingering sting of metal cooled too quickly. Tall smokestacks rose like jagged fingers from the earth. Stone and steel clashed in the architecture, buildings hunched together in a jagged sprawl, half-built or constantly rebuilt, stitched with brass piping and odd mechanisms that whirred. It wasn’t a beautiful city. It was raw, alive, and unapologetic.
Nyssa stepped forward, boots crunching slag as she watched a pulley groan overhead, lifting a crate of ore. “Forgehallow,” she muttered, half-caution, half familiarity. “Still loud, hot, and half-mad.”
Aurelia squinted up at the skyline, her brows knit. “It smells like burnt lightning.”
Ash grinned, inhaling deeply through his nose. “No... this is the scent of invention. Chaos. Unchecked genius.”
Brother Aldrek gave a quiet grunt, not quite disapproving, but not far from it either. “I’ve seen cleaner battlefields.”
Dain turned his head, eyeing a group of soot-blackened smiths arguing over the calibration of a steam-powered hammer the size of a cart. “And I suppose they’d call that culture,” he said, lips curling in distaste.
Weslin stared at the steam hammer long after Dain looked away. He had grown up on the docks. He knew what it meant when men built something that could break other things. But this felt different. This felt like a city that had forgotten to ask whether it should. 
They walked the slanted streets. “Nyssa, you know this place better than the rest of us. Where should we go?” asked Dain.
“I have only been here once before.”
“That is one more time than I believe the rest of us.”
"'I have only been here once before. I do not know much. I stayed at a place called The Iron Flask once. It was decent enough.”
Harmon chimed in, “Then that is where we will go. Do they have rooms, or is it just a tavern?”
“No, they have rooms. Good ones.”
“Well, lead the way then, Nyssa,” replied Harmon.
The group made their way to the tavern Nyssa recommended, The Iron Flask. A cozy place built into a repurposed smelting hall. 
Stepping inside was like inhaling a breath of comfort amid the forge-born chaos of the city.
The air smelled of cinnamon smoke, aged wood, and the faint trace of clean oil, not the sour kind that clung to forgehands. Soft citrine-glass lanterns hung from the rafters, casting warm pools of light onto the mismatched but purposefully chosen furniture below, and the old crucibles had been turned into makeshift fireplaces. 
 Each table told a story: one carved with dragons by a local sculptor, another made from the tailgate of a decommissioned mine cart, polished to a deep sheen. A long stone hearth ran along the back wall, its fire always crackling and kept by a rotating charm that turned the logs with a soft mechanical click every few minutes. 
The floors were darkwood, kept remarkably clean despite the muddy boots that passed through, and the walls bore banners from a dozen mercenary companies, some retired, some fallen, some still active. Between the cloth hung portraits of adventuring parties, blurred from age but framed with care.
 The bar counter itself was an old cannon chassis, plated flat and burnished to a smooth finish. It curved like a crescent moon around a raised serving pit, where bottles sat in soft light, and brass pipes vented off the gentle hiss of cold storage runes. 
Nyssa spoke so only her team could hear, “See that man who is standing behind the bar? He’s the owner of the place. Old Cerran, as the locals called him. He was once a caravan captain, now a tavern-keeper with a knack for keeping trouble outside and trust inside. 
Cerran was a tall, weathered man with salt-and-ash hair slicked back, a beard that curled neatly around a strong jaw, and deep-set eyes that somehow saw everything without judgment. He moved with the calm efficiency of someone who’d dodged sabers and sweet-talked anything. His sleeves were rolled, his apron spotless, and his voice had the gravel of age but the tone of a father who knew when to be firm and when to laugh. Regulars greeted him by name. Outsiders got a nod and a warm drink before they even asked.
The clientele was diverse, but not dangerous. Some off-duty forgehands, inventors with ink-stained sleeves, a few mercenaries with scarred faces but polite manners, and a small band of staring tinkerers arguing over blueprints at the corner table. Laughter rolled through the air, softened by the warm acoustics of the arched stone ceiling and dulled timber beams. An old playerless piano in the corner had a note pinned to it: "Plays itself when she's in the mood."
Music flowed in gentle notes from a subtle enchantment, a soothing melody with an old miner's rhythm, not quite bardic, but familiar like something heard once in childhood and never forgotten.
She drew their attention to the upper balcony, which featured private booths, shaded by velvet drapes and used mostly by negotiators, thinkers, or lovers who wanted privacy but not distance. On the ground floor, the tables were full but never crowded. “They told me this is a safe place. Even the roughest laughter stays polite because of Old Cerran. No blade’s been drawn here in a long while, not since he set a man’s own sword in front of him on the bar, asking him if he wanted a second chance or a third grave.”
The performance dropped out of her voice for just a moment. “This place gives me the feeling of a well-worn coat, patches and all, but it was stitched with craftsmanship and dignity. This is the kind of place that didn’t try to impress... it just made you feel…. Welcome.”
The team from Opindale took a booth in the corner, beneath a carving of a massive warhammer with runes etched into the handle.
“All right,” Harmon said, setting his pack beside him. “We’ve got a prisoner in a fortified hold, in a city run by inventors and weapon smugglers. We need him out, clean, and without violence.”
“That’s like saying you want to rob a dragon and not wake it,” Nyssa replied. “Forgehallow doesn’t do subtle.”
“Which is why we’re starting with Dain.” Harmon leaned forward. "Charm your way in. You are a noble from Reldwyn. Riven is a political prisoner or a diplomatic asset, your choice. Coin is not the obstacle. Get him out clean." 
"The easy job." He said it with the tone of a man who knew perfectly well it was not easy and appreciated the framing anyway. 
“I’m giving you the one I need you at. Everyone else? Recon. Get to know the streets. Find where they’re holding him, guard shifts, escape routes. Nyssa, you’re on the recon team, and for the creator’s sake, keep your head down. Aurelia, Weslin, you’re with me. “Keep it tight. I’ll secure rooms and a place to plan. Stay put.”
“Understood,” Aurelia said, her tone cool and focused.
“Dain, go get what you need to get ready,” ordered Harmon.
Dain got up and made his way to Garr, “Going to need some coin, good lad, I need to get a few things to get this right.” Garr looked up at him and didn’t know if he just smirked or if that was a permanent look on his face. He shook his head as he fished out the coin purse. He handed over two coins. 
“Just two coins? Come on, I need finery, not homespun scraps.” He glanced around with mock offense. “No insult meant.”
“Look again,” Garr muttered as he put the purse away. “Then speak.” 
Dain opened his hand to see not silver, not gold, but platinum. “Never mind, this will do nicely.”
Harmon spoke up, “I will need change, and have them give you a receipt for it as well, now off with you.”
Dain rose, tucked the platinum away with the care it deserved, and walked out with his shoulders back and his chin at exactly the angle a lord's son learns before he learns anything else. 
Harmon looked over the group. “I’ll get us rooms, three to start. Stay put.” He rose and headed for the bar.
After Harmon left, the rest of the team just sat looking at each other, with this awkward silence. Garr made a coughing noise. When Ash spoke up, “So how long do you think it’ll take Dain to insult the entire garrison? I’m betting... what, ten minutes?”

Nyssa grinned. “Ten? He probably insulted half the garrison before he got through the door. That jaw’s sharper than his blade.”

Brother Aldrek said, “If he gets himself thrown in,” Aldrek said, folding his arms, “I’m not paying for two bail-outs.”

Neavin, without looking at the group as he took in the whole room, “Let’s pray the guards don’t mind speeches. Or nobles. Or his face.”

They all give a little chuckle at that. Aurelia said, “He means well, even if he speaks like someone who’s never heard the word tact.”

Weslin shied away from the conversation as he tries to think of something to say. 

His thoughts were broken by the heartmaid who showed up with a round of drinks for the crew. She placed each one in front of each person, then,o as fast as she could, she went to her next table. 
Weslin had been listening to all of it without finding a place to land. When the second round of quiet confessions came around the table, he held his cup and tried to think of what his reason was. Not the official one. The real one. After a moment, he said, without looking up, "I don't want to go back to being the boy who knocked over the armor." He said it to the table more than to anyone at it. "I want to be someone different when this is done." He glanced up, realized everyone had heard him, and looked back down. "Sorry. That probably sounds stupid."
Ash looked at him. "Nah," he said. "Sounds about right."
Brother Aldrek had not touched his cup. He looked at it the way a man looks at something that does not concern him. "I do not drink," he said. He did not make a virtue of it. 

“Did he just get us all the same thing?” Nyssa asked.

Garr laughed, “Drink it first, and you will see.” Each person kind of shrugs and takes a drink.

With a new smile on her face, she said, “This is wine and a good wine at that.”

“Mine is an Ale,” Neavin said.

Brother Aldrek looked inside his cup, and seeing what those two had just said, he took a drink, and to his delight, it was just water. He grunts in enjoyment, and he takes another drink.

In a better mood, Nyssa takes another drink, “Well," she said, swirling her drink lazily, "if the mission goes half as smoothly as getting here, we’ll be legends... or very colorful smears on someone's wanted poster.”
Ash leaned back in his chair, boots kicked up on an empty one beside him, "So," he said lazily, "guess it’s sink or swim now."
Brother Aldrek gave a dry grunt, the weight of too many battlefields in his voice.
 "Funny thing about battles, iron sharpens iron, or it breaks. Guess we’ll see which we are."
Nyssa drummed her fingers on the table’s scarred wood. "Sink, swim... or drown each other trying."
Garr rumbled low, crossing his massive arms over his chest. "Harmon doesn't pick fools. If he thinks this will work, I trust him. Even if it feels like madness right now."
Aurelia smiled faintly over her cup. "Not the point, I think."
Neavin, quiet until now, set down his mug with a soft thunk. "We keep acting like loners. We won't last the week."
That pulled a small, sharp laugh out of Ash.
 "Touch of optimism in you, Neavin. Thought you moody types were all about doom and gloom."
Neavin shrugged, the movement slow and deliberate. "Doom and gloom, sure. But survival, too. And right now? We need each other."
There was a silence at that, the kind that wasn't uncomfortable so much as heavy, like a weight settling across all their shoulders.
Ash rolled his mug once between his palms, watching the surface of the drink ripple before he spoke.
“Let’s not get sentimental,” he said lightly, but there was an edge under it. “We’re here because Harmon brought us, and Harmon pays on time.”
A few nods answered him. Nothing dramatic. Just an agreement.
Garr shifted in his seat, massive shoulders settling. “And because the man he answers to doesn’t tolerate messes,” he added. “Adin’s name carries weight. You don’t start fights under that shadow unless you’re trying to get remembered for the wrong reasons.”
Aurelia glanced around the table, measuring the room the way she measured people. “That’s not trust,” she said calmly. “It’s alignment. Temporary. Functional.”
Neavin snorted quietly into his cup. “Means we don’t need to like each other. Just need to not get in each other’s way.”
Nyssa leaned back, letting the moment breathe. “Fine by me,” she said. “I’ve done worse jobs with people I trusted less.”
Brother Aldrek folded his arms, the motion deliberate. “Coin buys cooperation,” he said. “Discipline keeps it from becoming something uglier.”
Ash raised his mug the rest of the way. “There it is,” he said. “We play nice. We get paid. We leave with the same number of holes we came in with.”
A pause settled over the table. Not warm. Not cold. Solid.
Then Neavin nodded once. “For the job,” he said. “That’s all it needs to be.”
Nyssa leaned in, voice soft but insistent. "Fine. Some are here for a second shot at something, aren’t we?" She looked down at her cup and leaned in, voice low. "Or a third." She looked deeper into her cup, "At something worth the trip." 
Ash raised his mug in a mock salute. "Third shot, or more if we're being honest."
Aldrek gave a low, humorless chuckle. "For me, it's penance."
Garr snorted. "For me, it's the fight. Always the fight. Plus, I owe Harmon."
Aurelia looked down into her drink, voice barely above the tavern’s low rumble. "I’m here to prove it wasn’t all for nothing." This gave everyone pause before they all took a drink.
Neavin glanced around the table, his pale eyes sharp. "Then maybe that's enough. For now."
They sat with that for a few heartbeats, different, broken, and stubborn, and for the first time, a thin thread pulled between them, unseen but real.
Ash broke the moment, slapping his boots down with a thud. "Well. If we’re stuck with each other, might as well learn to aim past each other’s heads, eh?"
Nyssa grinned wickedly. "Speak for yourself. I aim exactly where I mean to."
More laughter this time, a little lighter, a little easier.
And just as the tension started to finally bleed off, Harmon’s boots clunked back across the floor, a keyring swinging from his fingers.
"Rooms sorted," he said, voice low but satisfied. "I hope you lot didn't burn the place down while I was gone."
Ash tossed him a lazy salute. "Not for lack of trying, boss."
They all gathered themselves, the air between them just a little different now. Still strangers. But not entirely alone.
