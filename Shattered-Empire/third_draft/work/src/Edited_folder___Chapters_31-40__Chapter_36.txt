Snow drifted in lazy coils past the high windows of the old stables. The roof creaked faintly overhead, settling with the cold. In the loft above the main floor, past narrow wooden stairs and between beams thick with dust and hay, Reau and Weslin stepped into the hollow stillness of their temporary safehouse.

It was empty.

No voices. No movement. Just the scent of old straw, oil, and worn leather. Crates and hay bales had been pushed to the edges to form barricades, and peepholes cut into the loft walls gave discreet views of the alley, the yard, and the market road beyond. A single cold lamp sat on a crate near the center, giving off a faint yellow glow.

Weslin’s boots scraped softly on the planks. “We’re first.”

Reau glanced at him, noting the boy’s posture, the hunched shoulders, the clenched jaw, the way his hand trembled as it hovered near the yellow shard at his belt.

“That’s not bad,” Reau said evenly. “First means we’re alive.”

Weslin sat on a barrel near the window, head low. “Or it means we did not do enough,” he said. “What if we missed something?”

“You pushed the shard. That’s enough risk for one day.”

“I thought it would help,” Weslin muttered. “But now I feel like I broke something. In me.”

Reau crouched beside him. “You didn’t. You’re rattled, not broken. Let it pass.”

Weslin nodded slowly, but his thoughts still swirled behind his eyes, guilt curling tighter with every breath.

A muffled clatter of wheels and distant boots passed in the street below, then faded. A few minutes later, the door creaked at the far end of the stable. Harmon stepped up into the loft first, snow melting on his shoulders. Dain, Nyssa, and Garr followed, cloaks drawn tight. Harmon scanned the space quickly, old soldier habits never dulled.

“Anyone on our tail?” Harmon asked quietly, then nodded toward the far corner. Nyssa immediately slipped toward the storage room in the corner of the loft, boots clicking lightly. “This dress wasn’t made for rooftop sprints,” she muttered. “And these shoes? My feet are staging a rebellion.”

When she emerged minutes later, she was back in her tight black leathers, practical and familiar, but the noble makeup still framed her face. Dusky rouge, plum lips, and an icy highlight around her eyes.

Dain looked up, mouth twitching. “You forgot to wash the war paint.”

Nyssa tilted her head. “Sometimes a girl just wants to feel pretty.”

Dain didn’t blink. “You don’t need makeup for that.”

The quiet that followed wasn’t awkward; it was thick.

She deflected with a smirk. “Careful, Theren Vayle. You’re starting to sound sincere.”

They both smiled. Real ones.

Then Dain nodded once and turned toward the storage room to change. Nyssa’s gaze lingered on him longer than she intended.

Below, hooves kicked against wooden stalls. The horses, restless.

More snow hissed against the windows.

Neavin, Aurelia, and Brother Aldrek arrived next. Neavin looked like he hadn’t spoken since they left; Aurelia’s steps were light but cautious, and Aldrek’s eyes swept every rafter as though expecting something to crawl down from the roof.

“Quiet night,” Aldrek said.

“Too quiet,” Neavin replied, brushing snow off his shoulder. “Even the wrong people weren’t talking.”

Aurelia pulled off her cloak, shook snow from her hair, and offered a brief nod to Harmon before scanning the room. Her eyes landed on Weslin.

He sat near the back wall, half in shadow, clutching his knees. His breath came low and ragged, fingers still trembling slightly. He avoided her gaze.

Aurelia approached and knelt beside him. “What happened?”

Weslin didn’t answer at first. His jaw worked, eyes distant.

“I tried to listen,” he said finally. “I did what you told me. I let it flow, tried to hear the passive layer... but there was nothing. Like... like my head was underwater. Nothing but pressure and silence. So I pushed. Just a little. Then a little more. And then…”

He looked at his hands.

“Then I broke through something, a wall. I saw things. Horrible things. I can still see that room when I close my eyes. But to do it, I think I tapped the shard. I think I drew too much. And now... I think I’m broken.”

Aurelia gently placed a hand on his arm. Her face was unreadable, but something behind her eyes flickered, worry... or fear.

“You’re not broken,” she said softly. “But you did something dangerous. And that’s why you need to rest. Don’t carry it alone. We still have work to do before you're ready for that. It's ok, now you know what you pay when you rush.”

Reau’s voice came from just behind them. Calm. Steady.

“He did tap it,” Reau said, stepping closer. “But I was there. I talked him back down before he went too far. He was burning hot, but he came back.”

Weslin jerked away from Aurelia’s hand and stood abruptly, pacing two steps before spinning on his heel. “No. I messed up again. First the ward, now this. I don’t belong out there, I don’t belong here.”

Reau stepped forward and wrapped an arm around him, not as a command, but as an anchor. He pulled Weslin in close, grounding the storm.

“I was just like you,” Reau said, voice low. “Just as young. Just as scared. I didn’t know how to hold it either, not at first. I got thrown in deep and almost drowned. You’re not alone in this.”

He looked over Weslin’s head, nodding toward Aurelia. “She’s here for you. So am I. We’re all on your side.”

Weslin pulled away slightly, tears in his eyes. “But I have to prove I deserve to be here, that I have earned this opportunity. I need to show I’m not just a kid, Harmon felt sorry for.”

Aurelia’s voice, soft and unwavering, cut through the rising storm. “You’re here. That is the proof.”

Weslin shook his head slowly, exhaustion dragging at his limbs. He sank back down to the floor, pulled the blanket around his shoulders like armor, and stared at the dying light outside the stable loft window.

“Maybe I just need to rest,” he said quietly. “Then tomorrow I try again without losing control.”

Reau crouched beside him. “That would be best for now.”

Aurelia gave a gentle nod. “When you wake, you’ll feel better. And we can talk more.”

Snow continued to fall outside the window, a soft hush against the turmoil settling into the corners of the loft. The boy curled inward. And the others, for now, let him.

In the corner, Garr took up watch beside the eastern peephole, still armored, still tense.

“Should’ve been back by now,” he said. “Riven and Ash.”

“They’ve got good instincts,” Harmon said from a crate where he was cleaning the head of his cane. “They’ll come in hot, though. They always do.”

Dain lowered onto a hay bale and began unstrapping his boots. “You’d think we were being hunted.”

Nyssa sat beside him. “We are.”

A slow silence took hold. The kind that gets into the bones.

Then, a thump. Scuffed boots. The crack of the stable door opening below.

Everyone froze.

Ash burst into the loft first, half-covered in soot and blood. None of it was his. His grin was too wide. “Surprise. I brought a friend.”

Riven followed, limping slightly, one sleeve torn at the shoulder and dark with old fluid. His eyes swept the room like a cat cornered one too many times.

“We were followed,” he said. “But we lost them. Maybe.” Harmon’s eyes narrowed. “How many, how close?”
Garr was already halfway to the stairs before Harmon held up a hand. “Too late to chase shadows. We hold here.” Garr swore under his breath and swung back to the eastern peephole, scanning the yard.
Riven leaned against a post, catching his breath. “They were listening. Every step. I don’t know how, but they were waiting. Feels like the city itself is eavesdropping.”
Ash dropped to the floor beside the others. “We got in trouble. Again. But we brought a mystery.”

No one laughed. Reau stood slowly, and Weslin stood beside him. Outside, snow continued to fall. Inside, every boot that crossed that floor tracked a different story into the dark. 

The loft had gone quiet, but not still. Breath fogged the air. Cloaks steamed. Muscles twitched with strain. Hunger gnawed from within, and exhaustion sat just behind every eye like a second shadow.

Harmon didn’t raise his voice. He didn’t need to. “Let’s stop spinning around what you saw,” he said. “I want answers. Three of them. What happened to Stormhaven? Who’s behind it? And can we take it back?”

He looked across the room, his gaze hard as iron, but steady.

“Start.”

Neavin stepped forward, still wearing the dust of the streets. “The shrine we found wasn’t old. It was built to resemble its original appearance. Ritual tiers, red cloth, carved runes, all forged. The doctrine was nonsense. The titles are fake. The scroll was signed by someone calling themselves the High Minister of the New Flame. That position doesn’t exist.”

Aurelia nodded, arms folded tightly. “The people inside believed it. Real chanting. Real sacrifice. They weren’t actors. They’d been rewritten.”

“You think the shrine is the center?” Garr asked.

“No,” Neavin said. “It’s a decoy. They want people chasing symbols while they move underneath.”

“They? Who are they?” Nyssa asked, eyebrow arched.

“Not an army," Neavin said. "That's what bothers me."

 "It means they didn't need one," Nyssa said quietly. 

“You sure it wasn’t?” Dain cut in. “Because what I saw at that tea wasn’t peace. It was an occupation, with lace and a smile.”

Nyssa slid off the barrel she was leaning against. “They dressed it up, made it civil, but we saw the edges, loyalty tests. Families vanishing. The governor’s household? Refused the oath. Gone. Three noble houses followed.”

“And the rest?” Dain said. “Marching in circles. Around the tower. Over and over. They weren’t being watched. They were trained.”

Ash muttered, “Trained for what?”

“To obey,” Aurelia answered.

“They used politeness as a weapon,” Nyssa said. “A smile covers a dagger in that district now.”

“They call it ‘reeducation,’” Dain said. “But I watched a noble flinch when the word ‘Korr’ came up. Nearly spilled his drink.”

“Same name we found,” Aurelia said.

Neavin added, “Always whispered. Never explained.”

Riven, who’d been quiet near the post, stepped forward. “It’s worse than whispered. In the undercity, the name changes people. One man forgot it after he said it. Another froze. Someone else whispered it like a prayer.”

“You think it’s a person?” Garr asked.

“No,” Reau said, stepping forward. “We think it’s a symbol. A trigger.”

He laid down the sketches. The stonework. The runes. The basin.

“This is what we found under the forge district. A chamber. Built like a vault, but breathing like a womb. Pressure in the stone. Anchors intact. The runes were old. But active.”

Aldrek crossed the floor and tapped the diagram. “That is a warbeast vault, from the Second Magic War, or older. They were not just storage rooms. They were built to direct constructs. With thought, not voice.” Garr let out a low breath that was almost a whistle.

Reau nodded. “If they activate it, it won’t defend. It’ll lead.”

Ash gave a humorless chuckle. “And we’re worried about nobles with bad posture.”

“You should be,” Nyssa said. “They’re being used too.”

Weslin finally spoke. His voice was soft, but it filled the room. He swallowed once. “I saw it.”

All eyes turned to him.

“When I tapped the shard… I broke through. I know I wasn’t supposed to, but it just happened. When it happened, I saw this chamber. Dozens kneeling. And at the center, a figure. Tall. Masked. It looked at me.”

“Did it see you?” Aurelia asked gently.

Weslin nodded. “It knew I was there. Not sure what it saw, I just saw the mask.”

Neavin’s voice came low. “Did it speak?”

“No. But the others were saying something…Like a chant. Just one name. Korr.”

Ash shifted. “We keep circling that name, and no one has an answer.”

“Because it’s not a man,” Aurelia said, her voice cracking for the first time. “It’s a presence. A doctrine. A lie made powerful by how many people believe it.”

“You’re wrong,” Aldrek said sharply.

Aurelia turned to him.

“This isn’t just doctrine,” he growled. “It’s blasphemy. They’ve built a temple over sacred ground. They’ve replaced sacrifice with puppetry. What I saw in those tunnels, those symbols, they weren’t binding anything. They were feeding it.”

He pointed to the sketch. “You want to know who’s behind it? Look beneath your feet.”

“And the explosion at Ironmarch?” Reau said. “That wasn’t just a rebel strike, was it?”

“No,” Harmon said firmly. “It was destroyed for another reason.”

Garr spoke next. “We saw it. There used to be a protective circle there. Old. Layered through every governor statue. Each one added strength to the seal.”

"They blew it from below," Reau said. "The crack pattern goes up, not out. Someone knew the seal was there before they moved on to the plaza." 

“And whatever it was holding?” Aurelia said. “Is now awake.”

“Is that what the Red Cloaks came for?” Nyssa asked. “To wake it?”

“They weren’t the first,” Riven said. “We found names. Lists. The people they took weren’t random. They were chosen.”

“For what?” Dain asked.

“To disappear,” Riven said. “Or become something else.”

Weslin hugged his arms. “They’re being hollowed out.”

Garr turned. “Meaning what?”

“I don’t know,” Weslin admitted. “But I saw it. In the way the guards moved. The way the people walked in those loops. They weren’t being ordered. They were being emptied.”

“They were being replaced, either physically or mentally,” Aurelia whispered.

Neavin’s voice cut through: “That old woman we met, do you remember her, Aurelia?”

Aurelia’s brow furrowed. “Yes. I thought she was harmless.”

“You don’t know?” Neavin asked.

Aurelia’s voice dropped. “She quoted from a text that doesn’t exist. She smiled when she said the world was cleaner now. And then, two minutes later, she didn’t remember saying it.”

“Possession?” Riven asked.

“Reprogramming,” Aurelia said. “Piece by piece.”

Reau glanced toward Weslin, then Aurelia. “It’s like someone took what was human and filled it with pattern.”

Silence.

Neavin broke it. “So that’s what happened. They didn’t take the city by force. They infected it. Corrupted the layers. The nobles. The rituals. The wards. Then, when no one was looking.”

“They created a prison, they don’t know they are in,” Aldrek finished.

Harmon nodded once. “So who’s in charge now?”

“I don’t think there is a ‘who,’” Aurelia said. “There’s a name. There’s a mask. But it’s not worn by one person.”

“Someone’s leading the Red Cloaks,” Garr said.

“Maybe,” Neavin said. “But no one we spoke to has ever seen the governor. Just his orders. His statues. His will.”

“They don’t need a person,” Aurelia said. “They need obedience.”

“They need conversion,” Dain corrected. “That’s what they’re doing. Rewriting Stormhaven.”

Reau folded his arms. “The deeper we dug, the older it felt. As if the city’s foundation was waiting to be rewritten.”

“Stormhaven was not built over ruin,” Aldrek murmured. “It was built over a prison.”

“And that prison,” Aurelia added, “has cracked.”

“And if that warbeast activates,” Aldrek said, “they’ll march under its banner.”

Ash folded his arms. “So what do we tell the king? That the city possessed?”

“No,” Harmon said. “We tell him the truth.”

“Which is what?” Garr asked.

“That we don’t have one,” Reau said.

“We have pieces,” Neavin said. “No lies, but too many truths. Rituals, tunnels, missing families. But it doesn’t stitch clean.”

“Because it wasn’t meant to,” Aurelia said.

Nyssa stepped forward. “Then let’s start stitching anyway. Everything we saw. Every tunnel, every route, every face that vanished.”

“We map it all,” Reau said.

“Mark the shrine,” Aurelia added. “Even the fake one.”

“Especially the fake one,” Neavin said.

Dain looked at Harmon. “We’ll need more than facts. We’ll need proof. Pictures. Records.”

Harmon nodded. “We build the report. Everything goes to the king tonight. No delay.”

“What do we tell him?” Weslin asked. “What do we call this?”

Harmon’s eyes drifted to the warbeast sketch, then the scattered maps, then to the boy still shivering near the wall. He didn’t hesitate. “We tell him Stormhaven didn’t fall.” He looked up. “It was overwritten.”

Nobody breathed for a moment. The lamp sat untouched. The map lay flat under no one's hands. A pause. Not silence, but reverence. No one moved. Ash shifted his weight as if he wanted to make a joke, then thought better of it. 


Brother Aldrek stepped to the center of the loft. He removed one glove and placed it gently on the floor. Then he knelt. No one stopped him. He bowed his head and began to pray, not loudly, not with the force of his sermons, but with the soft rhythm of someone speaking directly to a god he feared was no longer listening.

“For what was built in stone, you gave shape. What was cast in blood, you gave breath. But now the bones stir with no soul, and the breath speaks lies. We ask not for vengeance, but for clarity. For light enough to see what walks among us. Grant us that, and we will not falter.”

Aurelia sat down beside Weslin. Nyssa leaned into the window frame. Ash stood at the back, arms folded, fire dimmed in his chest for once. Even Riven said nothing.

The wind outside rattled the loft's edge. Inside, a different type of chill took hold, one from a picture that kept forming questions where answers should have been. The room felt as if this night had no end in sight. 

Not just in the way Nyssa's hands twitched toward her belt pouch, or in the way Garr stood too still at the window, but in the way they all moved now, slower, stripped of rhythm. The kind of exhaustion that didn’t just sap strength, but bent judgment, stole clarity. Eyes drooped. Shoulders slumped. Muscles ached with cold. 

Dain leaned against a support beam, rubbing the bridge of his nose. Weslin, knees drawn to his chest, was still sketching spirals in the margin of an old operations report, charcoal snapping under his fingers every few strokes. Aurelia sat hunched, staring at the same knot in the wooden floorboard for what might have been minutes, lips moving faintly. Reau hadn’t spoken in some time. Aurelia twisted the old woman’s words in her mind like wire. And in the center, Harmon stood upright, composed, unshaken, because he had to be.

“Clean. Direct. What happened here, who’s behind it, and what will it take to get the city back?”
He let the words settle into the bones of the room. A few glances passed, but no one moved. One pass to empty the room. Now, a second to find what the first missed. 

The rafters groaned softly above them. “Start with the first,” Harmon said. “Who is behind this? Speak plainly.”

“I believe it’s the Red Cloaks,” Neavin said quietly. “But not just them. They’re the blade, not the hand.”

Riven snorted. “You think they’re acting alone? They’re soldiers. Fanatics. But they’re being led.”

“I agree,” Neavin said. “There’s structure. Timing. And too many psychological tactics to be accidental.”

“Morlin,” Dain offered. “It’s them. Or some that resemble them. We saw their signature,” He made a hand motion between himself and Nyssa, “Rigid social control, symbolic dominance, purging of nobility, restructuring of classes.”

Nyssa nodded slowly. “And deliberate severance from truth. We asked questions that made them twitch.”

“But Morlin wouldn’t need the rituals,” Aurelia said. “They’ve never bothered with that kind of veil. Whoever this is, they’re building a belief system.”

“Maybe they’re copying one,” Ash said. “Spook the locals into compliance.”

“No,” Aldrek said. His voice was sharp. “They’re not mimicking. They’re converting. We found theology, not propaganda. And it’s spreading faster than any force doctrine I’ve ever seen.”

Harmon looked to Aurelia. “Do you believe it’s religious in nature?”

Aurelia hesitated. “Yes. But not in the way most faiths rise. This is deeper. More invasive. It doesn’t ask for your belief. It replaces the need for it. It implants it.”

“And what about this Korr?” Garr asked, arms crossed. “We’ve all heard the name. But no one’s seen the face.”

“That’s the point,” Riven said. “It’s a title, something anyone can wear.”

“Or,” Weslin said softly, “something wearing a person.” Silence followed immediately. The wind slammed against the wall, causing Ash to jump.

Harmon broke the silence. “You saw this.”

Weslin nodded. “In the vision. When I tapped the shard, there was a figure, tall, masked. Everyone around it bowed. It didn’t speak. But it saw me. Not just… noticed. It saw into me.”

“You think that’s Korr?” Nyssa asked.

“I can’t prove it,” Weslin said. “But nothing else fits.”

Dain exhaled, lips thin. “So… a construct? A god? A puppet?”

Reau finally spoke. “An echo. Maybe something left behind and reshaped by what they broke under the city.”

“They didn’t just take Stormhaven,” Aurelia said. “They awoke something in it. Something that was buried.”

“You’re saying it was always here?” Garr asked, voice tightening.

“Buried,” Aldrek said. “Bound. That warbeast chamber wasn’t just a tomb, it was a lock. They broke it open.”

“And the Red Cloaks,” Neavin continued, “took the opportunity. But they weren’t the ones who caused the rupture. They exploited it.”

Riven leaned against a beam, arms crossed. “Then who set the trigger?”

They all looked at each other.

No one answered.

Until Aurelia’s voice rose again, haunted and quiet: “The people in that shrine didn’t seem coerced. They had nothing left but faith. That means this started long before they got here.”

Harmon nodded once, absorbing it all. “I've seen the Red Cloaks work. This isn't them. Not the whole of it.” He paced once, fingers moving at his beard. Stopped. Turned. “Fine,” he said. “We’ll build from that. Now, what happened to the city?”

Dain pushed off the wall, pacing now, fire returning to his voice. “This wasn’t a siege. It was a surgical replacement. Hit the tower, rewire the upper class, wipe out dissenters.”

“At that tea,” Nyssa said, “there were only four noble families. Four. Out of how many?”

“Sixteen,” Dain confirmed. “And one of those said the loyalty test was mandatory. The rest? Removed. ‘Reeducated.’ Or missing.”

“So the nobles are being targeted,” Reau said. “What about the rest?”

“They’ve been turned into watchers,” Aurelia said. “Each class reshaped to control the one beneath. Nobles patrol ritual patterns. Servants recite chant loops. Market folk don’t remember faces. It’s all closed circuit.”

“And the governor?” Riven asked.

“We asked,” Nyssa said. “All we ever got was ‘He is watching.’ Not where. Not when. Just... present.”

“No public appearances,” Dain added. “But new statues. New orders. They use his name, but no one’s seen his face.”

“They don’t need to,” Aurelia said. “The presence is enough. The idea is enough.”

“They're rewriting the city,” Weslin murmured.

“They’re desecrating it,” Aldrek said.

All heads turned. Aldrek stepped forward, unblinking. “I’ve seen what faith looks like when it grows wild. But this… this is deliberate heresy. They’ve buried truth under ritual, turned worship into submission, and they’ve mocked the Creator’s image with a hollow one.”

His fists curled. “The symbols they carve are not divine. They’re parasitic. Feeding on what’s left of the sacred.”

“You saw this?” Harmon asked.

“In the vault. The blood basin. The chanting. It was old magic, but inverted. There was no containment left. Only hunger.”

Reau looked to him. “The warbeast?”

“Still dormant. But twitching. If they finish the rite, that thing will march through stone like air.”

Nyssa gave a sharp laugh. “And what? Lead their army?”

“No,” Aldrek said. “Replace it.”

Garr’s tone turned cold. “So we can’t take the city.”

“No,” Aldrek answered.

“Not directly,” Reau added. “Every citizen could be a relay, or a soldier. Every wall could hear. And if that construct wakes…”

“Then it’s not just us who fall,” Aurelia said. “It’s everything, every kingdom.”

Ash leaned forward, elbows on knees. “So what, we wait? Watch it spread?”

“No,” Harmon said. “We report. We warn. And we prepare to burn the infection out before it crosses the borders.”

He stepped back from the table.

“Everyone, bring what we have. We make the report now.”

Reau moved first, gathering his sketches. Neavin and Aurelia passed the ritual documents to him. Weslin slowly handed off his notepad, knuckles white.

Harmon walked into the storage room and closed the door behind him, parchment already unfurled in his hand.

Outside the door, the others exhaled.

Ash cracked his neck. “Well. That was cheery.”

“I don’t like this,” Garr muttered. “It feels like we’re sitting on a powder keg with a lit fuse.”

“Then sit quiet,” Nyssa said. “We’ll need the energy.”
Riven leaned against a support beam, arms folded, his eyes fixed on Dain like a hawk watching a stray dog.
“You really think it’s Morlin? That neat and clean?”
“I never said that,” Dain replied without looking up.
“You didn’t have to,” Riven muttered. “That smug shrug of yours did it for you.”
Dain stood, slow and deliberate. “You’ve got a talent for hearing things no one said.”
“You’ve got a talent for saying nothing at all,” Riven shot back. “Nobles are good at that, speaking in fog and acting like it’s wisdom.”
“Oh, piss off, Riven.”
“Careful,” Riven said, his voice low but cutting. “You’re not as sharp as you pretend to be.”
“And you’re not half as clever as you think.”
That did it. The tone was too loud. Too pointed. Heads turned.
Dain stepped forward, his posture iron-straight, fists half-curled. “Keep talking. Maybe we’ll see if that clever mouth of yours can still function with a boot in it.”
Riven smirked like a man itching for a reason. “Go ahead. Swing first. You nobles love your formalities. Maybe I’ll even let you draw a circle first.”
Dain didn’t swing.
He shoved.
The crack of wood and leather snapped through the loft. A crate toppled nearby, dust pluming into the lamplight.
It wasn’t a blow meant to hurt. Just to land. To prove.
Riven’s smile didn’t fade. “You’ve got spirit, noble. I’ll give you that. Like a stray dog who doesn’t know when to back down.”
Before Dain could lunge again, Nyssa shoved between them, shoulder-first and full of fury.
“Enough,” she snapped, eyes flashing. “Gods, you two, grow up.”
They both fell still. The tension didn’t vanish. It just coiled tighter.
Nyssa looked between them, disgusted. “You want to fight? Do it after we’re dead. Until then, shut it down.”
She turned on her heel and stalked off, leaving the air jagged behind her.
Ash muttered, “That was productive.” Aurelia sat again, rubbing her eyes.
Riven’s grin faded as Nyssa stormed off. He watched her go, not with regret, but something harder to name. Then he turned, grabbed the coin off the crate behind him, and flicked it once into the air before pocketing it without a word.
He moved back to the shadows near the stairwell and leaned against the post, head lowered. Not sulking. Waiting. Coiled. Whatever storm had stirred in him hadn’t passed. It had just gone quiet.
Dain didn’t follow Nyssa with his eyes. He didn’t move at all at first. He just stood there, breathing slowly through his nose, hands flexing open and closed like he was counting seconds. Then he sat back down, not on the crate he’d been on before, but on the cold floorboards, legs drawn up, arms braced on his knees. Trying to get his anger to leave.
He didn’t look at Riven. He didn’t need to. Whatever passed between them wasn’t over. Just paused. Weslin leaned against Reau’s shoulder, eyelids drooping. The wind howled outside.
Suddenly. A knock. Slow. Hollow. Everyone froze. Garr and Neavin moved fast. Weapons out. No panic in it. There was only quiet. Then a faint creak.
The note was pinned to the inside of the doorframe, ink still bleeding slightly from the cold. Whoever left it hadn’t fled. They’d walked away with calm confidence. 
That detail bothered Harmon more than the note itself. Someone knew where they were. And instead of using that knowledge against them, they'd used it to help. That was either the most dangerous kind of ally or the most dangerous kind of trap. 
Neavin peeled it free and brought it back upstairs. His eyes met Aurelia’s, and without a word, he handed it to her.
She unfolded it carefully, her voice cutting the silence like a drawn blade.

‘The governor’s bloodline has not yet been spilled.
They are held beneath the tower.
Cleansing begins tomorrow evening.
The pattern completes at dusk.’
No one spoke.
Reau's breath whistled once through his nose. Nyssa’s hand went to her hip. Garr shifted toward the window.
Aurelia reread the note, aloud, slower this time. Weslin swallowed hard. “They know where we are.”
“And they want us to know,” Reau said grimly.
Neavin stepped toward the side room where Harmon had gone to begin his briefing. “He’s still in there.”
He didn’t knock. He opened the door and walked through, followed by Aurelia, then Nyssa. The others remained behind, the tension crackling in the air behind them.
Inside, the mirror on the far wall shimmered with living light. On its surface stood King Edric, his broad shoulders lit by the high torchlight of his war chamber. And beside him, blurred slightly by the edges of the mirror's range, stood Governor Meridian Drake, fine robes half-unfastened, his eyes hopeful, a sheen of sweat across his brow.
Harmon stood before them, arms braced on a crate-turned-desk, old battlefield posture half-returned. He turned sharply, brow drawn as he saw Neavin enter unbidden.
“Now’s not the time…”
Aurelia stepped past Neavin and raised the parchment. “It is.” She held it up so both men in the mirror could see. The effect was immediate. Governor Drake staggered a half-step closer, pale with disbelief. His voice broke. “My wife. My daughters. My son. My sister… gods.” He turned to the king, half in plea, half in panic. “They’re alive. They are ALIVE! Please.”
King Edric turned sharply toward Harmon. “Is this credible?”
“To my knowledge, yes. This note was nailed to our door,” Harmon said flatly. “They knew where to find us. They knew who we reported to.”
Governor Drake moved until his face dominated the mirror’s field of view.
“If we don’t act,” he said, voice cracking beneath the weight of it, “they die. They are the last of my line. I’m paying for this mission, not just with coins, but with my reputation. I will not pay for it with their lives. There is a high price to failing, as well. Please save them, for all of our sakes.”
The silence held, thick, brittle.
King Edric was the first to move. He turned his attention fully onto Harmon.
“Can your team handle this?” he asked. “You’ve been out there. You’ve seen the city. If I give this order, do they have what it takes to see it through before dusk tomorrow?”
Harmon stared into the mirror, not with defiance, but with certainty.
“That’s why you agreed to this,” he said. “I trust my team.” He allowed a rare smile to flicker. “You should, too.”
The king’s lips pressed into a line, but he nodded once. “Therefore, change of orders then, recover the family and get out, bring back what information you can, and we will go from there.”
King Edric Wyman turned fully toward Harmon, voice sharp with command.
“Then you’ll have every underground map Governor Drake can provide. We’ll transmit them within the hour. Make ready. And may God help you.”
Governor Drake was still in the mirror's frame. His expression was tight, eyes red-rimmed from barely checked desperation. “There’s a vault,” he said, voice low. “Beneath the Capitol’s south wing. The old records chamber, sealed off after the archive fire twenty-one years ago. Most think it collapsed. It didn’t.” He took a breath. “The original plans of Stormhaven are there. Every tunnel, every forgotten route, even the sealed catacombs. And a few maps that were never meant to be drawn.”
Harmon leaned forward slightly. “What about the vault’s protection? Keyed?”
Drake nodded. “There’s a statue of the city's mother in the gardens, high above the city on eastern cliffs. Beneath the dedication plaque at its base is a hidden panel. Press in, then slide left. You’ll feel a catch. Pull it. A lead capsule will drop. Inside is the master key.”
He locked eyes with Harmon across the magical distance.
“If the plaque’s been moved… then they know. And you’ll have to break in.”
He paused for a moment before continuing. “I ask you, please ensure this is done before the end of the feast, so I will have a clear heart when offering to God the Creator in the final ceremony tomorrow.”
King Edric Wyman said nothing for a moment, then nodded. “You have your orders. Move quickly.”
Harmon saluted. “We will.”
He turned sharply from the mirror and crossed the room, already shedding the stiffness of formality.
Without looking back, he said evenly, “Get me Riven and Nyssa.”
