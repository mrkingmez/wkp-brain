Editing Habits Quick Sheet
Read It Aloud
Your ear will catch clunky rhythm, repeated words, or dialogue that doesn’t sound human. Even better: use a text-to-speech app (different voice than yours). If the line makes you wince, it needs smoothing.
Do a Highlighter Pass
Print a chapter (or use Word’s highlight tool). Use one color for narration, one for dialogue, one for description, one for action. If one color dominates too long, the scene is unbalanced.
Reverse Read for Flow
Instead of reading in order, read backwards one chapter at a time (24 → 23 → 22…). Prevents being lulled by story flow and forces you to judge each scene alone.
The Last Line Test
Go to the last line of each scene/chapter. Ask: ‘Does this line shift something (emotion, tension, information)?’ If not, it needs tightening.
Index Card Scene Map
Write each scene on a card (POV, Location, Purpose, Ending Shift). Lay them out. If two scenes do the same job, cut one.
Filter Words Check
Search for: felt, saw, heard, realized, began to, seemed. Replace with direct action. Example: Weak: ‘He felt the wind bite.’ Stronger: ‘The wind bit his face.’
Dialogue-Only Read
Copy just the dialogue into a new doc. Read it straight through. Each voice should be distinct even without tags. If they blur, revise.
Trim the Front-Load
Often the first 1–3 sentences of a scene can be cut. Drop straight into the action. Readers don’t need the warm-up.
Find Your Tics
We all overuse words (just, still, dark, sharp, suddenly, breath, silence). Make a ‘kill list’ of yours and run a search purge near the end.
Time Yourself
Use Pomodoro (25 min sprint, 5 min break) or set a chapter goal (edit 2 chapters tonight). Keeps editing from feeling endless.
Journal in Character
Free-write a journal entry from a POV character about the last chapter’s events. Reveals emotional truths to weave back into dialogue and narration.
Beta Reader Trick Without Beta Readers
Change font and background color before rereading. Your brain will see it as new text and spot issues you missed.
