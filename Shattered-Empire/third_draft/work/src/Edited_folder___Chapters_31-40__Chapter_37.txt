It was near midday when Taerel finally stepped back from his leatherwork, blinking hard as the shapes of the runes bled in and out of focus across his vision. His eyes were dry. His fingers ached. He could still feel the delicate vibration of the scribing blade in his wrist, echoing up through tendons and into his shoulder like a ghost that hadn’t yet let go.

The bands lay in a neat row atop the canvas beside him, unpowered, but intact. Carefully looped, double-stitched, and etched with runes in the pattern he’d memorized from Luca’s bracelet. This method he’d learned from an unconscious memory, not from teaching.

The leather he used had come from the stolen merchant horse saddle bags, which he had liberated from the bandits a day before, and it was clean, well-tanned, with the fresh smell of oil and dye still lingering in the grain. It wasn’t ideal, but it was better than scraps. He’d reinforced each band by doubling it up and finding scraps of metal around the cavern to work as fasteners. 
That was the thing with scribing. You didn’t write from diagrams. You wrote from the pulse in your blood. The deeper the magic, the older the lineage, the louder the pulse.

He touched the final band with two fingers. The runes were crisp, precise… and inert.

They wouldn’t activate until he enchanted them. And for that, he needed a shard.

He exhaled through his nose, leaned forward, and held his forehead in his hand.

The camp was moving around him. That was the only word for it, moving. Not thriving, not struggling. Just… moving. People hauled crates from the far stores, checked rope stockpiles, and relit lamps in deeper corridors. They were scavenging all they could find around them. There were children here, hiding, mostly, but he saw one pass by with a piece of bread, mouth already working on the crust before her feet stopped.

Luca walked up from the lower corridor, wiping a stained cloth across his wrists. His sleeves were rolled, his hair tied back, and the acrid scent of boiled roots and rot clung to him like smoke.

“How’s it going?” he asked.

Taerel gestured to the bracers. “Ready. Structurally. Not enchanted.”

“Still no shard.”

“Nope.” He glanced at the runes. “They’ll hold the enchantment once I can transfer power from a shard to the runes. I built a slow-feed into the binding loops, won’t take much power to keep them running.”

“How many?”

“Three bands. One spare.” 

“Wish we had one for Lysana and Aymir.”

Taerel nodded. “Would’ve made things easier.”

Luca’s gaze flicked toward the entrance. “When are they due back?”

“No clue,” Taerel said. “Depends on whether the trail was clean. Depends on how far the caravan made it. Depends on whether they’re improvising. I guess it just depends.”

Luca said nothing. Then: "Yes. It does." 

Aymir stepped into the alcove, voice low. “Do you think he was right to ask us to find where they’re going?”

“Not my place to answer that, but I would like to know if there is a back door through the mountains. Also nice to know who is at my back when the killing starts.”

Aymir nodded once. Didn't look convinced. A distant whistle echoed down the corridor. Taerel and Luca stiffened at once.

Another shout followed, closer this time, from one of the outer scouts. “Recent movement!” a voice called. “Wheels. Cart-size.”

Taerel shot Luca a look. “We didn’t send a cart.” 

“No,” Luca said. “We didn’t.”

They reached the upper ridge just as the narrow switchback path trembled with clattering wheels. Dust plumed in the cave’s filtered light, and two figures rode alongside a loaded cart pulled by the same horses that had left with the soldiers three days ago.

Luca’s eyes narrowed. “Well, I’ll be damned.”

The soldiers waved. The cart creaked in. Ropes bound the top like a makeshift lid, and inside were barrels, bundled food, nails, hinges, tools, even a sack of worn linens tied off with a civic seal.

Taerel raised his voice. “Where the hell did all this come from?”

One soldier hopped down, breath short but grinning. “Ridge outpost. East of the old treeline. It was being used as a merchant drop. We knocked. Someone knocked back.”

The second soldier added, “Local farmers had stashed supplies there, scared, mostly. But they offered. Said better us than the Orcs.”

Luca pulled aside the tarp and saw rows of coiled chain, sealed water skins, and a satchel packed with tools that still smelled of the forge.

He blinked. “And they just gave this to you?”

“They’re sending more tonight,” the soldier added. “Few of them want to join when we hit the camp. They’re arming up now, leaving a skeleton crew behind to hold the outpost, just in case.”

Taerel opened a side crate. “No trouble?”

“None,” the soldier said. “Quiet all the way back.”

“No ogres?”

“No.”

Luca nodded slowly. “Good, that means you were followed, too.”

They hauled the supplies into the deeper alcove, where the cavern-turned-command post had been hidden from the world. Some of the people they’d rescued from the city helped unload, thin and quiet but willing. One woman cried when she saw the bread sacks. A child kept staring at a spool of thread like it might vanish if he blinked.

“I’ll take these to the runners,” Luca said, lifting a box of gears.

The soldiers followed, bringing what they could to the narrow crawlspace that connected their cavern to the inner fort. They’d widened it just enough to pass food, tools, and scrawled instructions to the engineers inside the broken gatehouse.

The fort’s eastern wall had jammed. The counterweight was split. Until it moved again, they couldn’t send anyone through. But now they had the parts. Now they had a chance.

Later, after the crates were stacked and the people fed, Taerel found himself pacing the upper ridge. The stone felt familiar now. He walked it daily. Watched the camp beyond.

It was always the same. Orcs moved in loops. Repeated actions. Fetch wood. Load catapult. Fire. Wait. Not once had they advanced. Not once had they approached the gate itself. They weren’t trying to breach the fort. They were trying to… maintain something.

Taerel narrowed his eyes. He watched the rhythm. The pattern. The calculated sameness of it all. He’d fought orcs before, feral ones, tribal ones, warbands from the far east. This wasn’t like them.

Then the thought struck him. This was staging. A performance, not an assault. But to what end? That was the question biting at the edge of his mind. He left the orcs to their pattern and went to find Luca.

Lysana and Aymir had been tracking the cart since before the sun had fully crested the horizon, its first glow bleeding slowly into the canyon like the embers of a dying forge. Early light stretched long and golden through the sparse clouds, tinting the distant stone a reddish hue, like old blood dried into granite. Wind whispered low through the ridges, tugging at cloaks and scattering sand like tiny scraping voices.

Aymir rode ahead, his rifle tied across his back, reins in one hand and a half-eaten apple in the other. The crispness of the morning had long since deepened into a biting chill, the kind that sank into their gloves and stiffened joints with every passing hour of the ride. Below them, the orc supply cart moved slowly along the basin trail, a worn path carved more by repetition than purpose. Dust kicked up around the mules' hooves, lingering in the air like smoke. The orc driver didn’t look back once.

They had kept to the high ground most of the way, pausing at ridges, crossing crests under cover of thin rock spires, staying low whenever the trail bent wide or narrowed too much for concealment. They hadn’t spoken much since they set out, relying instead on hand signals and shared glances. It was work, quiet, methodical, precise. Neither of them trusted easy words when hunting.

Now, hours in, they stopped again beneath the shadow of a slanted crag, both crouched and silent. The cart had disappeared.

Lysana knelt beside a boulder, fingertips brushing the trail’s edge. “Tracks go straight through,” she murmured, eyes narrowing, “and then… nothing.”

Aymir leaned beside her, scanning the terrain. “No wheel grooves. No dust trail. No fresh turns. Just stops.”

He shifted to his feet, squinting at the jagged ridgeline ahead. A narrow split in the stone yawned open like a half-closed mouth.

“Could be a sinkhole,” he said.

She shook her head. “Too clean. No collapse, no drag marks. The rock’s undisturbed, just… gone.”

They stared at the cleft.

Aymir’s fingers gripped the pommel of his blade. “Well. That’s unsettling.”

They led their horses down into the narrow gulch, tying them beneath a low overhang half-covered in bramble. The animals snorted and pawed the ground but didn’t resist.

“Stay,” Lysana whispered, patting her mare’s flank.

Then, as one, they turned to face the split in the rock.

The air spilling from the crack was cold and stale, like a tomb that hadn’t been opened in years. It carried a strange, almost metallic scent, a mix of iron and dust with something sour and earthy. The stone bore no tool marks. The edges were oddly smooth, but not from erosion. The shape was too... intentional.

“Looks more like something tunneled out,” Aymir muttered.

Lysana nodded. “Recently?”

He knelt, brushing at the ground. “No. The tracks are layered over dust. This tunnel’s old. Whatever dug it, it was here long before the Orcs started using it.”

He stood and drew a thin-bladed dagger. “Let’s find out where it goes.”

They moved slowly inside, boots soft on layered dust. The air grew colder the deeper they walked, and the walls changed, no longer simple rock, but shot through with veins of dark crystal and strange, hardened streaks that looked almost like fused bone. The tunnel widened unexpectedly, and a gust of wind whistled through from somewhere deeper, carrying a faint sound like whispering voices.

“Lovely,” Aymir muttered.

Lysana gave a tight grin. “Don’t worry. If it’s a ghost, I’ll let you run first.”

“Chivalry,” he said. “Dead and buried.”

They stopped to listen. Nothing ahead. Just the wind.

After a moment of quiet, Aymir broke the silence again. “So… what did you say to him?”

“To whom?” Lysana asked, puzzled.

“Luca.”

She raised an eyebrow.

He gave a small shrug. “You know, we have noticed. So what did you say to melt the assassin’s heart? Word is, he once cut off a man’s tongue just for finishing a job too loudly.”

She laughed softly. “You believe all the old stories?”

“I believe Adin, and he told me that one. Said Luca left a mark on the table afterward. A reminder to speak softly and don’t gloat.”

“Well,” she said, brushing dust from her sleeve, “maybe I didn’t melt anything. Maybe I just didn’t flinch.”

Aymir smirked. “You think that’s the trick? Not flinching?”

“Worked on you, didn’t it?”

He barked a laugh, quiet but genuine. “Yeah. But don’t tell Luca. I’ve got a reputation.”

They kept walking, the banter easing the tension between them as the tunnel curved again. The light from their torches danced wildly now, long shadows leaping ahead of them on walls shaped like ribs. The deeper they went, the more the stone resembled something biological. Not carved, not even dug. It felt… grown.

“Don’t say it,” Lysana whispered.

“I wasn’t going to,” Aymir replied.

“You were thinking it.”

“I was thinking,” he said slowly, “that I really don’t want to meet whatever creature called this home.”

She didn’t laugh this time.

Then, movement. A scraping sound. Shuffling claws on stone.

Both froze. A glimmer of something pale skittered into view, then ducked back into the dark.

Lysana dropped into a crouch, arrow already nocked.

Aymir scanned the shadows and listened. “Three… maybe four of them. Not orcs. Not human. Too small.”

They waited.

From the dark came a snarl and a chittering hiss.

A shape lunged, jagged limbs and glinting eyes. Lysana’s arrow thudded into its chest mid-leap, and it dropped with a screech. The others came fast, lean, feral things with sinewy torsos and insectile jaws. The air exploded into motion.

Aymir met the next with steel, blade slashing upward in a blur that caught it across the throat. Blood, not red, but black and smoking, splattered the wall. Lysana spun behind him, dropping two more with precise, fluid shots.

The last creature fled into the shadows, howling.

They panted, standing still, backs nearly touching.

“You good?” Aymir asked.

“It got my shoulder. Just a graze,” she muttered, wincing as she rubbed the welt.

“Not poisoned?”

“Wouldn’t be the first time.”

He glanced at her. “We should talk about that later. Sounds like a good story.”

Further in, the air thickened. The ceiling opened into a wide, hollow space, and below them stretched a vast under-chamber, lit faintly by glowing green fungus and streaks of mineral light. Dozens of Joblins moved about in rhythm, hauling crates, directing carts along wooden paths. The scale of the operation was stunning.

“They’ve turned this place into a damned supply hub,” Lysana breathed.

“They’ve been using this passage to funnel goods straight through the mountain,” Aymir said. “This isn’t just a supply line,” he muttered. “It’s a damn artery.”

As they backed away, movement near the far mouth caught their attention.

Another cart emerged, drawn by fresh mules.

“Another one?” Lysana whispered.

He cut his eyes toward her. “Is it coming our way?”
She was already moving. “Yes.”

They slipped out quickly, rising into the fading daylight just as the tunnel receded behind them.
They were halfway back to their horses when Lysana slowed, hand rising instinctively. Aymir followed her line of sight across the open terrain toward a bend in the lower ridgeline.

Dust. A plume rising against the horizon. He shaded his eyes. “Riders?”

“Eight,” she murmured, narrowing her gaze. “With a cart between them.”

They moved to the slope’s edge, hidden from above, but still with a clean view downhill. The cart and its escort weren’t approaching the tunnel; they were further off, traveling southward, hugging a trail that eventually curved toward the river-cut valley far below.

Lysana crouched low, scanning the riders one by one.

All wore armor, half-plate by the cut, each set matched and polished to a dull gleam. Not military regulation, but not bandit scrap either. Custom. Expensive. Purpose-built. Their horses were well-groomed, laden with rations and water skins. This wasn’t a raiding party. It looked more like an insertion team.

“What do you think?” Aymir asked.

“They’re not from Aldoria military, I can tell you that. Too polished. Too clean. And no insignias.”

“No supplies in the cart,” Aymir noted. “Which means either they’re bringing it in empty to pick something up, or… they already dropped it off.”

They both watched as the group rode steadily toward the distant haze that marked the riverlands.

Lysana frowned. “If they’re smugglers, they’re rich ones.”

“If they’re mercs, they’re far too comfortable.”

Aymir exhaled through his nose. “Whatever they are, they’re not part of any kingdom I’ve seen. You don’t ride like that unless you’re answering to someone powerful… or dangerous.”

Lysana hesitated, then nodded, making marks on a folded map. “We’ll mark the trail. It might be worth tracking later if Luca wants to know where they came from. But for now…”

“Let’s get back,” Aymir agreed, already turning toward the ridge.

They mounted swiftly, leaving the strange convoy behind as the sound of distant wheels and hoofbeats faded into the canyon wind.

Aymir rode for a while without speaking. Deep in thought over what they say, 'Eight riders. No marks. No banners. Heading south.' He turned it over in his mind. What was happening beyond this area? Was someone moving pieces quietly? And they were doing it from more than one direction. 

They knew they were getting closer when the air started to smell of food again. Smoke drifted upward in smooth ribbons from the controlled cook fires. Somewhere beneath the canopy of hanging tarps, children laughed for the first time in nine days. It was not joyous, not yet, but it was something close to safe. Safe was a miracle.

Civilians rotated in from the town, moving carefully along the hidden route. Many bore splints, wraps, or crutches; others carried bundles wrapped in cloth, dry bread, scavenged herbs, and the odd strip of jerky. Every time someone returned from the ruined city, a quiet tension gripped the camp. And every time they made it back, that grip lessened. It was like the cave itself was breathing again. Those who couldn't make the trip weighed on everyone, and every returning face got stopped for news. 

Taerel sat by the low ledge of the eastern drop, watching the orc camp through his rifle glass. Behind him, the hum of distant voices echoed softly, conversations floating in and out of earshot.

He didn’t glance up when Luca approached. He simply shifted slightly, giving the assassin space to crouch beside him.

“They move like machines,” Taerel said. “Every hour, the same rotations. The same pattern. Even their bombardments hit the same points. Like they don’t care if they break the wall… or maybe they’re not trying to.”

Luca narrowed his eyes, tracking the distant movement. “A diversion?”

Taerel shrugged with one shoulder, his bandaged arm stiff. “Could be. Or something worse. Something patient.”

“They’ve got a foothold on this side of the mountains,” Luca said. “They should be pushing, not sitting.”

“Unless they already have what they came for,” Taerel said.

“What do you think that could be?” Luca asked.

“It reads like patience. And something patient is waiting for a specific moment,” Taerel said, eyes still on the camp below.

“Agreed, then we keep watching until we see why,” Luca said. 

The words hung for a moment. A quiet passed between them. Then Luca exhaled, slow and steady.

Behind them, soldiers finished offloading the last of the supplies from the arriving team; the five from the outpost had finally joined the fight. They brought what they could carry on foot after hiding the cart below the second ridge: crates of field rations, spare arrows, coils of climbing rope, and a wrapped bundle containing four more sledge hammers. 

Luca and Taerel followed them into the cavern. A young boy darted past with a wooden spoon in one hand and a half-split heel of bread in the other. A woman called after him, laughing through the tightness in her voice.

“You see that?” Taerel muttered. “That’s the first time anyone’s run in days.”

“They’ve earned it,” Luca said.

“So have you.”

He didn’t reply.

Coming down almost on their heels were Lysana and Aymir returning.

Their cloaks were streaked with dust and wetness, hair wind-tossed, expressions sharpened by the hours on the trail. Their horses stood near the ramp, tied off and drinking from a shared pail. As they stepped closer, the chatter around the fire thinned.

For several minutes, they relayed their findings in clipped, low tones, each moment shared not as a report, but as a memory still raw on their boots. Their voices lowered slightly when they spoke of the tunnel’s shape, not natural, not carved, but something in between. Something made... by a creature long gone, or perhaps something that had simply left for now. The discovery that there wasn’t just one cart supplying the Orcs, but several. And a small mention of the strange company of eight mounted men, riding with discipline but no sigils, no banners. Not Aldorian military. Not marked at all.

When they finished, Luca nodded and turned away for a moment. He stepped into the shadows along the edge of the cavern and whispered across the bond, sending the information to Weslin. His voice carried only across the link.

When he returned, he didn’t speak at once.

Instead, he walked to the crate at the fire’s edge, pulled free four vials, each sealed, each distinct, and set them on the crate.

“Here’s what we’re doing.”

Everyone leaned in.

“The first,” he said, holding up a dull green glob about the size of a walnut, “is for the stew. It dissolves clean. No smell. Wait until the pot starts bubbling and drop it in fast. It’ll blend and start poisoning the broth in under a minute. Fast-acting. Ugly death.”

He tapped the second, a pale blue powder sealed in a paper twist. “This goes in the spice satchel. I will switch their mix for tonight’s meat, so when it gets seasoned, it gets this.”

Lysana raised a brow. “And they won’t notice?”

He grinned faintly. “It’s orc cuisine. I doubt they’ll notice anything short of a flaming arrow.”

Lysana rolled her eyes. A grunt of amusement came from Brehn. Someone muttered, “Charming.”

“And the third?” Aymir asked.

Luca picked up a dark vial filled with swirling red-gold liquid.

“Their ale,” he said. “They store it in a single keg, west side of the camp. Lightly guarded. I spike it, they drink, and they die in time for the main course.”

“And if something goes wrong?” Lysana asked quietly.

He picked up the fourth vial. “This is for arrows. The new men have bows. If any survive the first three, that handles it.” His eyes found Lysana. “And if the whole thing goes sideways, you ride. Everybody out. Burn the plan.”

She nodded once. “Fair.”

Aymir and Taerel exchanged glances. “We’ll cover him from the ridge,” Aymir said.

“Already picked my favorite angle,” Taerel added. “Straight through their firelight. If he goes down, they’ll lose three before they blink.”

One of the new soldiers, a dark-haired man named Brehn, stepped forward. “We’ll hold the fallback line leading down to their camp. Just say the word, and we’ll push or pull.”

Luca looked around at the team, the assassins, the scouts, the soldiers, and the civilians who’d somehow held a sliver of ground amid the ruins of a kingdom.

“Then let’s move.”

They ascended back to the overlook ridge. As they climbed, shadows lengthened, and the sky turned the color of dried blood and bruised slate.

From their vantage, the camp sprawled like a nest of embers. Fires stoked, shadows grew long. The Orcs were settling into their routine, the pattern they’d never once broken.

Luca adjusted his gloves, stepped out from behind the ridge, and melted into the darkness.

Taerel watched him go, rifle steady on the rock. “Hope he dances better than he flirts.”

“Barely,” Lysana muttered. She stepped up beside Aymir and smiled slightly. “But he means it.”

Below, the orc camp stirred. Fires roared higher. Sentries rotated. A stew pot boiled in the center of it all.

Chow time was coming.

And death… came with it.
