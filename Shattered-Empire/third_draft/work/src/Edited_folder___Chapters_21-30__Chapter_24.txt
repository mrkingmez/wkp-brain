The twenty-seventh Hall of Fowther Palace shimmered beneath a thousand lanterns, their golden light dancing over silk banners and arching stone. A string quartet played from the upper balconies, their instruments laced with subtle enchantments to carry every note with perfect clarity. Outside, the bells of the fifth night of the Accord Renewal rang out across the city.

It was the fifth day of the Ten-Day Accord Festival, an ancient tradition that celebrated the enduring peace between Opindale, Aldoria, and Moradaer. Lords, emissaries, and guardsmen from across the realms mingled in laughter and rivalry, unaware how fragile the night truly was.

“I hear your mines have slowed production,” said Lord Vaugre of Moradaer, swirling his cup. “Something about unstable rock beds?”

Governor Meridian Drake smiled. “Only unstable tongues, my lord, " he said, though the warmth didn't quite reach his eyes. “We’ve unearthed a vein of green iron that should make next year’s trade all the more profitable. Unless Moradaer prefers pearls to swords these days?”

“Pearls make excellent bribes,” Vaugre replied. “And sharper lords than you have been undone by them.”

King Edric watched the exchange with faint amusement. He leaned toward his wife, Queen Mirelle, and whispered, “The fifth night always brings out the peacocks.”

“Let them strut,” she murmured. “You’re still the lion.”

Musicians shifted to a lilting march, and a few of the younger lords and ladies began to dance. Gowns swirled, and laughter spiraled toward the high rafters.

Edric stood. His voice carried without magic, clear, strong, and commanding.

“Friends,” he began, his voice carrying without effort. “Tonight, we celebrate not just memory, but meaning. Since I was a boy, I’ve looked forward to the Accord Renewal. This table, these banners, they remind us of what is possible when kings and governors choose peace over conquest. I pledge, before gods and grain, that Opindale will honor this Accord until my final breath. And beyond.”

A round of “hear, hear” echoed, led by the High Duke of Moradaer and the Governor of Aldoria.

Applause swept through the chamber like wind over fields.

When it faded, Edric gestured to Chancellor Ayhmeri, Master of Arms Aldric Terance, Governor Drake, the High Duke of Moradaer Calven Thrymos, and Head Steward Colton. “Now, if my honored leaders and your aides would follow me, we have urgent matters to discuss.”

They moved into the Hall of the Griffin, a private chamber of dark woods, etched maps, and silver candelabras. The air inside was still, almost sacred.

Edric began, “You’ve all likely heard rumors of an attack at the Pass and another on Stormhaven. I’m here to confirm both of them. Coordinated. Deadly. And we’ve heard nothing since.”

There was a moment of shared alarm, glances traded, aides whispering.

“We’ve deployed a reconnaissance team,” Edric continued. “A small unit. Meant to insert and gather information. Before we do anything, we need to know what we are up against.”

“They’re scouts then?” asked the Calven Thrymos.

“A hybrid,” said Edric. “Two of Harmon’s rangers. The rest are the non-traditional type. Untrained together,” Edric said. “Which is its own risk. Yet Harmon handpicked them all.”

“Non-Traditional? We’re talking criminals?” the Moradaer aide asked with disdain.
“Harmon said talented,” Edric replied. 

“And their orders?” Ayhmeri asked.

“Reconnaissance only,” Edric answered. “They’re there to gather information. Observe, report. I have no intention of violating our newly signed treaty by launching military operations inside friendly lands.”

Chancellor Ayhmeri nodded. “Unorthodox, yes. But effective.”

“Because anyone bold enough to attack the pass is an enemy we must name,” Edric said. “Before more men die for guesses.”

High Duke Calven Thrymos started to ask a question when Head Steward Colton leaned to Governor Drake. “A word, my lord.” The scrape of Governor Drake’s chair broke the conversation when he stood. Edric was grateful for the brief respite. 

The two of them stepped into a stone corridor on the other side of the room. Colton’s face did not change until they were beyond the glow of the chamber doors. Even then, his voice stayed level, as if the corridor belonged to him as much as the hall did.

“We have them,” he said, voice level. “Your wife. Your children.”

Drake paled.

Colton continued, “You will do what is required before the final feast. Or their end will be... methodical. Painful. Clever. Dare I say a work of art?” He handed over a velvet pouch. Drake opened it by shaking hands. Inside was a bloodied lock of hair.

Colton smiled. “Hair grows back, dear governor. Body parts don’t. Please, don’t hesitate.” Colton lingered just long enough for the words to settle, then stepped away, leaving Drake with his thoughts.

Drake took one slow breath, then another, forcing his hands to still. When he turned back, the mask was already in place, even as his stomach tried to climb his throat. He stood there a moment longer than he should have. The door was just a door. The people behind it were just people. He told himself that until his hands stopped shaking, then went back inside. 

“What do you know of Lord Merrick?” Edric asked, eyes narrowing.

Drake took a moment, then he folded his arms behind his back. “Lord Merrick? He’s a minor lord,” Drake said lightly. “More ambition than pedigree. My council finds him more amusing than anything.”

“What do you know of his relic recovery jobs?”

Drake’s eyes darkened. “He calls himself a connoisseur of the art and what is old. Let him dig up relics. We’ll let him choke on dust.”

The others murmured approval, half-laughing at the bit. Chairs scraped; robes whispered as the circle broke apart. Most returned to the great hall, while Drake veered off toward a balcony.

The cold wind dragged at his sleeves as he stepped into the night, as one of his aides waited.

“They know about Merrick,” Drake whispered. “Edric and the rest.”

“Do they know what Merrick’s truly building?”

“No. And they won’t either. Not from me.”

The aide hesitated. “If he succeeds—”

“He’ll break the Accord in a heartbeat,” Drake muttered. “As he does, we all will have to suffer for it.”

Drake looked out into the night sky, staring into the distance as a man with the world on his shoulders. 

Back in the great hall, music swelled. A new tune began, string and flute twining together in an elegant waltz. The younger nobles returned to the dance floor. Wine flowed like a river through jeweled goblets, masking the unease beneath the music.

A merchant lord from Moradaer whispered to an Opindalian trade baron about new tariffs. A noblewoman from Aldoria hinted that her daughter might be betrothed to a minor cousin of the royal line. Deals danced as much as feet.

The king stood near the terrace arch, watching and listening quietly. Queen Mirelle moved beside him.

“If they get it wrong,” he said quietly, “we don't get to call it a misunderstanding. Not in a friendly country.” He watched a young lord spin his partner across the floor below. “Harmon believes they're the right people. Criminals, sellswords, and ghosts, the lot of them. ” A pause. “I just keep asking myself what happens if he's wrong. We are so close now.” 

“But Harmon believes in them?” she asked.

“Yes, and I believe in him,” Edric replied. “Gods help me, I do.”

She touched his arm, “Then so do I.”

They turned together toward the room. A servant moved discreetly toward Drake and handed him a folded note. Drake opened it under the cover of his sleeve. It began:

‘Before the final feast. Or the next piece will not be so small.’

Drake’s eyes teared up as he continued to read. He folded the parchment and slipped it into the inner pocket of his coat after reading the entire note, a quick wipe of the eyes. Hell, he thought, is not fire. It is a room full of people laughing while you burn. 

Ayhmeri caught Edric’s eye and gave a subtle nod. The two of them moved to a quiet corner, and Ayhmeri handed him a sealed scroll.

Edric opened it. “It’s not safe. Your life is not safe. Not as safe as you are made to believe. Signed a friend,” Edric said. “What do you make of this?” he asked, handing the note back to Ayhmeri.

“I have no clue, Sire. I will find out.” Knowing what his king was going to ask of him. 

They stood together, watching the feast glow with light and illusion. But behind the music and the laughter, war was coming. And only shadows could stop it.

The walls of Lord Merrick’s private sanctum trembled with the force of his rage.

A shattered goblet slammed against the mural of the Founding, splintering red wine across the mosaic of ancient kings. Another piece of pottery, a Syrellian ash dish, exploded against the hearthstones, its thousand-year-old glaze crumbling underfoot.

Merrick roared.

“Gone?! Vanished?! Do they think me a fool?”

The fresh fruit platter lay overturned across a velvet chaise, seeds bleeding into the fabric. The smell of citrus clung to the air, now soured by the scent of broken glaze, old parchment, and fury.

His breath came ragged. The veins along his neck bulged as he stalked from one ruined relic to another, one trembling hand trailing along the edge of a cracked obsidian plinth that once held a statue of the Old Flame, now toppled and in shards.

“I gave them everything, and they leave me with nothing!”

Merrick was tall, imposing even without armor, with the build of a man who once knew hunger and swore never to know it again. His long black coat, threadwoven with blood-red trim, fluttered as he turned. The chain across his chest bore relic tokens, false medals for wars he bought his way into.

“No one will drag me back into the gutters,” he hissed aloud to no one. “Not after I’ve climbed so far.”

A knock came at the side door, soft, rhythmic, and respectful.

Merrick didn’t answer. He simply stared, seething.

The door opened slowly, revealing a man in a dark marshal’s cloak. He stepped in, boots perfectly polished, hair tied back in strict Forgehallow regulation. He wore the sigil of the Forged, a serrated black star over a silver flame, emblazoned at his shoulder.

“Lord Merrick,” the man said, bowing low, fist to heart. “Commandant Jerric, reporting.”

Merrick turned sharply. “Speak.”

“They’re gone. No trace. The entire party vanished from the lower vaults and escaped into the outer wilds. Our men tried to block them but were cut off by fire from within.”

Merrick’s eyes twitched. “I know they escaped. What I want to know is how. Who helped them?”

Jerric stayed still, voice clipped and measured. “We’re investigating. From what we’ve gathered, they were seen frequenting the Iron Flask tavern the days prior. Our teams are currently interrogating the innkeeper and combing through every possession they left behind.”

“Do you think they left clues?” Merrick scoffed. “These are professionals,” Merrick snarled. “Thieves. Killers. I was one of them once. There will be nothing to find.”

Jerric inclined his head. “They were precise. They were clean. They took their wounded with them. There’s evidence of a rigged explosion to disrupt our second-level reinforcements. Fire damage on three floors. The Forged suffered five losses. We estimate a dozen, give or take,” Jerric said. “Too many sets of prints. Too many hands at the breaches.”

He turned back to the central table, now cleared save for a detailed map of the surrounding lands. His fingers hovered over the inked outline of the northern ridges.

“I know where they’re headed,” Merrick murmured. “They’re going after my vault... my treasure... the legacy I dug from beneath your precious crowns.”

He slammed his palm on the table, eyes wide.

“They've stolen my thief, my hammer, my scholar. A year of work. Gone. ”

Jerric took a slow breath. “Shall we dispatch the contingency?”

“Yes,” Merrick growled. “Send them into the eastern climb. Through the old survey route. I want them caught. Alive!”

He looked up slowly, voice now cold and exact.

“I want to make them scream.”

“Yes, my Lord.”

“And let it be known,” Merrick continued, turning with fire in his eyes, “Five hundred gold for verified information. A thousand gold for one delivered alive. Seven-fifty for a corpse, provided the head’s intact.”

He moved to a nearby shelf of scrolls, still trembling with rage. One he picked up, read, and tore down the center.

“I did not crawl from filth to be humiliated,” he whispered. “Not by… Not by...” He paused, voice low, thoughtful. “What was his name? Yes… an Emberlight lord from Estmere,” Merrick said slowly. “And Nyssa is tied to him as well.”

He crushed the parchment in his fist. “They will pay. All of them. I will get my pound.” He flung the scroll into the fire. “I will salt the sky before I let them take what is mine.”

Jerric bowed again. “We ride within the hour.”

Merrick did not answer. He simply stood before the burning scroll, eyes reflecting the flames.

The chamber crackled with firelight and broken glass. But beneath it all, something deeper stirred, the scent of power reclaimed, or perhaps the hunger that would never be filled.

The quiet of Queen Mirelle’s bedchamber was a far cry from the warmth and revelry of the Grand Hall. The soft crackle of the fire in the hearth battled gently against the muffled echoes of distant laughter and clinking glasses, now dulled by thick drapery and stone.

Mirelle stood near the mirror, her long braid half-loosened as her closest handmaid, Melianne, helped undo the delicate fastenings of her ceremonial gown. The queen’s back was bare now, pale under the firelight, the fabric slipping away with practiced care.

“You were radiant tonight, Your Grace,” Melianne said with a smile. “His Majesty couldn’t take his eyes off you. None of them could, really.”

Mirelle offered a small smile in return. “Let’s not flatter me too much, Mel. I may start believing it.”

The maid chuckled, folding the gown into a cedar-lined chest. “It’s true, though. It’s been years since I’ve seen a gathering like this. So many carriages today, we had to reroute them through the south lane. You should’ve seen it, felt like half the kingdom poured in. And even with all that, the guards barely checked the carts. Not the way they should have. Too many arrivals at once, and the captains were trying to keep the lanes clear so the gates did not choke with people.”

Mirelle turned toward her, now dressed in a flowing ivory nightrobe, the lace tracing her collarbone. “That doesn’t seem wise.”

“Oh, no,” Melianne said, quickly realizing the slip. “I mean, it’s the fifth day. Spirits are high, the gates are overworked... I’m sure it’s nothing. Just... so many people. It makes the kingdom feel proud. Whole.”

 Mirelle walked to the window, parting the curtains. The city glowed beyond the hill, the castle’s highest towers shimmering like crown points under the moon.

But the moment struck her oddly. Like breath held too long.

“Is your husband still on wall detail?” she asked without turning.

Melianne nodded. “Westgate. Long shifts, but he says the view’s worth it.”

The Queen said nothing for a long moment. Just watched. Then the dread crept in, not sudden, but in a slow, curling tide. She placed her palm flat against the glass.

From the shadows of a rooftop opposite the Royal Wing, a figure crouched low beneath the overhang of a tiled chimney. Dressed in dull leathers and soot-slicked cloth, the watcher kept perfectly still, a silhouette only to the stars.

He had been there since dusk. Listening. Measuring. Calculating.

A small polished lens hovered before his left eye, tethered to a silver monocle bracket by a thread-fine armature. It held steady as if pinned to the air. Through it, the Queen’s chamber was a blur of soft lamplight and golden movement, until the curtain parted.

There she was. He didn’t breathe, not for those five full seconds.

Then he slipped the lens aside and reached into the folds of his sleeve, withdrawing a thin cylinder no longer than a dagger hilt. He unfurled a narrow scroll inside, not to write, but to read.

It was names. Two crossed out. “Soon,” he whispered, restrained, almost breathless. His hand passed over the sigil stitched inside his coat. He tucked the scroll away, slid into the shadow, and vanished.

Behind him, a single glimmer of the Queen’s window still shone. The night wrapped around the castle like a cloak, not of comfort, but of patience.
