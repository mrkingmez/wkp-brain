The Broken Bolt sang with life. Even with the sun high over Opindale, the Broken Bolt roared like midnight, a perfect place to disappear. The festival crowds crammed the benches. Fiddles shrieked, boots stamped in time, mugs slammed down hard enough to froth ale over the rims. Dice clicked across a back table while half the room bellowed a song the other half didn’t know. Smoke from the hearth curled up into the beams and clung to the rafters, giving the lamplight a low golden haze.
Harmon stepped in, his cloak wrapped around his shoulders and a walking stick in hand, his gait measured and slow. To the eye, he was just another weary old traveler. To those who truly watched, his steel-gray eyes were anything but tired. He let the door fall shut behind him and gave the room a sweep that seemed casual, but marked every angle.
Serving girls threaded the crowd with the precision of a drill line, skirts flashing candlelight as they spun between tables. At the bar, a heavyset man bent over his plate like a starving wolf, elbows high and greedy. Lovers laughed too loudly in a corner booth. Two soldiers rattled dice, both cheating with the kind of clumsy confidence that only grows after six mugs of ale. Harmon watched it all pass, not for the charm of it but for the cracks beneath.
The knife was strapped to a boot under a table. The quick nod two men exchanged across the room before pretending not to see each other. The tavern was a chorus of laughter and clatter, but Harmon’s skin prickled. “Merriment this loud rarely comes without purpose,” he said to himself in a low tone.
Near the far wall, a boy sat perched on the edge of his chair. Weslin. His chin was lifted high, trying for manly, but his feet didn’t quite touch the floor. Two mugs waited untouched before him, beads of condensation sliding down the sides, and he guarded them with all the fierce loyalty of a page on his first errand.
Harmon almost smiled at the sight. Almost.
He moved deeper into the room, cloak brushing chair legs. It didn't take him long to make his way to the bar. Harmon slid into an empty seat at the bar, leaning his walking stick against the counter. He tried to catch the attention of a passing serving girl, but she was gone before he could raise a hand. Another slipped past with three mugs balanced on a tray, never looking his way. He gave a faint grunt and settled in, foot tapping despite himself to the fiddle’s quick tune.
From the corner of his eye, he noticed a man in a plain tunic rise from the table behind him. The man gave an irritated sigh and ducked behind the bar, vanishing into a small cove, cleverly set where the press of bodies and poor angles would have hidden it even from a careful eye. Harmon turned slightly in his seat, studying the angles he’d missed, then faced forward again just as the man returned and came to stand before him.
“What can I get you, sir?”
Harmon blinked. The man towered over most of the room, shoulders broad. Not a barkeep. The thought arrived before Harmon could stop it. He filed it and said nothing." Then keep the full second description intact. 
“I’ll take your best ale,” Harmon said.
“Short or full?”
“It is my night off,” Harmon replied dryly. “Make it a full.”
“That will be a copper.”
Harmon arched a brow. “That is all?”
The man shrugged once. “Yes! Should stop any complaints.” He turned to draw the drink, leaving Harmon to study the crowd again.
By the time Harmon looked back, another figure had taken the barkeep’s place. Short and stocky, he was not built for intimidation, but the way he carried himself demanded respect all the same. His face was weathered, the kind of face that seemed older than its years, each line etched by a lifetime of hard choices and harder living. He reached for a glass behind the bar, poured, and raised it to his lips in an easy motion, drinking while Harmon studied him in silence.
“What is one of the King’s polished gems doing in a pit like this?” the man asked, his Orin accent rough and heavy.
Harmon reached for his mug of ale. “Looking for you." Harmon took the ale, drank, and set it down with care. "Men like you don't stray far from what they know. Where else was I going to find a piece of dung like Adin?" 
They locked eyes across the bar, silence pressing tight between them. Adin’s grin split into a laugh that shook his shoulders, one hand slapping the counter, causing Harmon’s ale to jump in its glass. “By the gods! Time spared you nothing.”
“And you,” Harmon said, “still as ugly as the day I met you.”
Adin’s grin softened, though his eyes stayed sharp. “Your patrols picked up some of my lads. Are you here to drag me in?”
“If I meant to arrest you,” Harmon said flatly, “we wouldn’t be talking.”
The answer hung, sharp and final, until Adin laughed again, lower this time. “Fair. You have never softened.”
Harmon leaned back, gaze sweeping the bar, his meaning clear. “Not here. Let us talk where no one can overhear.”
Adin gave a small nod and signaled to the tall barkeep. “Luca,” he called, “bring us a table.”
As the man approached, Harmon’s eyes lingered on him longer this time, noting the quiet strength in his frame, the constant sweep of his gaze. 
Luca was already moving by the time Adin pushed off from the bar. The man did not weave through the crowd like a common server. He cut through it. Every step measured. His plain tunic did little to hide the muscle in his arms or the calluses of a man who had gripped more than mugs in his time. His eyes, dark and restless, never stayed still, sweeping the room, catching every glance, every twitch of a hand toward a pocket or a knife.
Harmon watched him approach and felt the hairs rise on the back of his neck. This was no barkeep. This was a man used to scanning battlefields. “Gentlemen,” Luca said, voice low and even, already turning. “This way.”
He led them across the tavern floor, threading them through laughter, music, and shouted wagers until the noise thinned. At the far end, he stopped at a booth half-swallowed in shadow. The candle there burned low, its small flame casting just enough light to see each other’s faces, but not enough for prying eyes.
Adin slid in with the easy confidence of a man who trusted the ground beneath him. Harmon took the seat opposite, cloak settling around him as he leaned his walking stick against the wall. Luca placed a fresh pitcher and two cups on the table, then stepped back without a word. He did not return to the bar. Instead, he stationed himself near a support beam, arms folded across his chest, eyes sweeping the room again, always watching.
Harmon’s gaze followed him, lingering. Soldier, his instincts whispered. Or something near enough. Harmon had seen men like that before, men who smiled with their hands full of mugs but always kept one eye on the door. The kind you wanted on your side, but never fully trusted.
Adin caught the look and smirked. “Now you see why I keep him.”
The two old friends sat across from each other, the noise of the tavern fading to a dull roar beyond the shadowed booth. Adin leaned back, his smirk shifting into something sharper. “Very well. We speak in earnest.”
For a moment, neither man spoke. They raised their mugs, knocked them together with the ease of long habit, and drank deep. The sound of the mugs clashing was sharp and familiar, an old joke between them that had outlived battles, oaths, and betrayals.
The moment their cups thudded back to the table, both men broke into laughter, the kind that came from memory as much as humor. For a heartbeat, they were not commander and outlaw, but younger men again, remembering simpler times. "It is so good to see you, old friend," said Harmon. Something crossed Adin's face that wasn't quite a smirk, there and gone. "Yes," he said simply. Then: "Harmon, still?" He tipped his cup once in acknowledgment. "It was always the truest one." 
“Yes, it's been too long. What is your name now, Harmon? Good to see you as well,” said Adin.
The laughter faded, leaving the candlelight and the weight of the years between them. Adin leaned forward, lowering his voice. “What’s with the boy? What’s his story?”
Harmon didn’t hesitate. “Weslin? He’s a page from the palace. A good lad. There is weight in him.” He gave Adin a knowing look.
Adin’s eyes narrowed. “Not another project, Harmon. Not like Arthur’s son. We both remember how that ended.”
Harmon’s jaw tightened. “That was another matter entirely. Arthur’s boy had promise, and I would make the same choice again. What happened to him was not due to a lack of talent. The world had other plans, he had other plans after—” He stopped himself, the words trailing off.
For a moment, Adin said nothing, his scar pulling tight as he studied Harmon’s face.
Harmon’s eyes drifted back toward the bar, where the tall barkeep was already wiping down mugs, his gaze never still. “So that is Luca,” he murmured, the words less discovery than confirmation of what his instincts had already marked. “Tell me about him.”
Adin followed his glance, lips twitching into the faintest smirk. "Let me put it this way. Every man who has come through that door, meaning harm, has left without finishing what he started. Some didn't leave at all." 
“Not a common tavern hand,” Harmon said, evenly. “If I’m to trust him, I’ll need more than that.”
Adin leaned forward, lowering his voice so only Harmon could hear. “Luca is no hand. He is a special case. I am his steward, in a way. His skill set… it is not one you can measure by coin or by training. It’s a gift,” Adin said quietly. “And a curse. One given from higher hands than ours.” If you ever see it, you will not see it again. Understand?”
The words carried weight beyond boasting, and Harmon felt it. His gaze flicked once more to the man behind the bar, then back to Adin. He gave a slow, deliberate nod. “I understand.”
Adin leaned back, letting the matter close. “Good. Then let's leave it there.” 
Adin refilled their cups, his voice dropping low. “All right, Harmon. You didn’t come to trade old memories. What’s gnawing at you?”
He drank deep, but when the mug came back down, his expression had shifted. The humor drained, replaced by something heavier that settled in the lines of his face. Adin saw it at once, and whatever jest he had ready died on his tongue. He leaned in, knowing the next words would matter.
Harmon leaned in, the candlelight sharpening his features. “Stormhaven has been struck. Aldoria reels, and the word is that the Veydrassil are behind it. Reports are scarce, but the damage was heavy.”
Adin’s hand stilled on the pitcher. “Aldoria?” He gave a bitter laugh. “Our closest ally burns, and the first I hear of it is from you?”
“It isn’t only them,” Harmon said. “The northern pass was attacked as well. Whether the garrison still holds or not, no one can say. And yet Opindale is already preparing to march in support. They may be walking straight into ruin, blind.” His eyes locked on Adin’s. “What do your people know?”
Adin’s smirk was gone. He set the pitcher down and spread his hands. “Nothing. Not a whisper. My eyes and ears, from the docks to the frontier, have given me nothing. That should not be possible. For something this large… silence is not natural. It’s enforced.”
Across the room, two men laughed so hard that one of them spilled his ale and didn't notice. The fiddle climbed high and bright. None of it touched the shadow where these two men sat. 
Silence stretched, broken only by the din of the tavern beyond their shadowed booth. Harmon sat with it, his fingers resting on the rim of his cup, weighing the void of answers.
At last, Harmon spoke. “I was hoping you had something. Now the urgency just grew.” Harmon's eyes went narrow.
Adin knew that look. He leaned forward. "The armies, Harmon. When do they march?"
"They don't. Not yet." The cup turned once in his hand. "We know nothing. And I will not march men into a grave for it."
“If I know you, there is a plan in that head of yours. So, let's hear it? There is never just one Plan,” said Adin. 
“I do indeed. You remember the idea. The one I would never let die.” He paused, looking for recognition in his friend's eyes. “A team that can go where armies cannot. Slip in, slip out, being the eyes a commander needs, before the banners march.” His voice cut through the gloom. “That is what we need, now. Not a host to draw eyes. That even the dullest eye would catch. Not a council to slow them down with debate. A handful to answer to me and me alone, who can work outside official means and take action when called.”
Adin huffed a short laugh, though there was no humor in it. “You are still chasing that idea? I thought it was tavern-talk, something we dreamed up over too much ale. Too long ago.” His eyes narrowed. “And now you mean to build it?”
Harmon’s reply was steady. “Now I mean to use it.”
The booth seemed smaller for the words, the shadows pressing close as though the tavern itself held its breath.
Adin leaned back, rubbing a thumb along the rim of his cup. “So that’s your answer, then. A handful of specialists instead of banners and drums.”
Harmon nodded once. “Armies march too slowly, and they draw too much attention. We need a group small enough to slip through cracks, sharp enough to survive what they find, and loyal enough to bring word back.”
Adin smirked faintly. “Loyalty’s a tall order. You know the kind I run with. They’ll come for coin, not crowns.”
“Then they’ll have it,” Harmon said. “If loyalty can be bought at all,” he said, his hand settling flat on the table.
Adin leaned back, considering. “You’ll need blades, quick hands, eyes sharp enough to read the dark, and someone who can patch a man back together after the rest goes wrong. That much I know. But who fills those roles?” He shook his head. “I will look into it. But I will not promise you results.”
Harmon glanced toward the bar, then back at Adin. “Can I have Luca?” Harmon said, eyes still on the man, the decision already settled behind them.
Adin’s scar twitched as he studied his old friend. After a pause, he gave a slow nod. “For you, my friend, I will consider it. That is what I can give you… for now.”
Harmon inclined his head. “That will be enough.”
Adin’s brow furrowed as he studied his old friend. “When?”
“Fourth bell. Tonight. Brackenstall Stables,” said Harmon.
Adin gave a low chuckle, though there was no mirth in it. “Tonight, then. I’ll bring what I already have within reach. No time to be choosy.”
The words hung in the candlelit dark, an agreement cut from silence and necessity.
Harmon watched Adin over the rim of his cup and allowed himself a small smile. “You will come through. You always do.”
Adin snorted, but his eyes warmed. “Do not start flattering me. It makes me nervous.”
The laughter between them faded, leaving only the flicker of the candle and the low murmur of the tavern beyond their booth. For a heartbeat, it almost felt like old times, but the silence that followed was heavier, as though both men knew the moment for jests had passed. Adin’s eyes lingered on Harmon, searching his face the way only an old friend could, and he let out a slow breath.
Adin broke the silence first. His voice was low, almost rough. “I cannot ride with you,” Adin said, eyes dropping to the table. “You know why.”
Harmon’s hand rested on the table, the faint glint of the ring catching the candlelight. Adin’s eyes flicked to it, then back up. They didn’t need words to understand.
“I know,” Harmon said quietly. “I know the price it would cost you. I know what you’ve gone through. That is why I will not ask it of you, old friend.”
Neither spoke. Somewhere past the edge of their booth, a fiddle hit a sour note, and the laughter spiked. Neither man looked up. Some silences don't need protecting from noise. They survive it. “God knows I would like to,” Adin leaned back, a heaviness in his tone. 
For a moment, the weight hung between them. Harmon broke it with a thin smile. “Besides, I could use your hands here while I’m gone. I will need you to get your spiders and birds moving,” Harmon said with a hint of authority behind it. "Whispers, Adin. Everything your people can find before we move. Send it the moment you have it." 
Adin gave the smallest of nods, the shadow of pain in his eyes replaced by grim acceptance. The glance they shared was brief but deep, a wordless bond forged from years of battles neither man could count.
For a heartbeat, Adin held his stare, then he gave a rough laugh that carried more sadness than humor. “Still know how to twist a man’s arm without touching him.”
“Truth does not bend,” Harmon said.
They sat in it a moment, the candle guttering, the tavern’s roar soft beyond the shadowed booth. Then Adin raised his cup. “To the work,” he said.
“To the work,” Harmon answered. They knocked cups, drank deep, and let the old ritual settle their nerves.
Harmon stood. “Weslin.”
The boy didn’t stir. Across the room, his eyes were fixed on the dance floor where the fiddlers played and serving girls spun in quick circles. He looked spellbound. The world beyond the music had vanished.
“Weslin,” Harmon called again, louder this time. A few heads turned, but the boy remained rooted, lost in the sight.
“Weslin!” The third call cut sharply through the tavern. Weslin jerked as though struck, his face flushing crimson as he realized half the room had seen him standing slack-jawed. He stumbled an apology to no one in particular and hurried toward Harmon’s booth, weaving past patrons and tables until he arrived, head bowed.
“Sorry, sir,” he said quickly, his face turning red. 
Adin’s brow arched, the scar above his eye catching the candlelight. “Easily distracted, this one.”
“He’s young,” Harmon said, though there was no real bite in his tone. “And he ran farther tonight than most grown men would dare.”
Adin gave Weslin a slow, appraising look. “Palace page, eh? You’ll have your hands full if you stick to his side. But maybe you’ve got the legs for it.”
Weslin straightened, the shame receding enough to speak. "I'll do my best, sir." He wasn't sure why, but the man's appraisal had felt like being weighed on something older and heavier than a palace steward's scale. 
Adin let out a rough chuckle. “It will have to suffice.” Adin leaned back, a smirk tugging at the scar on his face. “Where are you off to now?”
“I have to convince the boy’s father to let him come with me.”
Adin raised his voice in a mock-grand tone, drawing a glance or two. “That should not be so hard. You’re the Sword of the Realm. The man should be honored.”
“Well, he is the baker’s son,” Harmon answered dryly, giving Adin a knowing look.
Adin blinked, then laughed. “Not that baker’s son?”
“The same.”
“Ah, now I see.” Adin shook his head with a grin. “Well, good luck with that. Harmon, that is what you are going by now, yes?”
“Yes,” Harmon said, lifting his cup. “And thank you. Here’s to us, and the hell we raised when we were young… and again when we were not so young.”
Both men raised their cups. “Hear, hear.” They drained them in one pull, slamming the mugs down together in the old rhythm. Neither noticed Luca slip by to refill them mid-conversation, but the mugs were still heavy in their hands. Harmon fared the better, though Adin set his mug down with a wince that earned a grin from his friend.
Harmon rose, steady as ever, the ritual complete. “Fourth bell,” he said. “Tonight.” He left as fast as his words were spoken. 
Adin gave a slow nod. His eyes followed Harmon through the crowd, through the laughter and the dice and the stamping boots, until the door opened and the street took him. The tavern closed around the space he'd left, loud and indifferent. Adin reached for his cup. Set it down without drinking. Sat with the candle a moment longer.
