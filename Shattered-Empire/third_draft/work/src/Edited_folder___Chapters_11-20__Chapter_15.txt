“Luca’s found an orc raider camp.”
The room went still. Weslin’s voice carried the weight of it, low and unshaken, the faint glow of his bracelet reflecting against the shuttered window.
Aurelia sat watchful near the wall, Neavin leaned back in a chair still recovering from overlinking with his bonded, and Ash stood at the doorframe, winded but alert. Nyssa paced while chewing the inside of her cheek.
Harmon did not look up right away. Bent over the battered travel map spread across the small desk they’d dragged in from the hall, his eyes followed the ridgelines and river forks marked in ink. Behind him, the others leaned into the silence, the kind that came when soldiers heard what they already feared.
“Is he certain?” Harmon asked.
Weslin nodded once. “He is watching them now. Mobile camp. Not… big. Maybe four near the pen. Another enclosure, makeshift. Holding… humans.” 
Harmon’s head lifted. “Alive?”
Weslin’s voice thinned. “He believes some are.”
Nyssa’s tone broke the silence, dry and forced. “Please tell me someone didn’t devour all the bread. I scraped my skin raw on thorns and brush. I deserve one bite of something that does not taste like bark.”
“Bread is dry enough to be bark,” Ash replied. “Take it. Call it thanks for not making me run alone.”
“Do not either of you talk about moving again,” Neavin groaned, rubbing at his temple. “I just spent half a day riding a bird’s mind, keeping it from smashing into a cliff. My bonded did the flying, I just collected the headache.”
Aurelia smiled faintly, yet calmly said, “Impressive effort, Neavin. But unless your flight brought back something useful, I will not name it scouting.”
“Useful?” he muttered. “I learned birds are smarter than half this team.”
Ash barked a laugh. “That explains your attitude. You left part of your brain in the nest.”
“At least mine’s still working,” Neavin shot back. “Last I checked, lighting things up ain’t a trade.” 
Nyssa leaned on the table with a sharp smile, eyes locked on him.“Careful, Ash. He’s got a point. You spark at every argument you lose.”
“Better than stealing the cutlery every time someone blinks,” Ash said.
“It was one spoon,” Nyssa countered. 
The laughter faded, replaced by the low hiss of wind against the shutters. For a heartbeat, the weight of Weslin’s report hung there again, cold and heavy.
“Why does it even matter?” Neavin asked quietly. “They’re hundreds of miles away. We can’t help them from here.”
The silence that followed tightened.
Ash spoke first, voice lower. “It matters because someone’s out there bleeding for the same side. Even if we can’t reach them.”
“Or it matters because it could be us next,” Nyssa said. “We still don’t know what we’re walking into.”
“That’s comforting,” Ash muttered.
“It’s called honesty,” she shot back.
"The intelligence is clear," Aurelia said, her tone cutting through evenly. "Five orcs, a mobile camp, prisoners alive. That is what requires our attention." 
Nyssa folded her arms. “Organized orcs. That’s what worries me.”
“Orcs aren’t careful,” Neavin said, rubbing his temple again. “These were. Too quiet. Too deliberate.”
“And Luca’s charging in without a healer,” Ash added. “Brilliant.”
“Three days with this crew,” Nyssa muttered, “and I already don’t want any of them dead.”
“Sentimental,” Neavin said. “I’ll alert the historians.”
“You do that,” she replied. “I’ll make sure they spell your name wrong.”
Weslin’s voice again, steady. “He can confirm two corpses. Six more tied to trees. Hard to tell their state.”
“Bastards,” Nyssa said. “That camp’s not for raiding, it’s for silencing.”
Garr’s jaw tightened. “A choke point. Anyone fleeing the pass dies there.”
“Five orcs visible,” Weslin added. “Red cloth on their arms. Raid-mark, I think. Someone wants them recognized. Luca thinks there are more in the trees.”
“Tribal marking,” Harmon said quietly. “Raiders.”
Neavin gave a low whistle. “They’re sweeping west, forward trail, fallback route. That is not orc cunning, that’s command.”
“What’s Luca intend?” Harmon asked.
“Strike,” Weslin replied.
Harmon’s eyes narrowed.
“Three in high cover, rifles trained on the pens, too far out for them to spot us.” Weslin continued. “A bow on the far side. Luca drops from the slope to free the prisoners. Lysana moves to join, and others hold overwatch. If the orcs scatter, they’ll pick them off.”
“Timing?” Garr asked.	
“As soon as shadows cover the approach.”
Ash leaned forward. “Quick. Precise.”
“Too precise,” Harmon said, tone like a blade’s flat edge. “Tell him to take no unnecessary risks. Move with a steady footing. I leave my trust with you. ”
Weslin tapped his bracelet, the glow sharpening briefly. “Telling him now.”
“Make certain he obeys.” Harmon finally looked up, command radiating from his stillness. “This isn’t about glory. It’s about silence. That camp’s strangling to scream. If Luca can cut its throat, good, but no more than that.”
Weslin inclined his head and relayed the order through the shimmering link.
The door creaked open with the ease of someone entirely too confident, and Dain stepped through first, brushing ash from his shoulders as if it had personally offended him.

“Well, good news,” Dain announced, his voice just loud enough to cut through the murmur. “We’re not dead, the streets still reek of horse filth, and Brother Aldrek somehow refrained from killing a single city guard for breathing too loudly. I call that a win.”
Aldrek followed, silent and heavy as always, the steel cap of his mace glinting under his cloak.
“Only because the last one outran you,” Nyssa said from her chair, smirking.
Aldrek gave a sound somewhere between a grunt and a laugh.
“Tell that to his boot,” Dain shot back. “It’s still in the alley.”
He paused long enough to let the line land, cloak settling as the grin faded.
Harmon didn’t return the smile. “Report.”
Dain adjusted his gloves, drawing out the moment just enough to look deliberate, not stalling. “The Magistrate’s Tower is exactly the festering pit I was warned about. Two guards at the gate, half drunk, half stupid. Walked past them like fog. The inner hall smelled of rust and arrogance.”
“They denied Riven’s name,” he continued. “So I tried diplomacy. A bribe, an insult, and finally Lord Saewyn Olgrave’s name.”
Harmon blinked once. “You used that?”
“With all the grace it deserves,” Dain said. “Worked, too. They hauled me straight to the Warden. Polite bunch, if you overlook the smell and the fact that one of them had his helmet on backwards.”
Harmon gave him a look that killed the grin halfway.
“Right,” Dain said, tone shifting. “The front chamber’s large, arched ceiling, torch sconces every ten paces. Single entry, double exit. The one on the left leads deeper into the main corridor. Counted four side doors off it before I reached the office. I saw no guards posted inside that stretch. They trust the turns and the locks, no need for patrols.”
He started pacing, letting the rhythm of the memory guide him. “Each door’s numbered and iron-banded. Two stood ajar, two sealed. One had stains under it, old, dark. Probably an interrogation room. You can smell oil and lye from the cracks.”
“The Warden’s office sits near the rear of the same hall. Standard lockplate, heavier hinges, no external bar. Weapons case behind her desk, crossbow, short blades, a cudgel, and she’s close enough to the armory that you can hear hammer strikes through the wall. If a fight starts, she’ll have company in seconds.”
His tone slipped toward humor again. “Quite the decor. The desk, carved from ship timber, still smells like salt and arrogance. Keys hanging behind her like trophies. She sits high enough to make sure you’re looking up when she calls you insignificant.”
Harmon raised a brow.
Dain sighed, catching himself. “Apologies. Still shaking the noble out of my voice.”
“She denied everything. Said no Riven in custody, no record, no concern. Called me refreshingly blunt, which is noble-speak for stupid. I pushed, she didn’t blink. I mentioned my House, she called it property.”
The humor was gone now. “That’s when it turned. She stood, circled, looking for a crack. Told me I wore the coat like it was earned. I almost gave her a reason to test that theory.”
He drew a slow breath, jaw tightening. “Then she slipped by saying three days, then immediately tightened her jaw as she’d said too much. That’s the truth she didn’t mean to give.”
Dain straightened his gloves, voice steady again. “So, tactically: one main entrance, four side rooms, two choke points, and the Warden within shouting distance of an armory. If we go in, it’ll have to be fast and loud.”
He looked up, a flicker of wryness returning. “And for what it’s worth, she’ll remember me. It's hard to forget someone you wanted to strangle.”
Brother Aldrek then spoke up, "He must have done something to spook her because of what happened next."
“Were you at the door the whole time?” Harmon asked at last, his voice low but firm.
 Aldrek gave a single nod. “As ordered. Watched the street. Waited.”
 Harmon gestured subtly with the cup in his hand. “Keep going.”
“Two came out,” Aldrek said. “Quiet as a breath. The first one stepped into the light like a shadow peeling from the wall, tall, lean, built like a man who lives on the road. His face… narrow. Tired. Lines at the corners of his mouth, but not the kind made by laughter.”
He spoke plainly, but his words painted the memory in sharp detail.
“Had a patchy beard. Just enough to forget. Clothes were wrong, looked like soot-stained merchant garb, but the wear was off. Too clean at the cuffs, too new at the collar for the road. That one’s seen training. Moved like he was always mapping exits.”
Harmon’s expression didn’t change, but his knuckles whitened just slightly around the stem of his cup.
“The second one was broader. Shorter,” Aldrek went on. “Lit a pipe like he didn’t have a care in the world. Fingers stained with ink, maybe a scribe, or someone posing as one. Carried a bit of weight, but not enough to slow him. His cloak dragged low, hung strangely over one side like he was hiding steel. He smiled too easily, but his eyes weren’t with the grin. Alert. Dry. Measured every child that passed. Had a hand that dropped too naturally to his belt.”
 “They split?” Harmon asked.
“Aye,” Aldrek said. “Sent word through the link. One headed toward the Noble District, blue cloak, trying not to look like he was rushing. The other took after Dain, wasn’t subtle about it. Gray duster, working boots. The kind that says ‘I’m no one’ just a little too loudly.”
 He paused, eyes narrowing faintly at the firelight.
“Dain led him a ways. Down through Forgehallow’s broken veins. Into an alley behind a dead forge. I followed in from the rear.”
 Harmon looked up from the fire. “Confrontation?”
“Not quite. Dain called him out. Calm. Professional. I stepped in behind. Drew halfway, told him to turn.”

“He said he wanted to speak,” Aldrek replied with a steady, even voice, giving Dain the side eye. “Knew this might be his only chance. He overheard Dain’s conversation with the warden. Thought a noble could help the others. He told us there are at least three others inside the prison, these off-book prisoners. He odd movements of the Prisoners. He also said he overheard guards bragging about the coin. Orders from somewhere higher.”
 Harmon let out a long, steady breath, eyes fixed on the embers. “And you believed him.”
Aldrek didn’t answer immediately. Then: “I did. Man was shaking, but not with fear. With relief. He thought he’d believed he made it to someone who might do something.”
“So we learn nothing other than the one we are searching for is in prison, but for some reason, as a special prisoner, but why?” asked Harmon, a bit frustrated.
Nyssa’s voice came low from where she sat, still pulling the last of the soot from her gloves. “I can answer that.”
The others turned, and for a beat, she didn’t meet their eyes, just stared into the fire, jaw tight.
“We tailed our mark through the merchant district into the Ironcrest Quarter. High walls, manor gates, guards with polished greaves who haven’t seen a real fight in years.” She finally looked up. “He wasn’t hiding. He wanted to be seen. Like bait on a hook.”
Ash gave a subtle nod beside her, letting her speak.
“We followed at a distance. Through the alleys, past bakeries with gold-leaf windows. Neavin had the skies. His falcon tracked from above. Then we saw where the man went. A side gate. Private entrance.” Her lips pulled into something between a grimace and a grin. “And of course, it was that estate.”
She looked directly at Harmon now. “Lord Iven Merrick. Twin-headed hammer wrapped in ivy. He always did have a flair for presentation.”
There was a pause. Nyssa leaned back slightly, crossing one leg over the other.
“You know him,” Harmon said. “Tell us how.” 
“ Merrick’s not just a noble. He’s a patron of the arts. A funder of museums. A sponsor of trade expeditions. He smiles at marble busts and speaks in soft diplomatic tones. But what is he really?” Her tone darkened. “A collector. Of rare things. Dangerous ones. A beat of silence followed

Nyssa’s words hung in the air like the lingering scent of burnt copper, unmistakable and hard to forget. The others had listened closely, eyes low or half-lidded from exhaustion, but all alert in the way people were when truths felt sharper than weapons.
 After a beat of silence, Harmon pushed himself off the table he’d been leaning on. His shoulders rolled, joints giving a quiet protest. He looked over the group with the same practiced calm he wore in the field, though now there was something more fatherly in it, a measure of earned command softened by concern.
 “All right,” Harmon said, voice carrying across the stone and wood of the old room. “That’s more than enough for tonight. We will move fast in the morning, and I need you all sharp, not half-dead from hunger and worn to threads.”
He glanced toward the door. “There’s a tab under my name downstairs in the common room. Use it. Eat something that didn’t come from a dried pack or out of a ration box. You’ve earned it. Yet stay vigilant, and mouths closed.”
A low, grateful groan came from Weslin, who was already halfway out of his chair. “That’s the best thing anyone’s said to me in three days.”
Ash gave a dry chuckle. “I second that. If I see another steamed root or prison biscuit, I might burn the table out of spite.”
“You’ll eat, then change,” Harmon said, leveling a look across the table. “And rest. I mean real rest. No pacing half the night, no tinkering with gear, and no brooding in dark corners pretending you’re writing poetry.”
Nyssa raised her eyebrows at that. “Was that last one aimed at me or Dain?”
“Both,” Harmon replied without missing a beat.
There was scattered laughter, worn down, yes, but real, and it loosened something tight in the room.
Harmon turned toward Aurelia next, giving her a small nod. “Cut the link, Lia. Let everyone breathe their own air for a bit.”
Aurelia was already gently pulling her shard pendant from where it nestled against her collarbone. She held it between her palms, closing her eyes. A faint shimmer passed over her features like moonlight on water, and then the link unraveled, thread by invisible thread, until the soft mental hum that bound them all quieted into silence.
“Done,” she murmured, slipping the shard back into the folds of her robes. “Though I’ll miss the chorus of your thoughts. So... poetic.”
 “Mine are mostly complaints and mental swearing,” Ash said, rising. “You’re not missing much.”
 As the others began to shuffle out, boots scuffing tiredly across the floor, Dain lingered. He hadn’t moved to follow yet. His hand came out gently, catching Nyssa’s forearm just before she passed.
“Hey,” he said, voice low, touched with something rare in it—honesty stripped of defense. “Back there... what you said. In my head. Thank you.”
Nyssa blinked, caught off guard for the barest second before that familiar crooked smirk tugged at her lips. “You’re welcome, your lordship,” she said, dragging out the title like a teasing whip. “Don’t get used to me being nice, though. I’ve got a reputation.”
Dain gave a small, surprised laugh, the kind that came unguarded, like breath stolen from a storm. “Wouldn’t dream of it.”
She studied him for a second, that tilt of the head she used when deciding if she liked someone or wanted to rob them blind. “You look better in silk, by the way,” she said. “Almost makes you look important. Until you talk.”
And with that, she vanished toward the stairs, steps light despite the exhaustion dragging at them all.
Dain stood there for a beat, watching her go, then looked down at the clothes he wore, dark, elegant travel gear more suited for a nobleman than a prisoner. His fingers traced the edge of the fabric, slowly. The weight of it sat heavily in his palm. Tailored lines. Gold-threaded buttons. The kind of thing he used to wear before things broke apart.
 “It feels good,” he admitted quietly to Harmon, who stood nearby, pretending not to eavesdrop. “Playing the noble again. For a minute, it almost feels like I haven’t lost everything.”
Harmon nodded, his gaze distant. “Clothes don’t lie, Dain. But they don’t tell the whole truth, either.”
“No,” Dain said, softer now. “They don’t.”
Harmon’s voice shifted, this time raised so the last stragglers could hear. “Let’s go. Food, then gear. If it squeaks or smells like rust, fix it or clean it. We move at full speed tomorrow. No dragging behind.”
A tired “Aye, sir,” came from Ash.
“By the Light, I’ll sleep through breakfast,” Weslin muttered.
“You always sleep through breakfast,” Aurelia added, not looking up.
“I regret nothing,” Weslin said, walking backwards toward the door.
Harmon chuckled and turned his head toward the corner where Weslin was just about to leave. “Weslin, hold up a moment.”
The young man paused, brows lifting.
Harmon motioned him over with a quiet seriousness. “A couple of things I want to go over with you. We will just be a second.”
Weslin gave a small nod, stepping closer, and the door to the quiet war-room finally swung wide, letting the smell of roasting meat and warm ale drift in from downstairs.
One by one, they left. The heavy wooden door eased shut behind the last of them with a muted clunk, and silence settled again across the war room. Only the flickering hearthlight remained, casting long shadows across the table.
Harmon stood there for a moment, arms crossed, jaw working slightly as he looked at the table’s spread of maps and notes. Harmon turned his gaze toward Weslin, who had hovered by the edge of the doorway like he wasn’t quite sure if he was dismissed or still needed.
“Walk with me a second?” Harmon asked, voice quieter now, gentler.
Weslin perked up, adjusting the strap of his satchel. “Of course, sir.” 
But Harmon didn’t move toward the door. Instead, he stepped back toward the room, gesturing for Weslin to follow. “Just needed a word before we head out.”
Weslin’s boots thudded lightly on the stone as he stepped closer, standing a pace away like a squire might before a knight’s review.
“Any word from Luca?” Harmon asked, the question coming out low, laced with something soft and tight beneath it.
Weslin’s face shifted, just a flicker, but he shook his head. “Not yet, sir. No final report. But it’s only been about an hour. I’m sure it’s coming.”
Harmon nodded slowly. “I know. Just… hard not to worry.”
“You know his name,” Weslin said, steadier now. “He is the Shadow Walker. There’s no cleaner blade in the dark. If there was trouble, he handled it. And if he couldn’t—”
“Then none of us could’ve,” Harmon finished, the ghost of a wry smile tugging at his mouth. “I’ve only ever heard the stories. The order he came from, half the tales sound like myths, but every one of them ends with someone dead and a crown scrambling for answers.”
“They say he once walked into a sealed court and walked out with the Duke’s thumb,” Weslin said quietly. 
“That one, I have heard.” Harmon looked away, a crease forming between his brow. “But stories or not, he’s with us now. He is part of this. He is in my charge. Do you understand?”
Weslin offered a faint grin and nodded his head. “He’ll send word. He’s fine. We will all be fine.” He said it not only to reassure Harmon, but to convince himself as well. He could see it on him, the particular stillness of someone managing something they would not name out loud. He said nothing. Some things did not need naming. 
Harmon looked at him for a moment, really looked, and nodded. Harmon took a slow breath, “This is why I made you my squire,” he said, voice settling into something firm but kind. “You’ve got heart. More than most men I’ve fought beside.”
Weslin’s cheeks colored, and he looked down, one boot toe scuffing slightly at the stone floor. “Thank you, sir. I’ll keep earning that.”
“I know you will,” Harmon said. There was a pause then, long enough for the quiet of the room to press back in. The fire crackled softly behind them.
“So,” Harmon said, easing into the next line like he already knew the answer. “What did you pick up today, working around the command center?”
Weslin brightened, happy for the subject shift. “Lots of messenger traffic. I helped move supplies as well.”
Harmon gave a low nod, “And Aurelia?” he asked, a little quieter now, leaning on the edge of the table as he turned toward him fully. “How’s the training with her going?”
Weslin stood a little taller. “It’s… different. Hard in a different way. She had me practice two things today. First was a stillness drill. You sit, not moving, not thinking, just letting your mind go quiet. Not easy.”
Harmon smiled faintly. “No, it isn’t.”
“And the second was a focus exercise. She gave me this pendant to hang in front of a candle flame and told me to hold every detail of it in my mind. Every crack, every reflection, without blinking. Felt like it was peeling my brain apart by the end.”
“That’s the point,” Harmon said. “Stillness to clear the mind. Focus to sharpen it. Do both tonight before bed. Half an hour each.”
Weslin nodded seriously. “Yes, sir.”
“And again, first thing in the morning. Once we’re back on the road, you’ll start proper training with me. Weapons, field discipline. You’ll keep studying under Aurelia, too. So don’t expect much free time.”
Weslin gave a tired grin. “Not exactly what I’d call relaxing, but I didn’t sign up to be a noble’s cupbearer.”
“No,” Harmon said, clapping a hand on his shoulder. “You didn’t. You signed up to serve something better. Now, run along. Eat while the food’s still warm. I don’t want to see you again tonight unless Luca messages.”
Weslin gave a sharp nod, almost a salute, and turned for the door. “Yes, sir.”
The door creaked open and shut behind him, his steps already fading down the hall.
For a while, Harmon stayed where he was.
The warmth of the room seemed dimmer without the voices in it. The maps on the table flickered in firelight, edges curled and ink smudged by use. He reached out, drawing one of them toward him with a calloused hand—the one from the northern border, where the Veydrassil had last moved in shadow.
 He stared at it for a long time.
 The faces of his team passed in his thoughts like ghosts. Dain, carrying grief beneath silk and bravado. Nyssa, sharp as a dagger and twice as fast. Aurelia, always watching, always calculating what she could and couldn’t heal. Ceadric, burning from the inside out. And Weslin, young, yes, but solid. A spark of something that might one day be a flame.
He exhaled through his nose and pressed a palm flat to the map. The report would come. He could wait. For now, there was only the quiet. The fire. The weight of the map under his hand. And somewhere in the back of his mind, still warm, the thought of a boy who believed in dangerous men and was working hard not to become one.
