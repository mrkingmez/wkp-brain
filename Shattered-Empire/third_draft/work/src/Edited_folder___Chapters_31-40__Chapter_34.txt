Stormhaven held its breath. The once-proud capital of Aldoria, carved into the bones of the Blackspine Range, now stood draped in quiet tension. Its slate rooftops shimmered under a gray morning sky, dew still clinging to eaves and cobbled streets as if the city refused to exhale. The wind wound through alleys like a whisper too frightened to speak aloud. The towering walls, once the envy of every kingdom, stood silent, not in defense, but in watchfulness.

Near the city’s heart, jagged rubble and scorched iron marked the ruins of the Governor’s statue in Ironmarch Plaza, the wound no one dared name aloud.

And still, the bells rang. Bakers sold bread. Children played. Laughter echoed too sharply. Nothing had stopped, everything had just… shifted.

Dain and Nyssa walked side by side beneath stone archways and weather-worn sigils, arm in arm like nobles with too much coin and too few worries. His emerald coat gleamed with just the right amount of arrogance, and her silver jewelry caught the morning light like teeth behind a smile.

She tilted her head, watching the crowd watch them. "Half of these people think we're inspecting stables. The other half thinks we're lovers." An old woman's eyes had not left her since they turned onto the street. Her fingers tightened briefly on his arm. 

Dain didn’t miss a beat. “And what, in your estimation, are we?”

“Professionals.” She glanced at him sidelong. “Though you are doing an awful lot of fidgeting for someone who grew up around ballrooms.”

He adjusted his cuffs with mock offense. “I’m not fidgeting. I’m brooding. It’s fashionable.”

“You’re glancing at my neckline,” Nyssa said. 

“I’m keeping watch for knives,” Dain said.

A patrol rounded the corner, too slow, too alert. Every smile on the street froze for half a breath before laughter resumed.

She gave a quiet snort, then reached up and adjusted one of her silver hair combs, an idle touch that, Dain noted, also subtly reset her field of vision.

“You’re sharp today,” he murmured.

“I’m always sharp. You just forget when I’m not throwing things.”

He leaned closer. “So what is it with you and high society? You sound like someone who’s sat through too many dull dinners.”

Nyssa’s smile flickered, just for a second. “My father hosted auctions, not feasts. But if you want a room full of warhorses and fat merchants to eat from your palm, you learn how to use the right fork, and how to stab with it if the deal sours.”

Dain blinked. Then grinned. “Remind me never to take you to a wedding.”

“Too late,” she said, gesturing to the boutique up ahead. “We’re practically engaged.”

The stone-front shop loomed before them now, all delicate carvings, ornamental lanterns, and windows with thick curtains drawn tightly closed. Two guards in polished black cuirasses stood like statues beside the entrance, eyes sliding over Dain and Nyssa in quiet evaluation.

The smell of lilac oil and scorched metal hung heavy in the air as Dain and Nyssa ascended a marbled avenue flanked by wrought-iron fences and spindled lamps. The cobblestones here were smoother, worn by generations of noble boots and polished carriage wheels. Ornate doors closed silently as they passed. The air was too still. Too watching.

“This district’s too clean,” Nyssa muttered behind a smile as they passed a pair of stone-faced guards in crimson sashes. “Almost like it’s trying to convince itself nothing’s wrong.”

Dain didn’t glance at her. “Which is exactly what people do before a funeral.”

They crossed into a terrace lane overlooking a manicured garden, where a trio of cloaked nobles stood near a fountain, murmuring with cups of steaming leafwater. Dain touched Nyssa’s elbow and veered subtly, guiding them toward a boutique draped in sheer blue curtains and amber glass.

Inside, the scent shifted to lavender, rich cloth, and a hint of something chemical underneath. Gold-threaded tunics and formal gowns hung on suspended rails. A thin woman with ink-black hair and a quill tucked behind one ear looked up from a ledger.

Nyssa leaned in one last time before they crossed the threshold. “You sure you’re ready to play suitor to a third daughter with a dowry of mares and attitude?”

Dain offered his arm like a gentleman. “For you, my lady, I’d brave the entire merchant guild council.”

“Good,” she said, taking his arm. “Because after this, I might need you to carry me. These shoes are an unusual torture.”

And just like that, they stepped through the doors, all silk, poise, and perfectly rehearsed lies.

“Welcome to Celairyn Silks,” she said, bowing without warmth.

Dain returned the gesture with just enough poise to imply old money without old arrogance. “Lord Theren Vayle, and this is my companion, Lady Liora Quell of Fairmere.”

Nyssa’s curtsy was deep and devastatingly elegant. “A pleasure. I’m here to ensure he doesn’t bankrupt his estate chasing silk.”

“Only chasing quality,” Dain said, letting his eyes travel the boutique before landing on her again. “Though I do tend to overpay for rare finds.”

Madrelis offered a small, sharp smile and gestured for them to come inside. “You’ve come on a good day, my lord. Shipment from Eastern looms arrived this morning. Rich fabrics, resilient dyes, perfect for acquisitions.”

Nyssa touched a bolt of deep burgundy velvet. “We were told this was where the discerning come for more than fashion.”

“Whoever told you that should consider a career in sales,” Madrelis replied smoothly.

“I’ve not seen you before, my lord. What business brings you?” Madrelis asked.

“Horses, I just bought that old stable by the blacksmith shop, looking to train horses here,” Dain replied with an air of pride.

They moved deeper into the shop, where delicate silver bells hung in the corners. Another figure stood examining a rack of ceremonial coats, an older man, lean and balding, with a noble’s polished bearing. He turned slightly at the sound of Dain’s voice, eyes narrowing with interest.

“Lord Vayle?” he said, stepping closer. “I don’t believe we’ve met.”

Dain offered a nod. “We’ve only just arrived.”

“Just arrived and already found Celairyn? Impressive. Most newcomers take weeks to be seen here.”

Nyssa smiled. “He has me. I find things.”

“She does,” Dain said. 

The noble chuckled, offering a hand. “Lord Halvayne. May I ask what brings you to Stormhaven?”

Before Dain could answer, Madrelis stepped in with uncanny timing. “Lord Vayle recently acquired the old training stable. With the treaty signed, there’s talk that the war games may resume. He’s looking to field a new generation of champions.” The detail wasn't for them. Dain noted it the way he noted a blade angled at someone else's throat. 

Dain nodded. “I believe there’s no better time to invest in the future. And no better place to start.”

Nyssa added brightly, “And no sharper eye than mine for picking the mares to do it.”

“Indeed, it's been far too long without the games,” Halvayne said, studying her with amused interest. “And your family?”

“My father runs one of the largest horse trading networks east of the Selenmar fields,” Nyssa replied, tone honeyed. “He's hoping this venture becomes more than just trade." She flashed a rueful smile. 

“Oh?” Halvayne’s eyes flicked between them. “A merger of sorts?”

“Something like that,” Dain said with a practiced grin. “I’m evaluating….” He turned and looked at her. “The offer.”

Nyssa gave him a warning smile. “You’ll find I come with few flaws. Yet many assets.”

Madrelis made a quiet sound, something between a cough and a stifled laugh, but her eyes flicked toward the door before she gestured toward the back. “Perhaps you’d be interested in viewing the private collection?”

“Please,” Nyssa said, hooking her arm through Dain’s.

They followed her through beaded curtains into a smaller room of tailored pieces. Madrelis glanced at the other customer over her shoulder and said, low, “There’s a lot of noise these days. People don’t speak plainly anymore. Not unless they know your cut.”

“We’re good listeners,” Dain replied, eyes watching the room.

Madrelis hesitated. “There’s a name. Korr. Just a whisper, really. But people seem to know it… even when they don’t. If you hear it, best to nod and say nothing. It scares me to be honest, but don’t know why.”

Nyssa frowned faintly. “Korr.”

Madrelis nodded, then froze as Halvayne’s voice drifted from the front. “Careful what you share with strangers, Madrelis. Gossip’s dangerous currency.”

She stepped back with a bow. “Of course, my lord.”

Halvayne reappeared in the doorway, smiling. “Still, I have a sense for people. You two strike me as ambitious, the sort who would appreciate a gathering where there are plenty of ears and fewer mouths, with deep pockets. This may be abrupt, but there is a tea later today. Third bell. You should find an ear you could catch.” His glare at Madrelis could have burned the entire shop down.

“Tea sounds delightful,” Dain said.

Halvayne chuckled. “You’ll need this.” He handed them a silk-wrapped token. “Don’t be late.”

Once he’d left, Nyssa whispered, “That was fast.”

“Too fast,” Dain muttered. “Like they wanted us there.”

She adjusted his collar again, then smoothed his sleeve with an overacted fussiness. “Well, Daddy does want me married off. Maybe he’s just trying to help.”

Dain arched a brow. “So now I’m the prize in this little masquerade?”

She leaned close. “You bought a stable, didn’t you? In this city, that makes you noble and rich.”

“Is that all it takes?”

“That and cheekbones sharp enough to mount a saddle on.”

Dain chuckled, but there was tension under it. “They’re watching, Nys.”

“I know,” she said. “And they’ll be watching more at the tea.”

He nodded once. “Then we give them something to see.”

Nyssa smirked. “Let’s go stir the rest of the city before the third bell.”

They were two blocks down, the crowd thinning, when a figure pulled himself out of a doorway that should have been empty. He was short, he was wearing what could be believed to be called clothing, but it was his eyes, they were cloudy, white right over the iris. That is when they noticed he was using a cane to find his way.

This blind man pulled Nyssa aside as she and Dain made their way down the street.

“You think you see clearly,” he rasped. “But they’re already gone.”

Dain frowned. “Who?”

“The nobles. Gone… Yet still walk the streets. I know because I hear them. Seen enough faces in my time to know when a voice doesn't match one. And those voices… don't match.”

Nyssa narrowed her eyes. “You expect us to believe that?”

He laughed. “Believe… Yes, that is a funny word, believe in a world of lies and illusion. Believe what you must. But remember this, those who think they can see are the ones who are truly blind, if you don’t wake up soon. You’ll never wake up at all.”

He vanished into the alley before they could press further.

“That was strange, yet it doesn’t feel random,” Dain said quietly. His hand brushed his coat where his knife rested, eyes sweeping the nearby doorways.

“From what we have seen so far, that sounds about right. How much stranger can this place get?” Nyssa replied. They started making their way down the street again, arm in arm, both taking turns looking over their shoulders to see if he would return. 

Ash and Riven stepped into the tangled mess of alleys known as Smokeslip, where the taverns had no names, and the doors had more locks on the inside. The smoke here didn’t rise. It clung, fat and gray in the alleyways, curling low to the ground as it knew better than to draw attention. 
Riven adjusted the cuff of his sleeve as they passed a crooked sign etched with a faded flame.

“You sure this fence of yours didn’t skip town with the rest of the rats?” Ash asked, scanning the shadows.

“If he did, he left his tail behind. Same idiot’s been watching us since we turned down Knife Alley.”

Ash didn’t look, but the corner of his mouth curled. “I saw him. Green coat, limp on the right?”

“Patch over the wrong eye, but close.”

They moved with practiced ease, Riven loose and feline, Ash heavy-shouldered with slow, boiling presence.

A door opened as they approached, letting out a waft of stale wine and pipe smoke. Riven didn’t knock; he tapped twice, then paused, then three times fast. The rhythm was old undercity code. Moments later, a slot in the door scraped open, revealing a bloodshot eye behind a rusted grate.

That eye blinked. “Storms and slag... Riven Duskwind?”

Riven grinned. “In the flesh, fully underdressed, and still not dead.”

The door creaked open.

Inside, old barrels doubled as tables, and light came from sun-holes bored through stone and painted with glass. Behind the makeshift bar, a wiry man with patchy red hair and too many teeth leaned on the counter. His name was Teck, a former smuggler with debts in every port.

He looked Riven up and down. “Thought the Red Cloaks bled you out weeks ago.”

“Only my pride. This is Ash.” He motioned with his chin. “He sets fires and tells the truth about half the time.”

Ash gave a lazy salute. “That’s a generous average.”

Teck poured a cup of something too thin to be wine and pushed it across. “You didn’t come back to settle old debts.”

“No,” Riven said, stepping closer, lowering his voice. “We’re tracking tremors. Word is the tunnels were cleared. You see who cleared them?”

Teck glanced around and lowered his voice. “They came down with branded steel. Red cloaks. Not all Adepts. Some of ‘em wore Morlin cut. Heard ‘em say it was time to ‘cleanse the gutters’.”

“Rounded up. Some fought. Some… joined them.”

Teck paled. “You heard them too?”

“No one knows. It just starts at night. Some believe it is the voice of those killed the night when the plaza went up. Others say it is just the prison. There are even whispers of demons. But no one knows for sure,” said Teck, leaning towards them all conspiratorially. 

“We’ve also seen people act like their strings got cut and retied by someone else,” Ash said.

Teck fidgeted, suddenly sweating. “There’s something under the city, boys. Not just sewers. Older places. There are whispers down there as well. When the Red Cloaks cleared us out, they were not hunting contraband. They were hunting people. They had a list.”

“Whose list?” Ash asked.

“I don’t know. But I heard one name twice: Sythrael Korr. Doesn’t sound like a person. Sounds like a spell someone forgot to end.”

A long silence passed. Even the smoke seemed to quiet.

Then Teck added, almost to himself, “One of the old runners, Tevin, he worked the tunnels near the Breachwell arches.” He laughed to himself. ”Working, more like hiding.” His eyes went distant. He shook his head, then continued as if nothing had happened. “He’s been hiding down there for years. Said he’d found something big before the purges started. Been lying low since. If anyone’s got a deeper trail to follow, it’s him.”

Riven stiffened slightly at the name. “Tevin’s alive?”
“Last I heard. But if you’re smart, you’ll pretend he isn’t.”
The door slammed open. A broad-shouldered man stumbled in, swaying like a broken hinge. He blinked at Ash and Riven, squinted, then laughed.
“Foreigners! Lookin’ for heroes in the dirt, are ya?”

Ash tilted his head. "Just the truth." 

“You won’t find it here,” the man slurred. “They already dug it out, bled it, and left its bones in the gutter!”

Riven watched him go with narrowed eyes. “We should go. He’s not drunk, he’s distracted. Someone’s pulling him.”

They nodded their thanks to Teck, who just muttered, “Be careful where you listen down there. Some voices listen back.”
Ash flexed his fingers. “So, we have tunnel cleansings, possessed drunkards, and a name no one understands.”
He adjusted the collar of his coat, eyes scanning the alley mouth like a wolf sniffing the wind.
“And now Tevin’s name is in this mess too.”
“Then we find him,” Riven said. “We have to.”
The alley swallowed them. Shadows on a hunt.
The early afternoon haze settled in the alleys of Stormhaven's merchant quarter like a half-remembered dream. The streets were quieter than expected, muffled in an uneasy hush. Weslin walked with his hands clasped behind his back, chin up, trying very hard to appear as if he belonged. His cloak was too clean. His boots creaked too much. He nodded politely at every third person as if he were rehearsing a nobleman's mannerisms and hadn't yet passed the audition.

Reau, in contrast, strolled beside him with his hood up and shoulders loose, eyes tracking every window and side street. He said little, letting silence fill the space until it begged to be broken.

"You always walk like that?" Reau asked.

Weslin startled. "Like what?"

"Like you're trying to impress a ghost."

Weslin flushed. "I was taught to walk with purpose."

Reau grunted. "Good. But maybe not so loud about it next time. You're announcing yourself to half the quarter."

Stormhaven’s Temple of the Shining Oath had once stood as a beacon, its marble towers and twin bells known across Aldoria. But now, as Aurelia, Neavin, and Brother Aldrek stepped through its arched entryway, the sanctum had transformed.

Gone were the prayer banners and quiet reverence. The stone iconography had been scrubbed of scripture, replaced by shallow proclamations etched into plaques:

"Unity Through Devotion."
"Harmony Is Obedience."
"Thoughts Aligned, Hearts United."

Aldrek's face darkened the moment he saw them. “Blasphemy,” he muttered. “They’ve desecrated this place.”

Neavin slowed, his eyes flicking between runes scratched into the pillars and the bizarre symmetry of how the pews had been realigned into rigid squares. “This wasn't just cleared out. Somebody shaped this place like a shaft before a cave-in. Looks solid 'til it ain't.”

Aurelia pressed a hand to one of the stone walls, her eyes fluttering briefly as she opened herself to the echoes.

She drew back, pale. “There were rituals here. Not just prayers… conditioning. Forced compliance. Like trying to overwrite a soul with a false one, and something answered.”

A hunched figure in faded vestments emerged from a side door, an elderly woman with a twisted spine and sharp, birdlike eyes. She carried a basket of dried herbs and eyed them with suspicion.

“We don’t take pilgrims anymore,” she said. “The Oath has changed.”

“Changed?” Aldrek stepped forward, his voice low but tight with restrained fury. “Who has the authority to change the Word?”

She stared at him, expression unreadable. “Change comes whether you want it or not. New prayers for a new peace. We follow the Voice now.”

Neavin blinked. “What voice?”

She hesitated. Then, softly: “Sythrael Korr.”

The name rippled through them like a lash. Aurelia’s breath caught, and Neavin visibly stiffened.

“Who is that?” Aldrek asked, voice sharp.

But the old woman just blinked at him. “No one. Everyone. I forget.”

The woman scratched her arm. “Since the fire. Since the bells started ringing wrong. Ten days, maybe. It all blurs.”

The old woman looked at her with broken clarity. “He told us... the heart must open... and the blood must bind. We gave it willingly. That was the rule.”

Ash tilted his head. “That's how you welcome an old friend these days, or are you still mad about the fire?”

Tevin’s mouth twitched into something that wasn’t quite a smile. “You always bring the funny one?”

“He brings himself,” Riven muttered, stepping forward. “We’re not here to make amends. We’re here for answers.”

Tevin stood aside with a grunt, letting them pass.

Inside, the chamber was thick with smoke and something fouler beneath it, rot, perhaps, or mildew that had grown teeth. Lanterns swayed overhead, their light distorted by greasy glass. Broken crates ringed a low table scratched with knife marks and old ink stains. On the far wall, a map of the undercity was pinned up, frayed, overmarked, a tapestry of desperation.

Tevin gestured for them to sit. “Make yourselves uncomfortable.”

Ash ignored the jab and leaned against the nearest crate. Riven stayed standing, arms crossed, scanning the map. Three sectors had been circled in red, all near the southern spillways. 

Tevin moved with a limp, dropping into a stool with the creak of old joints and older grudges. “We lost the Undercircle,” he said without ceremony. “Red Cloaks came with fire and blades. Said nothing. Just cut and burned.”
Riven nodded grimly. “We heard.”
“You heard wrong,” Tevin snapped. “They did not just come to kill. They came to erase. Whole sectors are gone. Contacts gone. Safehouses gone. Like they were never there.”
Ash narrowed his eyes. “Why?”
Tevin’s shoulders rose and fell. “Wish I knew. Some say it is reeducation, others say it is for a ritual. There is a place where they take them. No one comes back from it.”
Ash huffed a humorless breath. “So the choice is chains or kindling. That is encouraging.”
Riven stepped closer. “What kind of ritual?”

Tevin looked up sharply. “You think they let people get close enough to find out? They come masked, shrouded. Chanting things no sane man repeats. Whatever it is, it’s not just for show.”

Ash shifted, voice low. “There’s a name I’ve heard passed around places gentle folk don’t visit. Got some of them scared. You know it?”

Tevin’s good eye flicked to him. “You mean Sythrael Korr.”

Riven straightened. “So you do know it.”

“I’ve heard it,” Tevin said carefully. “That name… people whisper it like it’s cursed. Even the Red Cloaks, I've seen them hesitate at it. But no one knows who or what it is. Just… fear.”

Ash exhaled. “Great. We’re following ghosts now.”

Tevin’s gaze slid past them, toward the door. “Whatever you’re doing, do it fast. The wrong eyes are watching everything down here. Trust me, they don’t all blink.”
“Oh, that day of horror,” Tevin said. “The Red Cloaks came with fire and blades. Said they were clearing rot. They just killed everyone, everyone without something they called a writ.”
“Writ?” Ash asked.
Tevin nodded. “Some new paperwork. You either got it from the new ‘authority,’ or you're dead.”
Riven leaned over the table, resting his hands on the scarred wood. “We need a direction. Somewhere, the trail keeps running.”
Tevin didn’t answer right away. He stared at the map, lips pressed thin. Then he tapped a sector near the southern spillways. “There’s a chemist’s depot. Ruined now. Used to be one of our forward caches. A place called Duskwalk. You’ll find burned ground and silence, but if anyone left clues, it’d be there.”

Ash raised an eyebrow. “That's your best lead?”

“No,” Tevin muttered. Then he stood and limped to the back of the room. He opened a rusted drawer, pulled a folded scrap of parchment, and slipped it into a small leather pouch. Without a word, he returned and pressed it into Riven’s palm.

“For your eyes only,” he said. “And be careful who you trust.”

Riven frowned but nodded. “Appreciate it.”

Riven spotted an open bag on the floor that hadn't been there before. He turned it over. A ragged map slid out, the same sector near the southern spillways Tevin had tapped. One path was labeled with ‘Sewer Valve 13: Rupture Control Node.’

Ash squinted at it. “So that’s what they used to blow the plaza?”

Riven frowned. “Maybe. Or maybe he just wanted us walking into a wall.”

They both nodded, deciding to follow it anyway.
“One last thing. You sure you don’t know anything else about Korr?”
Tevin’s brow furrowed. “Korr?” he echoed, slowly, as if tasting the name for the first time. “Who is that?”
Ash and Riven exchanged a look.
“You just told us about him,” Riven said.
“I did?” Tevin blinked. “Told you about who?”
“Korr,” Ash repeated. “You said even the Red Cloaks fear the name.”
Tevin’s eyes clouded. “That name…” His pupils widened, then lost focus.
He went still for a heartbeat, too still, then his face reset. Calm. Blank.
“I will keep an ear to the ground,” he said suddenly, almost cheerful. “For your friend. If he is worth looking for, maybe I will start looking too.”
His smile was wide and toothy, the first they’d seen from him. Before either could speak again, he stepped forward and gently but firmly closed the door.

Outside, the tunnel seemed colder than before. The lanterns flickered as they stood there in silence for a moment. Then Riven opened the pouch.

Inside was a different address. A name neither recognized.

And a note. ‘Don’t trust what people say. Especially me.’ Ash watched Riven's face while he read it. Whatever Riven felt, he kept it. He folded the note once, pocketed it, and said nothing.
