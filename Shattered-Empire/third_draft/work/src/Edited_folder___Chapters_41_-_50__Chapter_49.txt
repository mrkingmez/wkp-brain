The rain whispered against the stained-glass windows of the war chamber, streaking the colored panes like tears. Logs hissed in the fireplace, dimming now to amber pulses of heat. A thick silence draped the stone room, broken only by the occasional pop of a coal giving up its life.
At the long obsidian table, King Edric Wyman sat forward in his chair, staring down at the mirror.
It hadn’t lit up again, not since dawn.
The polished oval sat still on its iron base, its surface dark and smooth. Given to him by Harmon before the teams departed, the mirror had been used several times throughout the past ten days, once to show him a map, once to transmit a coded report, twice just to confirm a location. It reflected the image of whoever held its twin, linking the two like a private door between minds.
Now, it remained dormant. Across the room, near a marble pillar, Governor Meridian Drake stood in the half-light, shoulders squared, hands clenched at his sides.
They hadn’t spoken in nearly an hour. Edric’s eyes flicked up. “Still nothing,” he said quietly.
Drake didn’t move. His voice, when it came, was tight. “We’re past dusk.”
“The final fire hasn’t even gone out yet,” Edric said. “And the seventh bell hasn’t rung.”
That wasn’t comfort. It was math. A grim reminder that the window hadn’t closed, yet. The Governor shifted, and Edric turned back to the mirror. The room felt colder than it should.
Drake’s fingertips twitched near his belt, then stilled. Sweat beaded on his brow, though the hearth hadn’t lost all its warmth. He tried to focus on the room, the dark banners of the Accord hanging high, the old map of the Northern Territories behind the King’s chair, the wine decanter that hadn’t been touched.
He didn’t look at the King. He couldn’t. He looked, instead, at the dagger pressing against the small of his back beneath his coat, the hilt bruising his spine like a secret he couldn’t confess.
A soft hum broke the silence. The mirror pulsed. Both men turned.
Light shimmered along its edge, flared once, then went dark again. Nothing followed. No voice. No image. No words.
Drake was the first to speak. “Did you make anything out?”
Edric stared at the mirror for another heartbeat before answering. “No. I don’t know. They could still be moving,” Edric said.
Drake ran through the rest faster than he could stop himself. “Or they had to break contact. Or the route changed. Or the signal failed.” He stopped. “Or it didn't fail.” He stopped again. 
“Yes,” Edric said quietly. “All of that's possible.”
Drake’s voice thinned. “Or the mission failed entirely. Or…” His voice cracked. “Or they’re dead, and the mirror’s just….”
Edric didn’t answer immediately. “It could also mean,” he said carefully, “that they succeeded and decided not to risk another transmission until they were clear.”
Drake nodded once, but his eyes stayed locked on the mirror.
Edric tilted his head, studying it. “Or Harmon pressed the wrong sigil and activated the wrong side. Wouldn’t be the first time he’s misunderstood instructions.”
Drake didn’t smile. Didn’t move.
Edric sighed, then stepped closer and placed a steady hand on Drake’s shoulder.
“Meridian,” he said. “I could mean a hundred different things. We will know when we know. You’ve done everything you can. Whatever this means, it doesn’t mean it’s over. Harmon is relentless. If there’s a path out, he’ll find it.”
Drake swallowed, but said nothing.
His gaze drifted inward, thoughts folding in on themselves, spiraling deeper as the silence closed back around them.
Then the bell rang.
Clank.
The first bell rang, deep and heavy from the courtyard.
A chill ran down his back, a memory, a field soaked with ash and ice. Horses screaming. Swords crossing. The moment he’d turned to find a young prince, Edric, standing over him, back-to-back, their blades red with firelight. They hadn’t spoken then, just fought. That night, they held the line together against the New Morlin army, and neither of them had forgotten it since.
Clank.
His eyes closed. A hallway. Candlelight. The smell of lilac and ink. Karethiel laughed as she read him a letter aloud, teasing him for mispronouncing a noble’s name. Her voice always calmed him. He had not heard it in ten days. He had written her a poem once, terrible and off-rhythm, and she’d framed it anyway.
Clank.
His heart squeezed as two little faces entered his vision. Camryn, cheeks sticky with honey, chasing butterflies in the garden. Lysa perched on his shoulders, asking if all kings wore hats that shiny. He remembered her hand in his, asking if he'd be back before the moon was full. He told her yes.
He lied.
Clank.
He flinched. A hallway again. This time, darker. Colton’s gloved hand reached into a velvet pouch and pulled out a lock of blood-matted hair. “Do what is required of you,” the Steward had whispered. “If you don’t… it will be a work of art.” Then he was gone.
Clank.
The blade’s name was Whisperfang. He hadn’t known it at first. The Steward told him only after he'd accepted it. A dagger that didn’t just kill. A dagger that hollowed. The soul of the target, gone. The body, left standing… for someone else.
Clank.
His fingers slid beneath the back of his belt. The dagger was there. Cold. Waiting. This was the moment. No more delays. One clean strike, and maybe they would live. His breath caught. He looked at Edric one last time. The King hadn’t moved. Still watching the mirror.
Still trusting him. Still.
Clank.
He drew the blade and lunged. It struck iron. The blade skidded sideways, and Drake's wrist wrenched with it. Edric had taken precautions. Drake hadn't planned for that. He should have.
Edric spun.
The chainmail glinted as the dagger deflected off his ribs. Without a word, the King struck him, hard, backhanded, and Drake staggered into the stone, pain blooming along his face. Blood dripped from his lip, hot and bitter.
The chamber erupted in motion. The door slammed open. Sergeant Josiah burst in, followed by two guards, steel drawn. But Edric didn’t yell, just raised a hand, and they all stopped mid-step.
He reached into his coat and pulled free a parchment. He held it aloft. On it, one line:
Don’t trust your closest friends.
Drake didn’t move.
He was on his knees, breath heaving, hands open. Tears streaked his face. He looked not at the King, but at the floor. Just one word came from the King’s mouth. “Why?”
“I’m sorry,” he whispered. Drake’s lips trembled. “I didn’t want to. I swear it. But they had them, Karethiel… Camryn… Lysa. They said if I didn’t, if I didn’t.” His voice broke. “Steward Colton. He gave me the blade. He gave me the hair.”
“Where?” Edric said.
“My room. He’s been here the whole feast. Ten days. Watching. Waiting.”
“And you were waiting for a signal?”
Drake shook his head. “No. Not a signal. Just… confirmation. Anything from Harmon. Something to tell me my family was safe. But it never came.”
He wept openly now. “I thought…” he gasped. “If I didn’t… they’d be gone. And I couldn’t. I couldn’t lose them. I waited as long as I could, but…I’m sorry.”
Edric looked at the dagger lying on the stone. He bent, picked it up, and studied the blade. “What is this?”
“Whisperfang,” Drake whispered. “It wouldn’t have killed you. Not really. But your soul…” He broke again. “They said you’d keep ruling. Just not as you.”
The room was silent. Then Edric straightened. “I understand,” he said softly. “If it had been Mirelle or a son or daughter of mine, I would’ve broken too.” He turned to Josiah. “Take him to his chambers. Post guards. Ensure he doesn’t leave, and no one reaches him.”
Josiah saluted. “Yes, Your Grace.”
Drake didn’t resist as the Guards helped him up.
It had been silent through the bells. Silent through the kneeling. Silent through the words that had come out of Drake in pieces. Then it flared. The mirror, still sitting at the obsidian table, alight now with Harmon's hue.
Harmon’s voice crackled through, “Governor’s family secured. Karethiel, Camryn, and Lysa are safe. They’re with me now. Tell him his wife never doubted him. Not once. We are making haste home.”
Drake stopped cold. He turned. His knees buckled. One of the guards caught him as he sobbed again, this time from relief.
Edric stared at him for a long moment, then looked back at the blade. “Lock it away,” he told Josiah.
“Yes, Sire,” answered Josiah. The guards led Drake from the room.
As the door closed, another opened. Queen Mirelle swept in, the hem of her gown still damp from the rain. She crossed the floor without a word and wrapped her arms around her husband’s waist. He didn’t flinch. She said nothing. Her hands found his face for one moment, checking, then dropped. She stepped back and let him be the king.
Josiah stepped forward. “What are your orders, Sire?”
Edric’s voice was steel. “Find the Steward. I want him in chains before the dawn.”
Josiah nodded and vanished.
Queen Mirelle entered her private chambers in silence. The wine poured first. Deep red, slow swirl, steady hand. She didn’t sip. Not yet.
The echo of voices from the war room still lingered like fog behind her eyes. Drake’s betrayal. The dagger. The note. The confession. All of it, a performance that was now behind closed doors.
She crossed the room without pause and stopped at the open balcony. The chill kissed her skin.
He was there. A shadow draped in silk and smoke, perched near the stone railing, unmoving. She didn’t look at him when she said, “You’re early.” A grin stretched beneath his hood. “I’m eager.”
“And?” she asked with a half-raised smile.
“Three. Gone.” He stepped forward, breath quickening. “It’s done.”
He held out a small pouch. She took it, opened it, and found bloodied cloth, torn insignias, and the stench of copper inside. She smiled softly. “Proof. Well done.”
He inhaled as he stepped closer, nose almost brushing her hair, fingers twitching at his sides. He moved close. His eyes stayed too long. The kind of looking that forgot it was looking. 
“Now… my payment.”
She turned to face him, eyes unreadable. Her hand rose slowly, a single finger pressing to his lips before he could lean in. She let her fingers rest against his chest for just a moment, light enough to be accidental, deliberate enough not to be. "You think I have forgotten?" she said, and something in her voice was genuine enough to be dangerous. "I haven't." She let that land. Let him wonder which part she meant. Then the smile came, slow and private. "I keep my promises. Every one of them. And when this is finished, I intend to enjoy keeping that one." She stepped back just far enough. "Good things are worth finishing for."
He kissed her anyway, desperate and gasping at it. She returned the effort, letting it last long enough to tether his hunger tighter.
His hand slid down her body to her hips, and he moved to pull her closer. For half a second, she held her face slightly away, restoring the expression before he could read the restoration. Then she stepped back. "Not yet." 
“But… I thought…” he said.
From her sleeve, she pulled a folded note. “This time, no names. Only instructions,” she said.
He took it, trembling fingers betraying the illusion of control. “After this… you’ll be mine?”
She smiled again. Let him dream. That was the leash. “After this, you’ll get everything you deserve.”
He leaned forward again. “One more kiss?”
She obliged. Deeper. Longer. She let her lips linger just enough.
“Now go,” she murmured. “My husband is coming.”
The balcony emptied in a blink. She stayed facing the door, hand on the stone rail. The scent of him still clung to her dress. She wondered briefly if Edric would smell it. Then decided she did not care. There was nothing in this room she would not tell him if he asked.
Footsteps. Warm arms wrapped around her waist from behind. A kiss to her neck. Then his voice, low in her ear, “Is it done?”
Her reply was a whisper: “Oh yes. Men are so easy to manipulate. For most… it is one thing.”
Edric didn’t move. “What has it cost?” His breath caught slightly. “Did you…?”
She turned in his arms and pressed a hand to his chest. “No. That is only for you. You’re the only one who’s ever been man enough to keep up with me.”
He smiled, then kissed her. This time, deeply. No politics. No games. Just him and her.
“It is rare in these royal marriages to find someone you love and someone who can rule beside you. I was told the two rarely came together. God the Creator made a liar of everyone who told me that.”
She smiled back. “He smiled at us both.”
They stood there for a moment. Then she spoke again, voice like silk-wrapped steel, “He’s ours now, Drake. He will never rise again. He owes you everything.”
Edric didn’t smile. But he didn’t disagree. “And after I ride into Aldoria with my forces to help secure their border, with the people watching me lead and him follow…” He exhaled through his nose, dark satisfaction in his voice. “He will bend the knee. And I will have control of them.”
Mirelle’s lips curved with approval. “Now it’s accelerated,” she said, brushing her fingers over the mirror’s base. “Thanks to Aldoria’s chaos, we can take advantage of that and move everything up. Once my part is finished.”
Then she looked up at him, eyes gleaming.
“…your father’s dream, your bloodline’s right, will be fulfilled.”
Edric pulled her into one final kiss, slower this time, but no less intense.
When they parted, he looked down at her with a faint smirk. “When that assassin returns,” he said, “he won’t get the reward he thinks he will.”
She let out a quiet laugh. “As I said, he will get the surprise he deserves.”
The warmth in his eyes did not match his smile. He pulled her close. "I want you tonight. Come to my chambers," he said, and the command in his voice was the same one he used when reviewing maps.
She raised an eyebrow, playful. “How many others have you invited to your chambers since we wed?”
“None,” he said, brushing her cheek. “You’re the only one who’s seen them after dark.” He kissed her. She let it be what it was. She had known before she asked, she always knew. Some questions were worth asking for the way he answered them, like he had never once considered saying anything else. 
They separated, and he turned to go. Her smile widened. “I was already planning to.” She let him take one step, his head half turned away, before she added, “I will be waiting for you in your chambers. Wearing only a smile.”
He turned back in a flash.
“It is time we gave the people an heir,” she said. “For me to give you a son.”
His smile changed. Quieter than the political one. Truer. "A daughter works as well," he said. He could see it without trying. A younger version of her, with her mind, her nerve. Her specific ruthlessness applied to problems that mattered. Whatever name the child carried, this kingdom would not want for leadership. 
She moved close to him again, their lips meeting once more. Then he pulled away, reluctantly. “I must go. Need to say goodnight to our guests.”
“Go,” she said softly, watching him go.
“Tonight,” he said as the door closed behind him.
She stood alone, picked up the glass of wine, and took a long drink, eyes still on the rainy sky beyond her balcony. Holding the glass by her face, deep in thought, she whispered to herself, “Soon.”
King Edric stepped out of his wife’s private chambers, adjusting his collar as he moved down the corridor.
Outside the great hall, nobles milled about in small clusters, their voices low with fatigue and wine. Servants extinguished lanterns as the final night of the feast wound down.
“Lord Merrin, safe travels in the morning,” Edric said with a nod to the aging noble and his wife, who bowed and began ascending the eastern staircase.
“Lady Sereva,” he added with a polite smile. “I trust the musicians didn’t offend.”
“Only their tempo,” she quipped, earning a chuckle from the king.
He turned toward the eastern archway and slowed. A compact woman in a deep indigo sash stepped forward with the deliberateness of someone who had been waiting for precisely this moment. Merchant-Admiral Sola Drey of Varek. Three island chains south of Opindale, ten port towns on the Merchant Island alone. She was the kind of woman who made other negotiators feel they were behind on their paperwork.
"Article Seven," she said without preamble. "I want to confirm my fleet reads the harbor access language the same way your trade minister does."
"I imagine it reads the same to both of you," Edric said, "which is why both of you left the table satisfied."
She studied him. Then something like respect crossed her face, quick as a weather change. "Good evening, Majesty."
He moved on.
Near the base of the main stair, a man stood alone. Factor Jeld of Ashmark, the one surviving city on the Mirror Sea's northern coast. He wore good formal wool, but the cut was heavier than the room called for, the collar higher than fashion required. A man who had learned to dress for indoors and outdoors at the same time because in Ashmark, the difference was sometimes academic. He had no country colors. Ashmark had none. Only the coin and the long memory of a city that had survived the burning of everything around it, then thrived from it.
"Things are moving in the north, Majesty," he said when Edric reached him. Not a greeting. Not quite a warning. "Not our business to say what. But Ashmark thought you should know the passes are opening earlier than they should."
Edric kept his face still. "I appreciate the observation."
"No charge," Jeld said. "Tonight."
He stepped back into the crowd without looking away. That was Ashmark's manner. They always watched while retreating.
A young lord fell into step beside Edric for half a corridor before seeming to realize that required nerve. Lord Colven of Serath, the small coastal nation west of the peninsula, here representing his father, who was too old for the crossing. He smelled faintly of salt air even indoors.
"The timber clause," he said quickly. "My father wanted me to say thank you. The timber clause was the thing."
"Your father negotiated it well," Edric said.
"He'll be pleased to hear that."
"He should be. He didn't give any ground he didn't have to."
Colven smiled and dropped back. That was Serath done.
A voice interrupted before the king could respond further. “Majesty,” called a man from across the hall, lifting a hand in greeting, one of the foreign dignitaries, his formal sash trailing. “Might I have a word before…”
Another voice cut in. Quieter. Older. Deeper. “Forgive the intrusion,” came a voice. “But I believe I’ve earned the next word.”
The dignitary blinked, faltered, and stepped back as a robed figure moved between them with unsettling ease, white-haired, weathered, and short in stature, but cloaked in a presence that stole attention. His garments were formal, but two generations out of fashion. Rings lined his fingers. His eyes held centuries. He walked without effort, like the air itself made room for him.
Edric turned, the warmth still on his face from Mirelle’s parting smile. It faded. The old man noted it before anything else. He had seen that particular warmth before, on a face this one would never have recognized. The same pull at the corner of the eyes. The same breath before someone said a name they trusted. He held it for one beat, then put it somewhere quiet. His expression didn't change. It never changed. But his hands were very still at his sides, the way a man's hands went still when he was holding something carefully that no one else could see. 
“I don’t recognize you,” the King said carefully. “Are you with one of the attending nations?”
The man gave a soft smile, neither kind nor mocking. Just… knowing. “No. Just a guest tonight. A friend.”
Edric raised a brow. “Of whom?”
The man’s head tilted. “Of the realm.”
The words found something in him that was already waiting for them. 
Edric stepped closer, lowering his voice. “And what does this friend want?”
The man’s gaze swept the crowd and then returned to the king’s with quiet weight. “To tell you something you need to hear, though you already feel it, don’t you?”
Something in Edric's posture shifted. "Feel what?" 
"That a door is opening," the man said. "One that was closed at great cost, not long enough ago. That beneath the lights and the laughter, something long denied has begun to move again. Something that remembers power, not age." 
Edric’s breath caught, just for a moment. “What do you know,” he asked, “that I don’t?”
The man didn’t answer at first. Looking at the king, he said calmly, “What I know would take more nights than this feast has left. Tonight, one piece is enough.” He took another step forward, his voice lowering like dusk falling. “The Uncrowned King is returning.”
Edric stiffened. 
“He just made his first play,” the man continued. “Stormhaven was the first jewel in his unclaimed crown. It will not be the last.”
The king swallowed. “You’re certain of this.”
“I am,” the man said. “And I come with more than certainty.”
His fingers tapped against the side of a silver ring. “My ears are many. They gather whispers. Warnings.”
He leaned in just enough for only the king to hear.
“Here is the one you must remember. The Time of Man is over.”
Silence stretched between them like a held breath.
Edric’s voice came low. “Who are you?”
But the man was already turning. Before he moved, his gaze swept the hall once. Unhurried. The practiced arc of a man who had been reading rooms longer than most rooms had been standing.
The crowd moved the way crowds moved. Glasses lifted. Heads turned. Laughter came and went.
One figure near the north stair column did not move with it.
He watched for ten seconds. Not the stillness. The eyes. A real person blinked when something moved nearby, when a laugh broke across the room, when a tray clattered two tables over. The eyes responded because the mind responded. This one's eyes blinked on their own rhythm, steady and private, like a pulse that had nothing to do with the room around it.
He had seen that tell before. He knew what it meant.
He kept walking. Natural. Unhurried. The path took him close enough to the column that the servant's gaze crossed his for half a second before moving on.
The chill he felt had nothing to do with the breeze from the open window.
The cold behind those eyes was different. It was not just a lack of feeling. It was a lack of everything. As if whatever stood behind that face had never once needed the things that made a person human. The servant looked at the people around him the way a man looks at graves he has not yet dug.
He had felt that before. Once. A feeling a person did not forget because it did not allow itself to be forgotten. A feeling that had reshaped the world the last time he had stood close enough to it. One he had believed was buried. Gone. Dealt with the way things got dealt with when enough people paid enough of a price.
He couldn't shake it. And then it clicked. Some things were more useful in motion than stopped. He kept walking. Fought the urge to look back, careful not to draw those eyes to him. He didn't truly vanish. He simply stepped into the current of the crowd and was gone, swept back into the motion of the chamber as though he’d never been there at all.
Edric glanced back toward the place the man had stood. Nothing. No cloak. No trace. Just an empty patch of floor and the particular silence a person leaves when they were there and then were not. He stood still for a moment longer than he meant to. Had he spoken with someone? The voice was real. He was certain of the voice. He was less certain of everything else.
Then he felt it. Eyes from above. The upper gallery, somewhere in the shadow of the second-floor railing. He did not look up directly. He had learned long ago not to look directly at things that watched from the dark. He let his gaze drift upward the way a man's gaze drifts when he is only thinking.
Nothing he could name. Nothing he could point to.
He relaxed his posture and looked back at the crowd. The delegates. The wine. The last of the lanterns were burning down. Someone had been watching him talk to the old man, that was all. One of a dozen people waiting for their moment with the king, held back by the stranger who had stepped in ahead of them. That was all it was. He moved on.
Across the hall, unnoticed by most, a servant watched the whole chamber from the edge of a tall support beam. His lips had moved with every word. He smiled faintly. Then turned.
As he passed a darkened side alcove, he stepped lightly over a crumpled body, the true servant, pale and lifeless, beneath the folds of linen.
With a soft roll of his shoulders, he shifted. Bones cracked. Skin rippled. His form bent and blurred. The changeling walked on in silence.
The weight of the words lingered, each syllable etched like fresh script across the back of his mind. Return.
Edric had nearly reached the great hall again when something pulled him back. A voice. Hesitant and edged with worry. Three foreign delegates stood near the stairwell, cloaked in their lesser kingdom’s colors. One bowed, another sipped nervously from a goblet. The third was Countess Avren Talisse of Drenthal, her forest-green sash the only still thing about her. Sharp-eyed, composed, with the posture of someone who had been deciding whether to approach for the last hour and had finally decided. 
“Majesty,” she said, “forgive the hour.”
Edric nodded. “You’re far from home. I make time for those who honored my feast.”
“We’re grateful,” she replied. “But… some among our parties have gone missing.”
Edric tilted his head. “Missing?”
“Our king,” she said. “And two of his advisors. We’ve not seen them since the fourth night. No word. No sight. They left their rooms, but never returned. We thought at first they’d left early, but their horses are still here, and their cloaks are still in their chambers.”
Edric’s fingers curled inward, the gesture small, but not unnoticed.
They weren’t on the list. No one else was supposed to die. Only the heads of state.
And yet, some eggs must break before the feast can begin, his wife’s voice whispered through memory.
There is no unity without sacrifice. We were never made to be small. He had heard it so many times that it no longer sounded like a voice. It sounded like his own thought. 
Edric closed his eyes for half a breath, then forced the thoughts away.
He met the delegate's gaze. “Our city opens itself fully during the Accord,” he said carefully. “It can be disorienting. I can promise you, some guests... wander.“
“Yes,” she said. “But the king rarely wanders far without his crown.”
A long pause. “I’ll have some of our best look into it,” Edric replied at last. He smiled, she returned the gesture, as he turned away.
But his chest clenched, tight and urgent, as if the whole dream might crack if he didn’t hold himself steady. If he wavered now, the kingdom they wanted would crumble before it rose. He let it pass through him and kept moving. There was still a feast out there that needed its king.
