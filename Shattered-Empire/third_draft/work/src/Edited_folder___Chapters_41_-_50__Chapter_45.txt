The barricade gave way with a deafening crack, iron rods and splintered wood clattering to the stone floor as dust billowed into the ruined prison chamber.

Through that dust stepped six Red Cloaks, disciplined, armored, precise, their crimson cloaks hanging heavy, their helms catching the flickering torchlight. At their center moved the Adept, lean and dark-robed, his lips moving soundlessly, blue glyphs already forming and pulsing in the air around his hands. Two of the Red Cloaks raised crossbows, aiming straight for Ash and Nyssa. 

Lethal purpose burned in the eyes of both sides.

Dain, panting, his blade dripping with black ichor from the dead beast-like creatures, took a half-step forward, trying to summon one of his trademark quips, but his throat was raw, his mind scattered and exhausted. Nothing clever came.

Nyssa glanced toward him, lips parting as if to speak herself, but closed them just as quickly, her gaze narrowing with sharp resolve.

Without a command, the Red Cloaks fired. Two crossbows snapped, bolts screaming across the short distance, aimed straight for Ash and Nyssa’s hearts. The Adept unleashed his spell a heartbeat later, a molten orb of arcane magic twisting through the air, a blast meant to obliterate whatever it touched. In that instant, Dain and Nyssa had the same thought. They saw it in each other's eyes. Then Ash moved. 

Ash tapped his shard, drawing everything the fragment had left. His fingers burned with crimson flame as he hurled both arms up. The fire shield roared to life, an arcing sphere of flame and force that swallowed the crossbow bolts in a puff of ash and smothered the Adept’s blast in a crackling explosion of light and heat.

The shockwave buckled the stone beneath their feet. Heat washed over their faces. Then nothing. They were still alive. 

Inside the sphere of protection, Ash gritted his teeth, sweat pouring down his temples. His hands trembled as he forced the wall to stay up, knowing the cost. His breath came in ragged bursts. His knees almost buckled.

Dain turned toward Ash, breathless, eyes wide with disbelief and fierce respect.
“By the gods’ beard, Ash… you didn’t just save us. You held back death itself.”

Nyssa said nothing. Her daggers were already in her hands. 

He could feel the shard fraying now, threads of power unspooling too fast, beyond his ability to rein them in. “Aldrek,” he gasped, “now…!”

The priest stepped up beside him, robes torn and blood-streaked but eyes clear. He placed one palm gently on Ash’s shoulder, and a soft golden glow flowed from his fingertips. The pain in Ash’s hands eased slightly, the trembling slowed, but Aldrek’s power was finite too.

“I can’t hold this forever,” Ash whispered, teeth clenched.

“I know,” Aldrek murmured, “but I can buy you moments.”

And with that, Aldrek raised his other hand, tracing a swift circle of white light in the air before him. A shimmering second shield snapped into place, paler than Ash’s, thinner, but reinforcing it.

The two walls pulsed together now, fire and light woven into a fragile sanctuary.

Dain moved to Nyssa’s side, his voice low but sharp. “We need a plan, now. The moment he drops that shield, we’ll have a breath, maybe two.”

Nyssa nodded, sliding a dagger into each hand, eyes flicking over their foes. The Red Cloaks didn’t rush. They waited, disciplined, as the Adept continued weaving his next spell, preparing another assault the moment Ash’s protection failed.

Dain swallowed hard, sweat burning his eyes. He felt every bruise, every ache, every breath scraping raw in his lungs. But he raised his blade. Dain's mouth felt dry as sand, his throat raw, but he spoke. "They're not going to stop." 

“No,” Nyssa replied, her daggers loose in her hands, knuckles white. “And Ash already has had it up for a long time.”

Ash was already shaking, sweat pouring down his face, jaw clenched so tightly his teeth ached. His shard’s warmth inside his chest had faded from a steady flame to a sickly flicker. He could feel it. He was bleeding it dry.

Aldrek stood beside him, his white robes streaked with soot and blood, his free hand pressed gently to Ash’s shoulder, maintaining a thread of healing light, just enough to hold him upright. His mace was gripped tight in the other, ready.

The Red Cloaks were positioning for the next strike, and Ash knew if he let this shield drop, their blades and spells would be inside them in seconds.

But the shield wouldn’t last. His power was slipping. He felt it slipping.

Dain’s gaze flicked to Nyssa and then to Aldrek, quiet resolve behind his exhaustion. “We move the second it drops,” he said.

Nyssa gave a tight nod. “No hesitation.”

Ash finally spoke, his voice hoarse but steady. “Get ready.”

He took a single breath, steadying his stance, flames swirling erratically now, his hands trembling violently.

Dain turned his head slightly, just enough to meet Nyssa’s gaze. For a heartbeat, they both knew. “On my mark,” he whispered. Ash grunted. “Tell me when… because when I drop this, they’ll come fast.”

“Now,” was all Ash heard before he let go.

The shield flickered once. Then again. Then shattered.

A rush of hot air and crackling flame whooshed outward as Ash let it fall. He staggered back, chest heaving, vision blurring, but the others were already moving.

“Cut them down!” barked one of the Red Cloaks, a voice from behind the Adept, sharp and commanding.

Aldrek set himself. He raised his free hand, and the second wall went up to replace the first. White light instead of red flame. 

The blast from his shield boomed out, pushing everyone outside of it back. The blast still hummed through the stones. And yet… the Red Cloaks didn’t break. They regrouped quickly, then spread out, disciplined, methodical, helmets gleaming, short swords drawn, eyes sharp behind steel visors. The Adept’s fingers traced more glowing runes into the air, building toward a second, deadlier spell.

“Rorek, flank left!” another shouted. Their discipline was clear, tight, and drilled, no wasted motion. But the team met them head-on.

Nyssa was first. She darted sideways, evading a lunging sword stroke from one of the crossbowmen who had dropped his bow for a short sword now. Her daggers slashed low, she felt the satisfying catch of steel on flesh as she hamstrung him, blood splattering across her boots.

His scream cut through the chaos, brief and sharp, but Nyssa didn’t linger. She rolled past him as another Red Cloak swung for her head, his blade hissing through empty air.

Dain parried a Red Cloak’s strike, steel ringing against steel. His arm ached, heavy and slow, but he pushed forward, catching his opponent’s wrist on the next swing and driving his rapier into the man’s side. The blade bit deep, ribs cracking, but Dain barely registered it, his body moving from reflex and desperation, getting away from his grapple. 

“By the gods’ beard,” he muttered under his breath, “just fall…”

Ash lashed out next, too weak for a wide flame burst now. His fingers sparked as he directed a thin lance of fire toward the Adept’s face. The Red Cloak Adept twisted aside, his own magic completing at last.

A sphere of blue fire formed in his palm, a searing, deadly thing, and he hurled it straight for Ash.

But Aldrek was already moving. He stepped in front of the blast, raising his palm in a smooth, practiced motion. A radiant sigil flared into being before him, ancient, precise, and the blue spell struck it dead-on.

The bolt vanished with a thunderclap of light and pressure, the energy swallowed entirely by Aldrek’s ward.

The White Adept didn’t flinch. His voice rang out in cold defiance, echoing off the stone. “Not today.”

Aldrek moved in immediately. His mace swept upward, catching a Red Cloak in the ribs with a brutal crunch. Blood spattered Aldrek’s white robes. He pivoted on his heel, his injured leg almost giving out, but he stayed standing, running on instinct and pure adrenaline.

A second Red Cloak rushed him from behind.

Aldrek raised his free hand, and a searing flash of pale light burst from his palm. The Red Cloak screamed as the light seared into his face, temporarily blinding him. Aldrek turned, brought the mace around, and struck the man’s knee, sending him sprawling.

Nyssa intercepted the staggered Red Cloak, daggers flashing. She buried one deep into his neck, twisting hard.

“Lorn, down!” one of them yelled. Another raised his sword mid-order: “Circle left! Shut them do…” Aldrek’s mace caught him in the side of the skull mid-command. The man’s voice cut off with a wet crack as he collapsed sideways, armor ringing like a dropped bell. “Enough of that,” Aldrek muttered, eyes hard. Their discipline frayed as their numbers thinned. 

Dain fought two at once now. He could barely lift his arm, but he ducked and spun, parrying one strike while narrowly dodging another.

He tried to speak, to offer one of his usual barbed jabs, but no words came. His lungs burned, his mind blank with exhaustion. Ash drew steel at last, his short sword rasping from its scabbard as he stepped into a Red Cloak’s guard.

His fire was gone, his shard almost spent. This was blade work now. He caught a strike on the flat of his sword and countered, driving the point into the man’s thigh. Blood flowed hot across his hand as the Red Cloak fell, screaming.

“Kael! Kael’s hit!” another shouted, a panicked voice now. Only the Adept and one sword-wielding Red Cloak. The Adept snarled, gathering magic for a final burst, but Aldrek was faster this time. He stepped forward, blood trailing down his leg, pain etched on his face. His hand raised, glowing again, not healing now but radiant force.

“Enough,” he said, voice hard, and released it.

A brilliant blast of white light shot from his palm, striking the Adept square in the chest. The Red Cloak Adept’s spell collapsed in a flare of blue sparks as he fell backward, slamming into the wall and slumping, stunned. Dain stepped forward immediately, driving his rapier through the man’s throat. 

One Red Cloak left, cornered, bleeding, desperate. He raised his sword but froze as Nyssa’s dagger found his throat. Her voice was cold. “Drop it.” His weapon clattered to the floor. She then brought an elbow across his jaw, laying him out. 

They stood together at last, battered, bloodied, drenched in sweat, but alive. Ash went to one knee, breath ragged, blood slicking his side from where a blade had caught him earlier. Nyssa leaned on her knees, panting. Dain wiped sweat and blood across his face with a shaking hand. Aldrek limped but stood, mace still in hand, white robes streaked red. The last Red Cloak was alive, but only just.

The air hung heavy with heat and soot, thick as wet cloth in their lungs. The last crackle of magic had faded. No more fire, no more bolts. Just the low hiss of cooling stone and the quiet sounds of pain.

No one spoke at first. Ash sagged against a wall, breath stuttering, his knees still trembling. Across the room, Aldrek was binding a torn muscle in his thigh with a strip of cloth, face pale but focused. The Red Cloak prisoner lay facedown, one arm twisted behind his back, still alive but unconscious. 

Dain looked around the scorched room. "Secure the door. Then rest." They gave themselves one minute, maybe two.

Nyssa nodded and moved to drag a broken chair into place, wedging it beneath the ruined handle. Aldrek joined her, checking their makeshift barricade with a tired grunt.

Ash moved first, slow and quiet. He crouched beside one of the fallen archers, sifting through the man’s gear with practiced hands. Bolts, three still in the leather quiver. He took the whole quiver. Another body nearby, another fallen Red Cloak. Another two bolts.

He pulled the light crossbow from beneath the corpse’s arm, tested the draw mechanism with two tired cranks, and heard a satisfying click. A Dwarven self-load, well-crafted, well-balanced. “Still works,” he muttered, half to himself.

He kept moving through the bodies in silence. 

From a third corpse, he pulled a satchel with dried rations and a length of waxed cord. “This one died mad,” Ash muttered. “His dagger’s clean.” He tossed the satchel aside. Then he moved to the adept, the one who’d thrown the blue fireball.

Ash flipped the man over and carefully pried open the chest plate.

Inside, nestled in a copper cradle beneath the ribbed gorget, pulsed a dull violet shard. Arcane. Still warm. Ash stared at it. Not his affinity. Not fire. But usable. He twisted it free and rolled it between his fingers. “Arcane shard,” he muttered to the others. “Won’t hit as hard as a red one… but it’ll carry me through.”

He slid it into the binding socket at his collar and exhaled. The warmth that pulsed into him was thin, sharp-edged. But it was something.

Behind him, Nyssa’s voice broke the silence. “Find anything else?”

“Making a pile,” Ash replied, motioning toward a crate. “Dagger, darts, one rope with enough length for binding, and,” he picked up a small pouch and gave it a shake, “steel ball bearings.”

He tossed them onto the pile.

“This yours?” He held up a slim throwing knife.

Nyssa snatched it from his fingers without a word.

Ash nodded toward another pouch and opened it. “Dain, you use firestones?”

“I prefer not to burn my eyebrows off,” Dain muttered. “But I’ll take one.”

Ash tossed him two. “You’ve got pretty eyebrows. Would be a shame.”

Nyssa gave a little laugh while looking at Dain, picturing him with no eyebrows.

Aldrek glanced toward a flask on one of the fallen’s belts.

Ash held it up. “Water. Nothing else.”

Aldrek shook his head. “Keep it. You’ll need it more.”

Ash took a swig, then passed it on.

Dain, meanwhile, crouched beside the prisoner. The man’s breath was shallow, his jaw bloodied. Aldrek knelt beside him, resting his hand on the man’s chest and sending a thread of white light into his ribs, just enough to wake him without truly healing. The Red Cloak groaned and stirred. “Don’t fix him too much,” Nyssa said. Aldrek didn’t look up. “Just enough to talk.” Dain rose slowly, wiped grime from his hands, and turned to the others. “How far do we want to push him?”

Ash leaned on his crossbow. “As far as it takes.”

Nyssa didn’t speak, just checked the edge of her dagger.

Aldrek’s voice was level. “I’ll keep him alive. That’s all I’ll promise. I don't kill someone who is out of the fight.”

Dain nodded once, then crouched and slapped the man lightly across the face.

The Red Cloak’s eyes fluttered open. His gaze was unfocused at first, then narrowed as he saw them surrounding him.

“Welcome back,” Dain said. “Now talk.”

A dry, broken laugh rasped from the man’s throat. “No.”

Dain didn’t move. “That wasn’t a request.”

“What’re you going to do if I don’t?” the prisoner muttered, voice gravel-thick with exhaustion. “Kill me? Leave me here to rot?”

“We’ve got options,” Nyssa said, crouching beside him, the point of her knife resting casually near his ribs. “Neither of them is pleasant.”

Talron's gaze darted to her, then to the others, Ash leaning on his crossbow, Aldrek looming quiet as a tombstone, the scent of soot and iron still thick in the air.

“I’m already a dead man,” he said flatly. “Whether it’s you or what waits upstairs… makes no difference.”

“That's just a matter of perspective,” Dain said, settling into a crouch. “If we like what we hear... arrangements can be made.”

Talron’s mouth twisted. "Tell you what? The secrets I had are cooling on that floor. Whatever's left won't matter by morning." 

He coughed hard, blood spraying his chin.

“You want names? Plans? Secrets? No," he said.

“You’ve got one minute, until…” Nyssa said coldly. “Make it count.”

For a long moment, Talron didn’t speak. Just watched them.

Nyssa studied him, truly studied him. The bruising, the blood, the lines of exhaustion etched deeper than any wound. She let out a deep sigh.

“It’s like you said,” she murmured. “You’re a dead man either way. Why not cooperate? You look like someone who stopped wanting to fight a long time ago.”

Talron didn’t respond, not with words. But his jaw shifted slightly, the first crack.

Dain’s voice followed, low but steady.

“I’ve stood where you are. Surrounded. Wounded. Thinking the only way left is down.”

He knelt beside the prisoner, meeting his gaze without threat, just weight. Truth.

“The only reason I’m breathing is that someone gave me a way out I didn’t believe in… not at first. So here’s your chance. Take it, or don’t. But I’m telling you… It’s real.”

Then he whispered, “My name’s Talron. It always has been.”

Nyssa raised a brow. “Why the second part?” 

“Because everything else in this city has changed.” He closed his eyes, jaw clenching. “I just wanted something left that didn’t.”

Dain tilted his head. “What changed?”

Talron opened his eyes again, dull, gray-blue, ringed with red. “We weren’t meant to take Stormhaven. Not like this. We were told to cause unrest. Strike key points. Pull the lion’s tail while the governor was off playing diplomat. It was supposed to be loud and fast.”

“How many of you?” Aldrek asked.

“Two hundred.” He swallowed. “And fifty of us were Adepts.”

He glanced toward the body Ash had stripped, the one lying twisted near the broken pillar.

“Forty-nine now.”

Ash’s eyes narrowed, but he said nothing.

Talron looked away, voice rough. “We came to sow chaos. But when we arrived… something changed. We weren’t met with blades. We were invited in. The Garrison commander opened the gates. Said we were expected.”

“Expected by whom?” Nyssa asked.

Talron gave a bitter laugh. “That’s the question, isn’t it?”

Ash stepped closer, arms folded. “What then?”

“Then more came. Reinforcements. Not Red Cloaks. Not Brotherhood. Men in strange armor, faces I’d never seen. They set up control posts. Whispered to our commanders. Then we were told to hold, not just strike. Hold the city.”

“And you listened?” Dain asked.

“We follow orders, don’t you?” Talron said simply. “And Stormhaven fell without a drop of blood spilled. The gates, the watchposts, the towers. All ours. It was beyond what anyone on my team could dream of.” 

Talron hesitated, his voice low and almost reverent. “They told us to take this tower. Secure the highest point.”

He glanced toward the ceiling, as if the weight of the stone above meant something more.

“There’s an old tale,” he said. “Older than the war. Older than the Red Cloaks. Says the tower isn’t just the crown of the city, it’s its spine. Break it, and Stormhaven breaks with it. And if Stormhaven falls, Aldoria won’t be far behind.”

He looked at each of them in turn. “Maybe it’s just a story. But the ones giving the orders believe it. That’s why the orders changed, well, that is why I believe they changed. We were told to place something on its crown.”

“What kind of something?” asked Dain. 

Talron shook his head. “A box. Tall. Sealed. No hinges, no latch. Blacker than pitch. Not the sort of thing you open, just something you carry like it’s breathing.”

The team exchanged uneasy glances. Dain said nothing. Nyssa said nothing. Ash looked at the ceiling, like he could see through thirty floors of stone to whatever was sitting up there. He couldn't. But the trying said enough. 

Aldrek took a turn to ask a question, “So Red Cloak leadership wants to take Aldoria? That doesn’t make much sense. Not from a rabble group, that's not what Red Cloaks do." He paused. "Someone told them to become something different. Who?" 

“I never said it came from our leadership; it came from someone. Someone with the power to control us, Veydrassil. They really don’t include me much in the planning councils.” 

Nyssa leaned forward. “Fine, what do you know about the excavation?”

Talron’s face twitched. “It was already underway before we got here. Deep below the old Unity Square. Something buried… old magic. Older than any of us. Some kind of arch or gate, or whatever. From what I gathered, it was sealed by the square and its statues, so they sent us to take care of it, well, the adepts.”

“So you destroyed them,” Ash muttered.

Talron nodded slowly. “Smashed everyone to dust. The most fun we had around here. ”

Dain’s voice went taut. “Tell us more about this gate.”

“Stone. Spiraled with runes. Cold even in torchlight. We were told to activate it, the adepts, I mean. It was always the adepts. When it flared to life, he stepped through.”

Nyssa’s voice was barely audible. “Who?”

Talron’s voice dropped into reverence. “He was our commander. Until then.”

“What’s his name?” Dain asked.

Talron’s eyes flicked to him. “His name doesn't matter now. When he walked back through the gate, he called himself something new.” He paused before saying, “Sythrael Korr.” He said it and looked immediately toward the door, as if speaking it aloud might bring something. 

“Sythrael Korr.” The name landed heavy, like a dropped blade. None of them spoke.

Talron went on, voice quieter now. “He was… changed. Taller. Paler. His voice echoed wrong, like stone underwater. He looked at us, and we knelt. We couldn’t not. Since then… we’ve followed. Everywhere we went we could hear that voice, sounds like it was coming from every wall.”

“Through walls," Ash repeated, like he was checking whether he'd heard it right. Ash’s knuckles whitened as he was squeezing his fist tight, not realizing he was doing it. 

Talron nodded, something haunted in his eyes. “Not aloud. Not always. He speaks like a whisper behind your thoughts. And sometimes… the stones listen too. He sees. He knows. He commands.”

“And you obey?” Aldrek asked.

Talron’s lips twitched. “He doesn’t ask twice.”

Dain stepped forward. “Was he the voice we heard? In the Tower, during the fight?”

Talron froze.

“You heard him, too?” he whispered. “Then I’m not mad. I’m not alone. Never knew for sure if it was just me.”

“What is he?” Nyssa asked.

Talron’s voice turned strange, half fear, half reverence. “He’s become something more. A vessel. A herald. He said the crown is broken, and we are to pave the road for the Uncrowned King.”

“The what?” Ash muttered.

“He is coming,” Talron whispered. “Korr rules in his name. Builds his army. Calls his beast.”

“What beast?” Dain asked.

"I'd guess it's what they dug up down there. Bound like stone. The Warbeast from before the Divide era. From what I was told, they are waking one. Soon it will stand guard over Stormhaven.”

“And who commands them?” Aldrek pressed.

Talron’s lips cracked into a broken smile. “The one named in shadow. The general you don’t see. The voice that waits. He watches. Waits for the final bell.”

“Why stay with them?” Ash asked. “You’ve got a brain. Could’ve walked.”

Talron’s gaze flicked again to the dead Adept.

“That was my brother,” he said. “I followed him into the fire. Watched him pledge to the Adept Lords. Swore I’d protect him. I did. Until the end.”

Ash said nothing. Ash looked at the body. Then at Talron. Then away. Some things didn't need commentary. 

Talron looked down, breath catching. “I bled trying to raise him when he fell. That’s when I knew… whatever’s left of me… It’s not enough.”

A silence settled.

Aldrek stepped forward at last, voice firm but kind. “Then maybe it’s time to remember who you were. Not what they made you be.”

He laid his hand gently on Talron’s head. “You are still a man. The choices are yours.”

Talron blinked at him. Then… he laughed. Not the dry, tired wheeze from before. This was broken. Hollow.

“A man?” he said, shoulders trembling. “I’m no man. I’m a puppet… a ruin… something that remembers being whole.”

His voice dipped low. “Since the day he walked through that gate, I haven’t had a real thought of my own. And now I’ve spoken. I’ve failed him.”

He looked at the ceiling as if it might split open. “I’m surprised he hasn’t killed me already.”

Nyssa took a step back.

“He doesn’t need to be here,” Talron whispered. “He never does.”

Aldrek crouched beside him, resting a hand on the side of his head. “Maybe I can help break that hold. Maybe you’ve already started.”

Talron didn’t flinch. But his eyes fluttered.

Aldrek focused, his fingers glowing faintly white as he probed with care, finding something wrapped tight like chains around the man’s mind. Solid. Slick.

But fractured.

“There are cracks,” Aldrek said softly. “There’s still hope for you.”

Talron turned his gaze toward the dead Adept, his brother.

“I just wish there was hope for both of us.”

He looked back at the group. “I need to go home. I need to tell our mother. I need to make this right. It was meant to be a strike. Noise and symbols. But now…”

He shuddered. “Now it’s something else. Something worse.”

And in that quiet, none of them disagreed.

Dain crouched again, voice low but direct. “We’re here for the governor’s family. Where are they?”

Talron blinked slowly, like it took effort just to focus. “They moved them. Said the cells weren’t... suitable. Too damp. Too dark. So they put them in the south wing on the thirty-second floor. Diplomatic suites. More comfortable. More public.”

Nyssa’s breath hitched. “And when are they set to hang them?”

“Seventh bell. Grand courtyard. Red banners. Gallows already built.” A cold silence followed.

Dain stood and paced a few steps. “We can’t fight our way up thirty-two floors.”

Ash muttered, “Not in the shape we’re in.”

Talron’s head tipped back against the stone. “I can help you,” he said hoarsely. “I know a way up… but you’ve got to do something for me.”

Dain narrowed his eyes. “What?”

“You let me go. Help me get out of here. I’ve given you this much already. I’m dead either way, but I’d rather not die in this damned tower.” A long pause passed between them. Then Dain nodded once. “Talk.”

Talron coughed, steadied himself against the floor. “Service stairs. Old dwarven cut. Hidden behind the kitchens, down at the lowest level. It was for the Thane who ruled this place before. Private access to the twenty-eighth floor. Doesn’t reach the suites, but… close enough.”

Aldrek gave Dain a quiet nod.

Dain nodded back. “Heal him enough to move.”

Aldrek returned to Talron and laid a hand on his chest, sending a controlled wave of white light through his ribs and spine. The man winced, but the worst of his tremors eased.

Nyssa stood, voice sharp now. “Even if we make it up... how do we get out again?”

Dain rubbed his jaw, thinking. “We improvise." 

He turned to Talron. "Supplies. Anywhere nearby?" 

Talron gave a slight nod. “Fifteenth floor, south corridor. Second door on the left. They used it as a storage annex for the old barracks. Should still have weapons, rope, maybe some other thing. I've only been there once before.”

Dain looked to the others. “We’ll check it.”

Then he straightened, glancing down at Talron. “And there's still the matter of you.”

Ash continued, “He said if he didn’t report back, they’d send someone. Probably come through that door we’ve got barricaded. We should move soon; it's been quiet for too long.”

Nyssa’s expression darkened. “Then we need a way out that doesn’t walk straight into the company.”

She looked around the blackened chamber, then toward the far corridor. “The way we came in is sealed. So unless one of you can bore through stone…”

She let the silence linger.

Ash turned, eyes narrowing. “What about the screamer? That bastard had to come from somewhere.”

Nyssa paused, considering. “You’re right. He got in here somehow. Maybe there’s another passage we haven’t seen.”

Ash’s gaze slid toward the hallway. “We saw him come from the warden’s office.”

They all looked toward the shattered remains of the man’s body, twisted and broken, half-crushed beneath a splintered support beam near the eastern archway.

Ash stepped past without flinching.

“Let’s check it out,” he said. “If there’s another way in, then there's a way out.”

And without another word, they moved as one, toward the warden’s office, and whatever secrets it might hold.

They shut the warden’s door behind them with a heavy clunk. The door had three locks on it. Ash slid home the top bolt, then the middle, then the bottom, thick steel bars that locked the door flush into its stone frame.

“That’ll hold longer than the barricade,” Nyssa muttered, pressing her palm flat to the wood. “Not forever. But long enough.”

They all knew the front barricade wouldn’t last. 

Dain turned and scanned the office. “Everyone, spread out. Look for anything out of place, book, lamp, tile. Something triggered that hidden door.”

Ash gave a tired grunt and started near the desk, fingers brushing past ledgers and a cracked inkwell. Aldrek took the shelves by the wall, scanning spine after spine of old ward records. Nyssa crouched beside a locked cabinet, testing its hinges with a dagger tip.

Talron was lowered into a wide-backed chair near the corner, still bound, still bruised, his expression unreadable. They’d kept the bindings tight, for his sake as much as theirs. Anyone who saw them needed to believe he was a prisoner.

Ten minutes passed. Then fifteen.

It was Ash who found it, a raised groove beneath the edge of the desk. He pressed it, and with a low click, a seam appeared in the stone wall behind the bookcase. A panel slid aside to reveal a cramped passage just wide enough for a man to squeeze through.

Dain stepped forward and eyed the opening. “That’s our path.”

They slipped into the passage one by one. The panel shut behind them with a final thunk. Ash went last and broke the release mechanism with the butt of his dagger, sealing off the way. Ensuring that no one could follow.

The passage slanted upward, dusty and narrow, eventually opening into a forgotten storeroom, Basement Level One. Crates and sacks lined the walls in musty rows. A door ahead led into the main corridor.

They moved quietly now. Talron took point.

“I used to ditch assignments all the time,” he murmured. “This way’s always been quiet. Too far from the officer’s quarters for them to care. That’s how I knew.”

“You weren’t a true believer,” Nyssa said.

“No,” Talron admitted. “I joined for my brother. That was the only cause I cared about.”

They crossed the main floor without incident, ducking through unused side halls and cold storage alcoves. Talron led them up to the Grand Floor and slipped them through a side entrance into the vast, echoing kitchen.

The smell of long-spoiled meat lingered. "There," he said, pointing to a crack in the tiles near the hearth. He stepped down hard. A faint grind echoed through the stone, and a seam split open near the larder, revealing a spiral stairwell descending into darkness and climbing steeply upward. "It goes both ways.” 

Dain gave a nod. “Stay here. Just out of sight.”

Talron nodded. “About halfway up. I’ll tuck into the shadows.”

He started up, then paused and looked back. “Wait.” They turned. “You asked before if there’s another way out. I don’t know for sure. I’ll check below.”

Dain narrowed his eyes. “You sure?”

Talron shrugged. “I’ll be careful. Just… give me a minute. I’ll see what I find.”

He began to descend into the lower levels.

“Oh,” he called over his shoulder, “remember the supply room? Should be on the 15th. Halfway down the corridor, left-hand side.”

He disappeared into the dark.

Dain turned to the others. “All right. We climb.”

And with that, they climbed. Nyssa's fingers found the hilt of her longest blade before she'd taken the first step.
