Market Street had been swallowed alive.
Harmon and Weslin stepped into it together, and for a moment neither spoke, the noise and color pressing in from all sides. This was not the usual feast day energy, the king's birthday crowds, the tournament week revelry, the high-born wedding processions that spilled out of the cathedral and into the market for a day. Those Weslin knew. This was something else. The city had been preparing for this for months, and it showed in ways that the regular feast days never quite managed.
The buildings had not just been cleaned. They had been transformed. Fresh paint on every storefront, not the quick coat of a landlord trying to impress for a week, but real work, careful work, the kind that said Opindale knew it was being looked at by people whose opinions carried weight across six kingdoms. Banners of deep blue and black with gold tassels stretched between every post, the city's colors flying with a precision that someone with authority had personally overseen. The cobblestones had been swept twice. Weslin had never seen the cobblestones swept twice.
The crowd itself was different. The regulars were there, the locals who knew how to work a feast crowd for extra coin, the merchants who doubled their prices and smiled while doing it, the children darting between legs. But woven through all of it were faces that did not belong to Opindale. Diplomats in foreign cuts of cloth, moving with the careful awareness of people in a city that is not their own. Delegates with small entourages, speaking in languages Weslin could not name. Merchants from the island cities with their elaborate hats and their habit of standing still in moving crowds, watching everything. Sailors from ports so distant their ships were a curiosity at the docks, drawing crowds of their own.
At the far end of the square, a fire-breather sent a plume of blue flame skyward, and the crowd roared. Weslin watched it the way you watch something you have seen a hundred times, with mild appreciation and no real surprise. He had grown up in this district. Every alley, every vendor, every loose stone in this road. The fire-breather had been doing the same trick since Weslin was nine years old. He moved through the crowd the way water moves through stone, not because he was in a hurry, but because he knew where everything was before it was there.
Harmon watched him and felt the ring heavy on his finger.
Near the heart of the district, a wide clearing had been made in front of the old stone courtyard, and the King's charity feast was taking shape. Long tables were being set, iron cauldrons already in position, and the first of the beggars had gathered at the edges with the patience of people who had learned exactly how long things took. The King's own kitchen staff would bring the food. Fresh bread, barrels of stew, fruit, and cheese were carried out in full view of every foreign dignitary who happened to pass. It was a good city. A city that remembered its poor even on its grandest day. Harmon had always thought so. He also knew it was deliberate. Edric understood what it meant to be watched.
The pact re-signing only came around once a decade, and it rotated between the kingdoms with a stake in it. Ten years ago, it had been held in Aldoria, in the great hall at Stormhold. Ten years before that, somewhere else. Today it was Opindale's turn, and the city was performing accordingly, every stone and banner and scrubbed storefront a statement to the delegations who had come from across the world to see whether this kingdom was worth the ink on the treaty.
Most of the crowd was here for the celebration. Weslin was here for the wonder of it. Harmon was here because somewhere in this press of foreign faces and loose coin and diplomatic goodwill, there were people who were not here for the pact at all, and he had spent enough years in rooms like this one to know how to spot them.
But that was not today's work.
Today's work was at the end of this road, in a bakery that smelled of warm bread and everything Harmon was about to disturb.
He fell into step beside the boy. "Do you think your father will welcome the news?"
“I am not sure. He has always been so protective of my mother and me. It took both of us to convince him to let me take the position in the palace.” 
“This is understandable. Are you his only child?”
“Yes, sir.”
“Then that is the sign of a good protective father. If this is something you want, wholeheartedly, then I will speak with him. So I will ask you plainly now, do you want this? Know there is no reprisal if you say no. This will be your choice.”
“Sir, can I be honest?”
“Always.”
“I do not understand why you chose me,” Weslin admitted. “I was only there today because someone fell ill.” His breath caught, then steadied. “I was only there today because Gus gave me a shot. I’m usually just an extra hand to help with the bed maids or the kitchen staff. So I don’t completely understand.”
Harmon’s expression turned serious. “I won’t lie to you. What I’m asking is a heavy responsibility,” he said, voice gentle but firm. His words land slowly, tested. “I chose you because you did not quit. I know knocking over that suit of armor was an accident, I’m not such an old fool as to think otherwise. But I watched you push yourself, out of breath yet determined to finish your task. That’s why you’re special,” Harmon said, resting a steady hand on the boy’s shoulder. “I believe it comes from how your father raised you. I know him, and I know he’s a good man.” Harmon paused to let his words sink in, meeting the boy’s eyes. “But none of this matters unless it’s what you truly want. So I’ll ask you again. Do you want this?”
“I know this will sound childish, but I have always wanted to go on adventures. See more than this one city. Be more than the baker’s son.”
“This is no storybook, son. It is work. Hard work. You will be my squire, but you will be more than that as well. You will be more of an aide-de-camp. I will teach you the skills you will need to complete your duties before I get into any wild dreams of weapons play. Understood? If your head is filled with stories, this is not for you.”
“I do understand. That is why I believe that is something I want to do. I want to be more, someone my father can be proud of.”
Harmon stopped him and bent over to look him in the eyes. “You are someone,” Harmon said. “I know it as surely as I know my own name that your father is already proud of you. Never doubt that. He may be hard, but he is proud of you.”
Both of them smiled at each other and then continued their march to the bakery. The crowd did not get any smaller, and they worked hard not to run into anyone. 
Just beyond the main area of the market was the road to the docks. From their higher position, it was a clear view of the entire Westside dock. It stretched toward the clear waters of the river. Trade ships and fishing boats rocked gently in their moorings. The great stone piers, built generations ago, jutted into the waves, lined with cranes and warehouses that smelled of damp wood and brine. It was here that goods flowed into the city, and with them, whispers of foreign lands, rumors of war, and the quiet dealings of those who thrived in the shadows. The closer they got to the docks, the more chaotic the revelry was. Sailors who had just made landfall mingled with locals and tourists alike, their coin flowing as freely as the ale. Taverns overflowed, their doors propped open to let out bursts of laughter, the scent of spiced rum, and the occasional stumbling patron. The stone piers, usually a place of work and barter, had transformed into an impromptu promenade, lined with vendors selling skewers of roasted fish, glazed nuts, and sugared fruits.
At the heart of this whirlwind sat The Silver Spoon Bakery, nestled between a cobbler’s shop and a spice merchant. Unlike its neighbors, it wasn’t adorned with bright banners or excessive decorations. It didn’t need to be. The smell of warm bread and sweet cakes poured from its doors like an unspoken invitation, cutting through the heavier scents of the street. A line had formed outside, a mix of regular patrons and curious foreigners drawn by the promise of something fresh from the oven.
“Dada should be in the back with Mom. Elaine will be up front. Sometimes she brings her granddaughter, Mira. I can’t wait to tell them.” Weslin broke into a full run, heading to the back door to avoid the crowd. 
Harmon slowed to a halt, taking in the sight of the Silver Spoon Bakery as Weslin dashed off. The unassuming shop of stone and timber glowed warmly against the bustle of the street, its open door like an inviting hearth. But to Harmon, that friendly glow felt more like the glare of judgment. The bakery loomed before him like a wall, an immovable obstacle between the life this family knew and the duty that would soon shatter it.
He had stood fearless before warlords in a gilded hall and faced sorcerers who could unravel a man’s mind with a whisper. Yet this simple building, with its shuttered windows and the comforting scent of fresh bread, held his next steps in a pause. Behind it was a man who had once spilled more blood than most armies. Now a humble baker, a father. A man who had buried his past, and Harmon had come to disturb that grave. 
He stood rooted to the cobblestones, fist clenched at his side, weighed down by the knowledge of what he was about to do. He was about to take this man's only son into a war that had not yet declared itself, for reasons that had seemed clear in a king's chamber and felt considerably less so in front of a bakery.  
He exhaled through his nose, forcing himself forward. By the time Harmon rounded the corner to the back entrance, the boy was already standing before the man who would decide his fate.
Mjakazi stood in the doorway, framed by the flickering glow of the kitchen’s hearth. He was dressed in a simple tunic, dusted with flour, his sleeves rolled up to reveal forearms still thick with the strength of his past life. His hands were wrapped in fresh bandages. Harmon knew those hands had once wielded a sword that could carve through men like paper.
The years had softened him, but not erased him.
Mjakazi’s white eyes, those haunting, unnatural orbs, settled on Harmon. There was no anger, no immediate threat, but there was something worse. Recognition. The knowing look of a man who understood exactly why an old ghost had come knocking.
Harmon straightened, shoving down the weight in his chest, and stepped forward. 
Weslin was already tugging at his father’s sleeve, proudly pointing out the formal page’s tunic he wore for the feast day. As Harmon stepped up beside him, the boy beamed with excitement. “Dada, look!” Weslin exclaimed. “This is the knight I told you about. He says he’s going to make me his squire! Isn’t that great news?”
Mjakazi’s eyes shifted from his son to the knightly figure of Harmon. His gaze was wary and unyielding. “Is that so?” he asked quietly, a hard edge underlying the words.
Harmon inclined his head respectfully. “Yes, sir. I’ve come to ask for your permission to have Weslin serve as my squire.”
For a moment, nothing stirred. Mjakazi’s voice cut through the heavy air of late afternoon, sharp as a hammer on iron, the sun dipping west but not yet gone. “No.”
Weslin flinched as if struck. “What? Why?” he burst out, staring up at his father in disbelief.
Mjakazi’s broad form filled the doorway. “I am not going to send my only son off to die at this man’s side,” he growled, his tone final.
“Please, Dada,” Weslin pleaded, desperation breaking into his voice. “I-I can do this. It could be good for me!”
“You don’t understand, son,” Mjakazi said, tempering his voice, though resolve still hardened it. “You’re not ready for the world beyond these streets. I know what’s out there.” His white eyes narrowed. “And I won’t throw you to those wolves.”
Weslin’s fists balled at his sides. “I am nearly fourteen,” he argued, his voice cracking, then he forced it steady. “No guild has called my name. This might be my only chance to be more than,” looking down, he said, “Than a baker’s son.”
Mjakazi drew a sharp breath, battling his own emotions. “What is wrong with being the baker’s son? No, don’t answer. My answer is final, Weslin,” he said sternly. “You will respect my decision, do you hear me?”
Tears of frustration brimmed in the boy’s eyes. In a last burst of defiance, Weslin turned toward Harmon.“Sir Harmon, please!” Weslin begged, his hands shaking. "Sir Harmon." His voice cracked clean through. "Please. You can fix this. Just tell him you need me. That is all. Just say the words. Please."
Harmon raised his hands in a calming gesture. “Weslin,” he said softly, “I gave my word. That holds. I will not force his hand, nor will I allow you to disrespect his authority. If he says no, then that is the end of it.”
Weslin stood trembling, his dreams crashing down around him. “No… Dada, you can’t—!” he started, voice choked with anguish.
“Enough.” Mjakazi’s command came out quiet but firm. He jutted his chin toward the door. “Inside. Now.”
The boy looked as if he might protest again, his face flushed, tears spilling over, but the resolve in his father’s gaze brokered no argument. Weslin swallowed hard, his eyes darting between Harmon and Mjakazi in hurt disbelief. Then, with a strangled cry, he spun on his heel and fled into the bakery. The door swung shut behind him with a slam, cutting off the sound of his sobs.
Mjakazi’s rigid stance eased slightly once the boy was gone. He lifted his pale eyes to Harmon. “It’s been a long time,” he said gruffly. “You look older.”
Harmon managed a faint smile. “The years have a way of catching up with me, I am afraid.”
Mjakazi crossed his arms over his flour-dusted tunic. "If this was about me," Mjakazi said quietly, which was worse than if he had raised his voice, "you do not come through him." He looked at Harmon the way a man looks at something he thought he knew. "You've hurt the boy. So why are you here, after all this time? It’s not like you cared what became of me. You left me,” Mjakazi said. “After everything.”
Harmon’s jaw tightened. “After everything…” he echoed quietly. “Mjakazi, I risked my life to get you out. I brought you here to give you a chance at a new life.”
“And then you vanished,” Mjakazi shot back, his voice low and accusing. “Yes, you saved me, only to abandon me. For all you knew, I could’ve picked up my sword the day after and gone right back to being the monster I was.” His white eyes flashed. “And you wouldn’t have been there to stop it.”
Harmon stepped closer, meeting the other man’s glare. “I had faith you wouldn’t,” he said. “Just because I wasn’t by your side doesn’t mean I wasn’t looking out for you.”
Mjakazi let out a hard, barking laugh. “Looking out? I was alone, Harmon. You were the only person I knew in this land, and then you were gone. You didn’t see the darkness I went through. The nightmares, the guilt, the rage, I faced all of that without the brother-in-arms who promised me a new start.”
“Do you truly think the fortunes that found you here were mere coincidence?” Harmon replied, his voice gentle. “Who do you think arranged for Adien to take you in at his tavern, all those years ago? Who paid your keep until you found your feet? And the dockyard job that led you to meet Thim Hather, the baker.” He paused for emphasis, “Did you think it happened by luck?” He held Mjakazi’s stunned gaze. “I set those things in motion, my friend. I paid him to take you on as an apprentice. I wasn’t equipped to guide you through your nightmares and grief, I’ll admit that. I did the only thing I could think to do. I made sure you had opportunities for honest work and people around who cared. I never left you to suffer. I was there in the only ways I knew how.”
Mjakazi’s mouth had fallen open. The memories came slowly, the way they do when you have to relearn what you thought you already knew. The baker who gave him work without question. The room was waiting when he needed it. The dockyard foreman who seemed to expect him. His hands curled in his lap, the bandages creasing at the knuckles. He cleared his throat, but nothing useful came out. He tried again.  “All this time,” he says, and the words come rough. “You were helping me from the shadows?”
Harmon offered a tentative smile. “I did what I could. And when I saw you with Zeriah, happy, building a family, I knew you would be fine. You had finally found peace.”
A faint, crooked grin tugged at Mjakazi’s lips at the mention of his wife. “Zeriah,” he repeated, softening. “Did you know she’s Nyahani’s daughter?”
Harmon blinked. “Nyahani… the healer from back then? I didn’t know.”
Mjakazi nodded. “The very same. After your ‘shadow work’ and Nyahani’s kindness got me through those first few years, I kept tabs on them, on her and her little girl. Then one day in the market, I recognized that little girl, all grown up.” A light entered his voice as he continued. “When I realized who Zeriah was, it felt like fate. We talked for hours… and, well, you can guess the rest.” He spread his bandaged hands in a gesture of wonder. “Somehow, that sweet girl I remembered became my wife. She saved me in her own way.”
"No wonder," Harmon said quietly. "A man can endure almost anything if someone is waiting for him on the other side of it." He hesitated, then asked, “Does Zeriah know… about your past?”
“Every dark detail,” Mjakazi replied. “And she still loves me. She saw the good in me when I couldn’t see it myself. Through every nightmare, every fit of anger, she stayed. I thank God every day for her.” His voice caught, and he drew a steadying breath. “I’ve spent thirty years trying to become the man she believes I am.”
Harmon felt a sincere smile cross his face. “You’ve done well for yourself, Mjakazi. Better than many men with far fewer scars.” For a moment, the two stood in amicable silence, the weight of old ghosts finally lifting between them. Then Harmon squared his shoulders, recalling the true purpose of his visit. “Now,” he said quietly, “I think you know I didn’t come all this way just to dredge up old memories. There’s something else.”
Mjakazi fixed his visitor with a steady, knowing look. “Aye. There is.” He straightened, bracing himself. “So, why are you here, Sir Harmon?”
Harmon drew a breath. “I’m truly here to ask for your permission to take Weslin as my squire,” he said plainly.
Mjakazi’s eyes widened in disbelief, then narrowed. “Really.” He pauses to take in the information before continuing. "So that’s it,” he said, voice rough. “After all these years, you come back to my door, not for me, but for my son? No. My answer is still no.”
“I’m not here to collect a debt,” Harmon replied firmly. “If anything, I owe you. I learned he was your son just before I made the trip here." 
“Then why him? Of all children, why Weslin?”
Harmon held his gaze. “Because I felt it the moment I met him. A spark,” Harmon gave Mjakazi a knowing look. “He is not just any boy, is he, Mjakazi? Tell me. Have you had him tested?”
For the first time, Mjakazi lowered his head and shook it.
“Why not?” asked Harmon.
A stiffness overtook Mjakazi, “Because I was afraid. Because I knew what it would say."
“You know how dangerous that is? Especially for him," said Harmon. 
“Yes, I know," snapped Mjakazi, his tone lessened when he continued, " God, I know.”
Harmon exhales once, controlled. “I understand.” 
His voice came low. “Tell me he is not like me.”
Harmon stepped forward. “Has he ever shown signs of it?” Harmon asked with a hint of worry in his tone. “Of being like you?”
Mjakazi’s answer came quickly, honest, and almost desperate. “No. And thank the Creator for that. He is a happy boy. A good boy. Light where I was in shadow.”
“Then let me help keep him that way,” Harmon urged. “You’ve done your part in raising him in peace. But peace doesn’t last,” Harmon said quietly, eyes never leaving Mjakazi’s. “You know it as well as I do. Some would twist him, use him as their weapon. That would double if they ever learned who his father was. They would shape him into something you’ve fought to protect him from.”
Mjakazi’s large hands curled into fists, his breath coming heavier. He turned toward the bakery door as though he could shield it with his body alone. “I will not let them take him. I will not see him ruined.”
“You won’t,” Harmon said quietly. “Not while I live. But, Mjakazi, war is stirring. I know you can feel it. Enemies, we both know, are gathering. Just like in your time before, the events that changed you. Weslin doesn’t need to become a soldier, but he does need the chance to grow into the man he’s meant to be. With training. With protection. He will be better prepared to face it, rather than let it sweep him up in the current.”
Mjakazi shut his eyes and let out a long, ragged breath. When he opened them, pain lingered there. “He’s not made for war, Harmon. I tried so hard to keep him gentle.”
“And you succeeded,” Harmon said softly. “But you can’t keep him untouched forever. Nor keep what he is from him any longer, either. Better he walks forward under your blessing, with my shield between him and the world, than to stumble into it alone when it comes for him anyway.”
Silence stretched, heavy as the stones of the street. Finally, Mjakazi spoke, voice rough as gravel. “This will break Zeriah’s heart. And mine. But if what you say is true, I cannot keep him blind to it. I wanted to be a better father to him than mine to me. Maybe if he were, I would be what I am.”
“I know, and you are,” Harmon whispered. “But I give you my word, I will guard him as if he were my own blood. And when the time comes, I will bring him home.”
Mjakazi’s shoulders sagged. The fight drained out of him, leaving only the ache of a father who loved his son too much to let go, and too much to keep holding on. His eyes shifted to the door, to the laughter and life within. “So,” he rasped after a long pause, “You’re leaving before dawn?”
Harmon nodded. “Before first light.”
Mjakazi closed his eyes, summoning strength. “Give me a moment,” he murmured. “Please.”
Harmon bowed his head. “Of course.”
Harmon stepped back and gave him the silence. Mjakazi looked at his feet, then at the sky, where the sun was burning low and orange over the rooftops. He turned to face the bakery as though memorizing it. His shoulders rose and fell three times. When he looked back, Harmon only nodded. There was nothing to say that the nod did not already carry.  
Mjakazi stood in the doorway of the bakery, his shadow stretching long across the cobblestones. “Weslin. Zeriah. Come out here.”
Footsteps pattered within, then the boy emerged first, cheeks flushed, eyes red. Zeriah followed, drying her hands on her apron, her eyes searching her husband’s face, knowing at first look what was about to happen. Her eyes were already starting to fill with tears.
Mjakazi’s voice faltered before he found his strength. “Your path is changing, Weslin. Sir Harmon has made a reasonable argument for you to be his squire.”
Weslin’s mouth dropped open, his eyes shining as though the world had suddenly cracked wide and poured its light on him. “Truly?” he whispered, then looked from his father to Harmon. 
“Yes, my son. Truly,” Mjakazi answered, struggling to hold back his emotions.
Zeriah froze. Her gaze darted from the knight’s steady stance to the boy’s eager face, then back to her husband. “Mjakazi…” she breathed.
He lifted a hand, silencing her gently, though his own throat worked against the words. “I tried to keep him here, safe at the hearth, away from all that I once was. But the world is changing again. We can no longer pretend it won’t reach our door. So I have given my word.”
Zeriah pressed a hand to her mouth, a muffled sob escaping.
Weslin, trembling, stepped forward. “Dada, does this mean…?”
Mjakazi pulled him into his arms, crushing the boy against his chest. His voice shook as he spoke into Weslin’s hair. “It means I am proud of you. More than I can ever say. You are my joy, Weslin. Remember that. Always.”
The boy clung to him, tears streaming, though his smile trembled through them. “Thank you, Dada. I’ll make the most of this. I swear it.”
When at last Mjakazi released him, Zeriah was there. She cupped her son’s face in both hands, her thumbs brushing away the tears. Her eyes shone with her own. “Be a good lad. You are my treasure.” She whispered. “Be brave, but never stop being kind. That is what will keep you whole.” She kissed his brow and pulled him into her arms, holding him as though to etch the memory of him into her bones.
Finally, she let go, her hands slipping away reluctantly. Weslin stepped back toward Harmon, wiping his face but standing taller now, as if the weight of his parents’ love gave him strength.
“You’ve given me more than trust tonight,” Harmon said. His hand gripped Mjakazi’s forearm with old familiarity. “I know what this costs you, brother. I will not waste the gift of your son’s life.”
Harmon bowed his head to Mjakazi and Zeriah both. “He will be guarded with my life,” he said quietly.
Mjakazi managed a stiff nod, his voice hoarse. “Then bring him home. Whole.”
Harmon laid a hand on Weslin’s shoulder. “Come, lad. It’s time.”
As they turned toward the road, the family’s tears glistened in the fading light. Behind them, Mjakazi drew Zeriah into his arms, holding her as though she were the only thing keeping him from breaking apart completely.
They walked into the crowd together, and the feast closed around them like water over a stone. The noise returned, the fire-breather, the merchants, the sailors with their loose coin and looser laughter. None of them knew what had just happened in that doorway. Weslin walked with his chin up and his eyes forward, and Harmon did not speak because there was nothing useful he could say. Some things needed to be carried for a while before they could be put down.
