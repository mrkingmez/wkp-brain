The girl clung to her with wide, dry eyes, not a word spoken since the courtyard. Beside them, Briala sat ramrod straight, not from pride but from duty, guarding the family with her presence alone. Talron rode in the front corner of the cart, silent and stiff, his hands kept where everyone could see them.

Aurelia was wrapped in a heavy blanket near the rear, her knees pulled to her chest, staring at something no one else could see. Occasionally, she muttered to herself, half-nonsense, half-warnings, her voice soft as unraveling thread. Neavin sat close, not touching her, but watching as if willing her to come back from wherever she had gone.

In the second cart, Harmon drove. Aldrek’s unconscious form lay cradled against the back rail, still breathing but unmoving, his armor scuffed and stained. Harmon’s gaze never left the road ahead, his jaw locked, his ring faintly glowing, prepared for anything. He said nothing, but his presence kept the group’s forward momentum from collapsing entirely.

The rest rode on horseback, grouped by instinct more than design.

They split at the old statue roundabout, just before the broken rail district, three teams, three gates, three slim chances to escape the city alive.

Ash and Nyssa rode on either side, guarding the family cart, with Garr still on the reins. Their route hugged the old warehouse quarter, weaving past fire-scarred walls and crumbled archways.

As they passed near the gutted barracks yard. A deep, wet groan echoed through the broken gate.

Nyssa looked around for the source of the noise. When she saw it, her mouth dropped open. Her eyes locked on what was walking down the street towards the main gate, “What in the name of all that is holy is that?” she said.

Garr turned and looked, voice flat. “That’s the Warbeast. That is the kind of problem that outlives the people who made it.” His eyes locked on it. “They are dealing with stuff better left alone. They will learn that some secrets are better left burned,” Garr said, unaware that he was talking out loud.

A cold chill of fear ran up all their spines at the mere presence of that thing. Chains dangled from its massive limbs as it lumbered toward the main gate road. The sigils were lit, all up and down its back. 
The chains weren't binding it. They were remnants. Whatever had once held this thing had broken, and these were what was left of the apparatus. The sigils on its back were not controlling it either. They were lit because it was awake. Those were different things.
Ash kept his mouth shut and kept moving. So did everyone else. The group veered off fast, covering the cart with urgency now sharpened by dread.

Riven and Reau led the westernmost team, looping through the merchant quarter and toward the riverbend stables.

There, they slowed. The townsfolk had changed.

Not just in posture, but in purpose. Men and women who had seen haggling over onions days ago now stood in silent formation, shoulder to shoulder in purple sashes, spears in hand, eyes vacant and shining.

Riven didn't slow the horse. "They didn't need soldiers," he said. "They already had a city full of them." 

"Stormhaven is lost," Reau said. Nothing else needed saying. They rode by in silence, just watching as these people in the distance were gathering their weapons of war. Just... watched and rode on.

Dain rode with Weslin, flanking Harmon’s cart with quiet vigilance as they cut east through the broken fountain plaza.

There, the air shimmered. A ring of a dozen or more Red Cloaks stood in a ring, arms raised in silent unison. They weren’t attacking. They were chanting. Energy arced between them like veins of molten glass, tethering their bodies to an ancient artifact half-buried in the cobbles. As the artifact pulsed, a dome of violet shimmer rose above the city’s heart, stretching wide, wide, wider, until the group realized what they were watching was no attack.

Harmon had not seen adepts channel in concert in his lifetime. Not this lifetime. The frequencies didn't align. They couldn't. And yet the evidence was in front of him, and it came down to whatever was buried in the cobbles. The adepts were not the source. They were the conduit. Something else was the source. He had known that distinction once, a very long time ago, in a different context, under a different sky. He did not enjoy being reminded of it. 

Light surged outward like breath held too long, and then exhaled.

“A ward?” Said Harmon in complete surprise.

A dome, invisible until it flickered, stretched wide over the center of the city. Harmon looked at Dain and asked, “Do you think it is a protection ward?”

Dain muttered a curse under his breath. “No,” he said. “I believe it is a prison.” Harmon said nothing, but his stare at it was as if to burn a hole in it.
Lightning lit the figure on the palace balcony. Cloaked in violet. The hint of a crown on its brow. Alone on the highest point of a city that had bent its knee without a war.
Harmon stopped breathing for two seconds. He was not aware of it. Then he exhaled. And the discipline came back, the armor he had been wearing since he was someone else, in a different era, under a sky that no longer existed. He put it on.
He had not seen that crown in a very long time. He had hoped, with the specific and private hope of a man who knew what the word meant over centuries, that he never would again. 
Not Korr. Korr was a herald. This was the thing that sent heralds.
He did not look away. He did not speak. 
Dain, riding beside him, tracked his gaze and saw the figure. "Harmon."
"I see him," Harmon said. Two words. Steady as stone. Giving nothing.
But his ring pulsed once, faint and cold, and that was the only thing in thirty years that had made it do that without being asked. 
Not a usurper. Stormhaven had not been taken by ambition. It had been taken by something patient enough to wait for exactly the right moment and old enough to recognize when that moment arrived. He shook his head. Get clear first. That call comes after, he thought.
He pulled his eyes from the balcony. Ahead. Road. Now. He clicked the horses. "We go," he said. His voice was even. It was always even.
Then the bell struck, seventh bell, first chime, deep and sonorous, rolling through the bones of the city.

Nyssa turned her head toward the sound, wind tugging at the hood around her face. She rode just ahead of Garr’s cart, one hand on the saddle horn, the other resting near the hilt of her dagger. She didn’t look back at the tower. She couldn’t. In the cart behind her, the family huddled, Lady Briala murmuring to the children, her voice barely audible. Talron hadn't spoken in miles.

Nyssa chewed her lip. They’d saved them, for now. But Stormhaven was gone, and whatever lay ahead, whatever they’d stirred, was watching.

The bell struck again, the second chime. Ash squinted through the mist as he guided his horse along the southern trail. He rode alone, save for the whisper of fire in the back of his mind. The path ahead split into brush and shallow forest. No one followed yet, but the feeling hadn’t left his spine, the burn of being hunted.

They’d done it. Pulled the thread. Broke the spell. Now came the unraveling, and it wouldn’t stop here. Ash rubbed his thumb along his scar. This city won’t be the last, he thought grimly.
And in his mind, the voice of the flame whispered. They’re waking up.

Third chime. Weslin gripped the reins tighter. His horse moved with a steady rhythm, but his thoughts scattered like leaves. They hadn’t told him what happened. Only that they were heading east. That Aurelia was safe. That Aldrek still breathed. That they’d done what needed doing.

But as the tower receded behind him, a shadow clung to the corners of his vision.

He heard the whisper again.
Not from the shard. Not from Aurelia. From something older, something buried, and watching.
And it knew his name.

Fourth chime. In Harmon’s cart, the wind snapped at the corners of the tarp. Aldrek stirred, groaning faintly. Neavin sat cross-legged beside him, eyes closed, reaching for the last echo of his spirit hawk’s sight.

Harmon didn’t speak.
Didn’t blink.
He watched the road stretch ahead, hands steady on the reins.

The crown he’d seen in the shimmer of the ward burned into his memory.
Not Korr. Not yet.
But something worse.

He would need to send word. They all would.
But not tonight.
Tonight, they ran.

Fifth chime.

The river bend came into view. One group peeled westward, hooves muffled in soft loam. Another veered south, weaving between broken hedges and orchard fields. The third angled northeast, cutting past a line of watchtowers gone cold. Above them, the ward glowed faint violet against the sky. Silent. Watching.
Dain kept pace with Harmon's cart, one eye on the road and one on the bundle of robes and armor in the back. Aldrek saved them by summoning a divine sentinel with a limp arm and then taking a bolt down a shaft for the privilege. Whatever came next, Dain was going to make sure the man lived long enough to have opinions about it.
It was the only thought he had for the next few miles, and he was committing to it.
Sixth chime.

Riven kept his eyes forward, the tower’s glow a faint smear in the distance. “We tell them everything?”

Reau’s answer came without hesitation. “Only what they can carry.”

The rest could wait.
The rest would keep them up at night.

Seventh chime.

They did not speak.
They did not stop.
They rode beneath the sound of a fallen city still echoing through the hills, each heart heavy with what they’d seen. And what was still to come.
