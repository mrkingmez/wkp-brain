They hadn't dared return to the safehouse after splitting up. Too risky. Eyes were everywhere now, and the city was no longer asleep.

Their breath clouded in the chill. The only sound was the whisper of their gear shifting and the scrape of Harmon’s blade as he used its tip to draw in the dirt between them.

Harmon finished the sketch. Three entrances. Two patrol rotations. Distances he'd already walked twice in his head. In the dirt, it looked small. He knew it wasn't. "The goal is to move before the city finishes waking up. Chaos favors speed. 

Reau shifted slightly behind the others. The movement was small, but the stillness around them made it loud.

"I'm not going with either team," he said. No one moved. Even Ash, who moved constantly, stopped. Harmon looked up sharply. "What?"

“I know it is.”

Harmon’s brow furrowed. “Even if you knew where it was, which you don’t, how would you get in?”

“I do know,” Riven said, stepping forward from the edge of the circle. “Merrick’s manor, east ridge of Ember Hill. Big stone estate with a slate-colored spire. I remember the dog statues at the gate. The inside's changed over the years, but the outside? Still the same.”

Riven smirked faintly. “Thieving days. I used to run messages for someone who worked the manor.”

“Most likely,” Reau agreed. “But if there’s even a chance my sword’s there, I have to take it. If everything starts unraveling, and it might, I need to be ready. That sword’s part of me. Without it, even if you take off this collar, I’ll still be crippled.”

Silence stretched again.

Riven raised a hand. “We’ll link back up. You’ll need a fallback point, right?”

Harmon hesitated only a heartbeat.

Riven dropped to one knee, cleared a patch of dirt next to Harmon’s, and began drawing a second layout. “Here. Blackcap Alley. The smuggler’s route I used years ago. It connects to the river path and two open-air grain silos, flat ground for wagons, and is enclosed enough for cover. You can get there with carts.”

“Speaking of,” Harmon said, glancing at Weslin. “You’ll need to buy those. Two carts. Doesn’t have to be pretty. Just fast, quiet, and big enough for wounded and supplies. Bring them to Blackcap Alley.”

Weslin paled but nodded once, already calculating routes and coin.

Dain looked around. “All right, so we’re splitting three ways now? Merrick’s house, the tower, and the wagon run.”

Garr grunted. “It's what's got to happen. We're past choosing and past waiting.”

Aurelia stepped forward, pressing her fingers gently to her temple. Her face was pale, drawn, the brightness behind her eyes dimmed to something quieter. “Let me try to link us again. If I can just….” She stopped. Winced. Tried again. A quiet grunt escaped her lips, and her shoulders sagged.

“It’s no good,” she whispered. “It feels like a smooth wall. Nothing to get a grip on. I can’t break through either. Something powerful is blocking it.”

Silence rippled again through the group.

Harmon’s jaw flexed. “No communication, then. All we have are verbal and hand signs. Stay visual when possible.”

“Anything else?” Harmon asked, scanning them.

He looked over. “Reau, Riven. You’re on the sword. No detours.”

Riven gave a lazy two-finger salute. “We’ll be ghosts.”

Reau tightened the wrappings around his collar, voice low. “We will go now, then.”

Riven gave Nyssa a wink. “Try not to bring the tower down until we’re done.”

Nyssa rolled her eyes. “Try not to die.”

Harmon began gesturing to his sketched map. “Here’s how we divide, Garr, Ash, Dain, Brother Aldrek, and Nyssa. You’ll breach the tower. Use the building layouts we pulled to slip in. Be sure to do a quick check to make sure no changes have been made, and improve the plan. Then improvise the rest. We’ll adjust on the ground.”

Ash rolled his shoulder and offered a toothy grin. “ So, the fallback is Blackcap Alley if everything goes to hell?”

“We meet at Blackcap Alley,” Harmon said. “No matter what.”

“And if we don’t make it?” Nyssa asked, her voice light but her eyes serious.

Harmon looked at her. Then at all of them.

“Then the rest of us finish the job anyway. I never said this would be easy or promised safety.”

A weight settled over the group. Not fear. Not yet. But gravity.

“This is the big one,” Dain said softly. “No do-overs. No backup. We pull them out today, or we fail.”

Brother Aldrek traced a slow circle over the hammer etched into the edge of his armor. “May the Light make a path.”

“And may we be fast enough to follow it,” Aurelia added, eyes closed.

The group stood in near-silence. The air smelled of cold ash and rising bread from some unseen oven. A dog barked faintly in the distance.

Then Harmon clapped him hands once.

“Weslin, go get those carts and loop wide around Ember Hill. The rest of us move as soon as the streets are light enough to see our feet. That tower doesn’t wait.”

He gave them all a once-over. “You start this, you'd better finish it. Once we step out… we’ve kicked the beehive.”

Dain snorted. “More like a hornet’s nest.”

Ash cracked his knuckles. “Let’s burn the bastards down.”

The group split without a word, slipping into alleys and shadowed streets.

And somewhere in the city’s sleeping bones…

The streets near the upper ward stank of burnt oil and damp stone.

Even after more than a full day inside the city walls, the absence of life still gnawed at them. Not empty, just curated. People moved through the avenues like figures in a puppet show, too quiet, too rehearsed. Merchants wiped counters that didn’t need cleaning. Children walked in perfect lines. Lamplighters ignored cloaked strangers at odd hours.

It scraped the edges of normal. 

Riven tugged his hood lower. “Still wrong. Creeps me out.”

Reau said nothing, his eyes fixed forward, but his posture had changed, taut, flexed, ready. His fingers twitched near the small of his back, as if muscle memory was reaching for a sword that wasn’t there.
They passed a butcher hauling empty crates. He didn't look up. Didn't blink. Just turned the corner and vanished like a dropped coin.
A woman at an open window was combing her hair. The comb moved in exactly the same pass, exactly the same arc, again and again, like a clock mechanism. She was looking at something outside the window that wasn't there.
The silence behind them pressed in heavier than footfalls.

They reached the mouth of Merrick’s district and paused under a crooked overhang of old timber and rusted gutter teeth.

Across the street, the estate loomed.
It wasn't elegant. It was defensive. A hulking merchant's fortress dressed in failing nobility, tall stone walls, crooked spire, and mismatched balconies. Someone had added iron shutters to the second-floor windows at some point, thick ones, the kind that bolted from the inside. Not for weather. For people. Ivy crawled over the surfaces like old scars. A rust-colored banner sagged in still air. No guards were visible, but that somehow made it worse.
“That’s Merrick’s house?” Reau asked, incredulous.

Riven chuckled, low. “You expected marble? The man was gutter-born. Spends like he’s still afraid someone’s going to take it all back.”

Reau tilted his head. “I thought it would try harder to pretend.”

“Refined is for show. This is a barricade with better furniture.”

They waited. While looking around, casing the place, they found cover beneath an old wagon carcass, half-sunk in the overgrowth. Riven used the flat end of his dagger to sketch a rough layout in the dirt beside them.

“North wing’s guest quarters. The east wing is his study. The west side is the servant access. Stables are off the courtyard. If the sword’s displayed, as you believe, it’ll be in the study, not the vault.” He tapped the hilt against a crooked X. “Patrols hit this lane every ten. There’s a drainage grate back here, tucked behind overgrowth. Gets us into the cellar.”

Reau nodded. “And after that?”

“We improvise.”

He arched a brow. “So the plan is ‘no plan’?”

“The plan is simple,” Riven said. “Adapt fast, keep your head down, and don’t invite attention.”

Before Reau could answer, a soft click echoed from the alley behind them.

They froze.

A figure leaned against the brick arch, hooded, cloaked, angular. She stepped into view like she’d been waiting for applause.

“Thieves in daylight,” she said, eyes glinting under the cowl. “Bold.”

Riven’s hand went to his belt. Reau didn’t move.

She stepped forward, fluid and unhurried. Her cloak clasp bore a sigil they both recognized, red-and-black thorns.

“Relax,” she said. “If I were going to raise the alarm, I’d have done it before you played cartographer in the dirt. Been tracking you two, during your not-so-subtle casing of my house.”

“You live here?” Riven asked.

She shrugged. “Define ‘live.’”

“You always greet trespassers?” he said, smiling faintly.

“Only the interesting ones.”

He studied her face as she dropped the hood. Violet eyes. Scar along her inner forearm, just visible beneath her sleeve. She was young, but moved like she’d grown up watching every step.

“You don’t seem worried,” Reau said.

She tilted her head. “You’re not here to burn the place down. And you’re not stupid enough to be amateurs.”

“What makes you say that?” Riven asked.

“You’re scouting at the right time, not the busy time. You didn’t try the front gate. And you're carrying more silence than armor.” She smiled tightly. “That narrows it down.”

“You’re his daughter,” Riven said at last, snapping his fingers.

Her expression flattened. “Unfortunately.”

He leaned slightly toward Reau and murmured, “She’s no older than Weslin’s age.”

Keryn didn’t blink. “Who’s Weslin?”

“Doesn’t matter,” Reau said, just a touch too fast.

She watched him, but didn’t press. “You’re here for something.”

“A sword,” Reau said. Riven backhanded him hard on the shoulder.

She smiled faintly. “Tidebreaker’s his favorite. Curved grip, gold accents. He claims he won it from a southern warlord in a duel. It’s mounted in his study.”

Reau shook his head. “That’s not mine.”

Her eyes narrowed slightly, thinking. “Silver and black? Script along the blade?”

Reau nodded. “That’s it.”

She straightened. “He displayed that one, too. Said it meant the gods chose him again. Hung it about two weeks ago.”

“Two weeks ago?”

Her expression sharpened. “Exactly.”

“That’s when he took it and me,” Reau said.

She paused. “I’ll help you get it. But I want something.”

Riven folded his arms. “Let me guess. Family heirloom?”

“A dagger. Silver guard, red gem. It was my mother’s. He keeps it in his desk. Locked drawer.”

Riven raised a brow. “Sentimental?”

“Stolen.”

Reau’s voice was even. “We’ll get it.”

She nodded. “The study’s east wing, second floor. South-facing. The vault’s a trap—he keeps it for show. The real stuff’s displayed, where he can gloat.”

Riven stepped forward. “Why help us?”

She looked at him. “Because I want to see the cracks start. And because I think you might be the blade that makes him bleed.”

And just like that, she vanished. One step into the shadow, and gone.

Reau blinked. “She’s good.”

“She’s better than good,” Riven muttered. “Come on.”

The garden was silent. Damp ivy curled along the stone like green smoke. They found the grate easily enough, just as she’d said. It gave with a soft sigh and opened into the wine cellar. The air inside was thick with cork rot, ash, and the heavy hush of wealth.

They crept up the stairwell and into the manor proper.

“Smells like ink and money,” Riven whispered.

“Smells like lies varnished with perfume,” Reau muttered.

No guards.

Inside was a collector’s dream and a soldier’s insult. Trophies lined the walls. A curved blade sat on one plaque, and another, smaller frame held ribbons from a campaign Merrick had never fought in.
And there, centered in a glass case atop a pedestal of polished duskwood, Reau's sword. Stark, elegant, veined with silver and shadow. Real.
He stopped breathing for a second. Not from relief. From something older than that.
"There," Reau said, voice rough.

His father had kept books like this, not many, just two, locked in his private study. He remembered the smell of them, old parchment and something sharper underneath, like struck flint. “Cuts under the wall,” Keryn said, pulling the vines aside. “You’ll come out near the riverbank.”

She laughed quietly. "I've been alright for a long time." She paused, just for a moment, her fingers still closed around the dagger. "Just go finish what you started."

Reau kept his sword tight against his spine.

Riven went through what he'd stolen. Nothing stood out. Until the medallion, still vibrating against his palm, steady and faint, like something breathing. He held it. He didn't know why. Not yet. 

The tunnel spat them out near a bend in the river where snow fell in slow, lazy flakes. The air carried that damp, raw cold that seeped into joints and sat there. The world felt muffled, footsteps quieter, voices distant, as if the city itself had retreated under a blanket of frost.

Riven pulled his collar up higher as they made their way along narrow alleys toward Blackcap Alley, boots crunching lightly on snow-dusted stone. Even midday felt like twilight beneath the low gray clouds, and everything smelled of damp stone and distant woodsmoke.

There was no change to the people playing their parts, and with these new conditions, it was getting harder to tell who was truly controlled and who was just pretending to survive.

At the mouth of Blackcap Alley, they finally saw him.

Weslin sat atop one of two carts, reins bundled in gloved hands, cloak too large around narrow shoulders. The horses, their horses, eleven in all, stood tethered and restless, nostrils puffing mist into the cold air. Four were already hitched to the two wagons, the others tied nearby.

“Can’t be here all day,” Reau added, eyes still sweeping the street.

Riven climbed into the back of the nearest cart, settling among a stack of folded blankets and burlap sacks. “Feels like half the city’s watching without watching.”

Riven tilted his head toward Reau, who was staring off in the distance, back from where they came. Worry was written on his face. “You thinking about that dagger?”

Reau rested an elbow on the rail, exhaling slowly. “Something in it felt… wrong. Not the metal. Something deeper.”

Riven nodded. “You think it was a mistake, leaving it with her?”

Reau’s lips pressed into a thin line. “I don’t know. Maybe. Maybe it’s worse in her hands.”

Reau’s gaze lingered down the alley and nodded. “Another day.”

A merchant passed at the far end, an old man, gray-bearded, wrapped in a heavy wool coat, leading a small pony-drawn cart of his own. As he passed, he muttered loud enough for them to hear:

“They’re watching us all… every breath, every step… I keep my head down, but it doesn’t help… none of it helps…”

Then, as if realizing he’d spoken aloud, he straightened abruptly and whistled tunelessly, guiding his cart forward, his steps too rigid.

Weslin cleared his throat, shifting in the seat. “We're waiting here then?”

“Yeah,” Reau said. “No one moves until the rest return. That’s the plan.”

The snow continued to drift. Horses stamped occasionally, steam curling from their nostrils. A long moment stretched in silence before Riven broke it, leaning forward on the cart rail.

“You ever play Havern Ball, Weslin?”

Weslin blinked, startled. “Havern Ball?”

“I did. A street league back in Ashgate,” Riven said, grin returning. “Leather ball, two net poles at each end, no hands allowed. And that midfield stone, if you bounced it right and scored within five seconds, it counted double.”

“I’m going,” he said.

Reau sighed. “That sounds like Harmon.”

Weslin was already gathering his cloak more tightly around himself. “I’ll wait until the hour’s up… then I’m gone.”

Riven clapped him gently on the shoulder as he stepped down from the cart. “Good man.”

Riven adjusted his collar and started for the alley's mouth. He got four paces before he stopped, tilting his head, the way he did when something he couldn't name told him to wait. 

Reau settled back onto the cart’s bench, eyes still scanning, but his fingers brushed the hilt of the sword at his back, as if reassuring himself it was still there.

And Weslin sat tall, determined but pale, as the snow drifted softly over their quiet waiting place, and the market beyond continued its too-perfect hum.

The snow kept falling, soft and steady, cloaking the sound of hooves and voices alike. The cold crept into seams and cuffs, threading itself through wool and leather.

Reau sat quietly on the cart’s bench, eyes ever-watchful, while Weslin adjusted a strap, fingers stiff with cold but determined. They’d settled into a waiting stillness, but Riven’s gaze kept drifting.

Something felt off, more off than usual.

He shifted in place, leaning forward on one knee. Then he caught a flicker of movement high above the opposite rooftop. A figure, too far to make out clearly, was moving slowly along the edge. Not rushing, not hiding. Just… there.

“Riven…” Reau’s voice was low, urgent. “Look down.”

Weslin was already standing beside the cart, face pale, eyes wide. His breath smoked sharply in the cold.

Riven reluctantly dragged his gaze away from the rooftop.

And froze.

The marketplace had gone completely still.

Vendors, pedestrians, workers, stable-hands, every last person. All perfectly still. All perfectly silent.
Every single one of them had turned to face the cart. Their faces were slack, but their eyes were sharp, bright with unnatural clarity. And then one of them, a man in a vendor's apron, his hands still resting on an empty crate, tilted his head just slightly, as if listening to something Riven couldn't hear.
Weslin's hands had gone white on the reins.
Riven felt his stomach drop. All staring directly at them. No sound. No motion.
The only thing that moved was the falling snow.
