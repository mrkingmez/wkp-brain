Morning was still working itself into noon when Dain adjusted the cuffs of the deep maroon doublet he wore, the fabric rich and smooth, trimmed in silver. His cloak hung from one shoulder, fastened with a polished brooch bearing a forged crest. He caught his reflection in the small polished mirror propped near one of the support beams and straightened the collar with a touch of disdain.

"You know," Nyssa said from across the room, stretching out one leg while tightening her boot, "I liked those silks on you the first time. Good to know you're still dressing to impress."

Dain gave a smug half-smile. "That is precisely why I kept them," he replied, smoothing the cuff as though the answer required refinement. 

She arched a brow. “A bit bold for a stable loft,” she said lightly. “Though I suppose nobles do not sweat like the rest of us.”

"You wound me, my lady," Dain said with mock offense, placing a hand to his chest. "Would it help if I said I wore them just for you?"

Nyssa rolled her eyes. "Please. You're too proud to admit that… even if it's true."

Nearby, Weslin stood awkwardly, fidgeting with the strap of his satchel. His eyes kept darting toward the small yellow shard tucked carefully into the inner pocket, a gift from Aurelia.

Aurelia, seated with one leg tucked beneath her on a crate, looked up at him and gave a gentle smile. "You’re nervous."

Weslin hesitated. "It just… feels different carrying one. Like I’m not supposed to yet."

She stood, moving with the easy grace of someone who had spent a lifetime balancing power and restraint. "You're not alone, Weslin. You're trained. You’ve practiced. This isn’t about being ready in theory, it’s about trusting your instincts."

She placed a hand lightly on his arm. "Use the flow, don’t tap it. Let it move through you. Control comes with rhythm, not force. Do you remember the spells we reviewed?"

Weslin nodded slowly. "True Eyes. Minor Illusion. Glamour. I think I can—"

"You can," she cut in softly, her eyes warm and steady. "You’re not alone out there. And you’ll do just fine."

Weslin managed a small, unsure smile. "Thanks. I’ll try not to screw it up."

"You won’t," Aurelia replied, her voice quiet and confident. Before Weslin could shrink back into his nerves again, Garr’s voice came from the corner. “You’ve… come too far to fall now.” He didn’t look up from the blade.

He was sharpening a blade with short, practiced strokes, counting each one. Reau sat on an overturned bucket near the map table, scribbling something into a folded leather journal. Aldrek leaned against a support beam, arms folded, occasionally glancing toward Harmon, who still hadn’t spoken.

Figuring there was time, Brother Aldrek knelt in silence for his morning prayer. He bowed his head, one hand resting lightly on the haft of his mace, the other curled in prayer. Lips moved without sound, the worn edges of his robe pooling around his armored knees. It was a habit from years past. Whenever a mission bore the weight of uncertainty, he gave his doubts to the gods before bearing steel.

Harmon stepped away from the table, his boots thudding softly on the wooden floorboards. He stood tall near the edge of the loft, one hand resting on the hilt of his sword as he took in the team.

"Eyes up," he said, voice low but unmistakable.

Heads turned. Conversations paused. Even Nyssa, halfway through another jab at Dain, went silent.

Harmon let the quiet stretch a bit before beginning. “You all know your assignment,” he said, pacing slowly, boots measured against the boards. "Stormhaven didn't fall with a scream. It fell in silence. No messenger. No flares. Just a whisper of smoke and a few half-dead refugees stumbling out of the dark.” He stopped pacing, folding his arms. His hand found the pommel of the cane without notice. 

 "We're not the hammer this time. We’re the lantern. I need each of you to dig in and find out what happened, without getting killed in the process. No heroics, no theatrics. Eyes open, mouths sharper than blades. Talk to people. Everyone. Shopkeepers, drunks, guards, children, it doesn’t matter. Information is gold, and right now, we’re broke."

He pointed toward the city with a tilt of his chin. "We need to know who is running this city. What happened to the nobles? Who are the Red Cloaks working with? Why the change to their tactics? Resistance? Bring back anything and everything. We will sort it later."

“Also, map the occupation. Evidence of who they are and where they came from.” From the far wall, Riven gave a sharp, quiet whistle. Harmon continued as if nothing had happened, “I know I am asking a lot, yet I expect a lot. I never said this was going to be easy. We meet back here.”

Riven leaned on the window frame. “And if someone asks us what we’re doing?”

“Tell them you’re wine merchants scouting for rare bottles,” Harmon deadpanned. “Or you’re lost. I trust your creativity, Riven. Don’t get arrested.”

“Tempting,” Riven said with a grin.

Harmon swept a hand across the map. “We’ll reconvene tonight. Don’t get fancy. Don’t get dead.”

Harmon paused just long enough for the words to settle, his gaze sweeping the loft as if weighing each of them against the risks ahead. He walked back to the table and rested both hands on the edge. "I’m pairing you up based on how likely you are to get results... or survive."

He turned to the first.

"Dain, you’re on your noble cover. Nyssa, you're his escort, make it believable. Blend in but keep those eyes open. I want you both in the guild districts and the markets. If anyone’s got gossip, it’s the ones with silver tongues and gold purses."

Nyssa gave a two-finger salute. "Always a pleasure to play Lady Ghost."

Dain raised a brow. "Looks like we both get to wear silks. My lady.”

She winked and gave a devious smile. "Mi lord." 

He offered her a smirk. "Mi lady. This could be fun.”

"Weslin. Reau. You’re scouting the middle districts, merchant lanes, taverns, anything on the edge of the noble quarter."

Reau gave a nod, his voice low. "Got it."

Weslin tried to match it. "We’ll find what’s worth knowing.”

Harmon continued. “Aurelia, you’ll be with Aldrek.”

He paused for a moment, then said, “Neavin, you join them. I think that might need some more manpower.”

Aurelia gave a warm smile. “Of course.”

Aldrek, kneeling briefly beside the mast in prayer, rose smoothly. “Where there is shadow, light must follow.”

Neavin grunted, the corner of his mouth pulling up before he caught it. 

“That’s the spirit,” Harmon said. “Check the temples, the silent corners, anywhere people might still gather when they’re afraid.”

Then his gaze shifted to Riven. “And you.”

Riven arched a brow, already grinning.

“You’re our key to the city,” Harmon said. “Word is, you’ve got a name that opens more doors than most keys. Time to prove it. Today’s the day to earn that pay.”

“Take Ash with you. Try not to set anything on fire.”

Riven smirked. “You had me at ‘prove it.’”

Ash rolled his shoulder, flame-etched tattoos pulsing faintly beneath his collar. "Let’s just hope they don’t recognize me from less... diplomatic days.”

“Garr, you are with me. We will check a place I noticed when we came in. After that, we lend a hand where we can. We meet back here tonight. Take the day. Find what you can. If someone is not back by the tenth bell, we search. Now go find the thread… and begin to pull.”

A stillness settled in the loft as Harmon’s final words echoed through the space. For a breath, no one moved, just the creak of the rafters above and the wind whispering against the outer walls. Then someone moved. Then everyone did. 

Chairs scraped. Boots thudded against the wood floor. Cloaks were drawn tighter, weapons checked, and belts cinched. The room didn’t burst into noise, but it stirred with a quiet, focused energy. Conversations came back slowly. The blade-sharpening resumed. Boots were checked. Someone tightened a strap twice. One by one, teams drifted toward their corners of the loft. 

Across the room, Nyssa had disappeared into one of the side storage areas and returned dressed in a flowing charcoal-gray dress that hugged her form without restricting movement. Her hair had been tied up elegantly, her face touched with just enough color to draw the eye without shouting for attention.

Dain nearly choked on his water. “Merciful gods.”

Nyssa offered a slow spin. “You like it, my grace?” she purred.

Dain smirked, bowing low. “My lady. You… clean up disturbingly well.”

Nyssa gave him a slow twirl, the corner of her mouth tugging into a grin. “I once had to pass as a noble’s cousin’s cousin to rob his estate. Got halfway through dinner before I stole his ledger, three rings, and the heart of his steward. Poor man still sends letters.”

Dain chuckled, eyeing her dress. “Where do you even keep that?”

She stepped closer, leaned in like she might share a secret, then whispered with a wink, “Same place I keep the knives. Want to help me find them later?” As she lifted her dress, exposing her leg, she showed off a garter with three throwing knives strapped to it.

Dain raised a brow, his smirk deepening. “Oh, I see. Deadly and well-dressed. Remind me to never argue with you in close quarters.” His eyes lingered a heartbeat before he caught himself, clearing his throat as if to shove the moment back into professional territory. “But yes… I think I could be persuaded to help with a search. For tactical purposes, of course.”

Riven passed them with an amused snort. “Don’t forget the curtsy, Nyss.”

“Jealous?” she shot back.

Riven winked. “You never dressed up like that for me.”

Nyssa threw him a mock glare while Dain's smirk faltered ever so slightly.

“Come on,” Nyssa said to Dain, linking arms. “Let’s give the city something to talk about.”

They swept from the loft, all together.

Nearby, Weslin sat beside Reau, visibly trying to calm his nerves. Reau, polishing the edge of a blade with steady, practiced strokes, glanced over with that usual calm of his.

“You alright?” he asked.

Weslin hesitated, then gave a small nod. “Yeah. Just… big mission. Bigger city.”

Reau grunted softly. “You’ll get used to it. Or you won’t. Either way, you learn to keep moving.”

Weslin let out a slow breath. “That’s… comforting.”

Reau cracked a faint smile. “Didn’t say it to be comforting.”

There was a pause. The air between them felt heavy with things unsaid until Reau’s voice dropped a notch lower, quieter.

Reau set the blade down. He didn't look at Weslin when he started. “Just… be careful with Harmon. I’ve seen that look before. I had it once. Someone should have warned me.”

Weslin blinked, confused. “What? Harmon… he’s the best. He hasn’t done anything to earn that kind of warning.”

Reau nodded. “Yeah. He’s that. And more. I’ve seen him do things no one else could. Man’s a legend for a reason. But he’s also a Ringbearer.”

He leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees, the blade forgotten in his hands.

“My father wore a ring. Different than Harmon’s, but the same kind of power. I grew up watching it shape him. He loved me. I never thought he didn’t. But once that ring claimed him, his love had rules. Priorities. The mission came first. Always.”

Weslin looked down, hands clasped in front of him, taking in what Reau just said. 
“I don’t know your full relationship together. I just wanted to warn you, that's all. Just know the ring comes first.”

Weslin then nodded, “I will keep that in mind.” After a moment, he fished a smooth yellow shard from his inner pocket, turning it over in his fingers.

“I keep thinking… what if I can’t do this? What if I mess something up? I’m not like the rest of you. I wasn’t trained for war, blades, or this. They gave me this shard as if it’s supposed to make me useful. But what if I’m not? What if I let him and everyone else down?” 

Reau exhaled sharply through his nose, not unkindly, just enough to cut the spiral before it swallowed the boy whole. He looked at the shard for a long moment before nodding.

“Yellow’s underrated. People see fire and stone and think strength. But Yellow? That’s where the insight comes from. The instincts. It’s the only kind of power that gets stronger the more you listen instead of acting.”

He held Weslin’s gaze.

“Trust the shard. Trust the power. But more than that, trust yourself. That shard doesn’t define you. It just gives you the chance to figure out who you already are.”

Weslin nodded, slowly.

“Never worry about what someone else thinks. You’ll make mistakes. That’s fine. What matters is what you learn from them… and who that makes you.” Reau leaned back, a smirk returning. “Also, if you ever do lose it, just shout really loud. I’ll pretend I didn’t hear you for a minute, then come save your ass.”

That earned a small laugh from Weslin, and for the first time that morning, his grip on the shard loosened, just a little.

Reau turned slightly, finishing the careful wrapping of a silver collar at his throat. The glint of metal had caught Weslin’s eye earlier, but now, with the movement, it became impossible to ignore.

Weslin hesitated, then asked quietly, “That collar… what is it?”

Reau paused a moment, his fingers tightening the wrap as if debating whether to answer. Finally, he sighed through his nose. “It’s a binding collar. Suppresses power. Cuts me off from what I am.”

Weslin blinked. “Cuts you off? Why not just… take it off now that you’re out?”

“It’s keyed to a specific frequency lock,” Reau said, voice low. “Only the object it was bonded with can release it.” A muscle feathered in Reau’s jaw, the only crack in his usual calm. “That’s not exactly something I’ve got in my pocket.”

Weslin’s brows drew together. “That’s… brutal.”

Reau gave a small shrug. “That’s Morlin for you.”

Weslin was still unsure how to ask, yet asked anyway, but very quietly, “So, are you an Adept too?”

Something behind Reau's eyes went still. He nodded. “Different than most.” A beat passed. Then Reau added, almost absently, “I don’t like talking about my abilities and what this has taken from me.” Another pause, then, “Maybe when we know each other better.”

Weslin didn’t press. He simply nodded, respectful of the boundary, but his gaze lingered a moment longer on the collar before turning back to the others gathering their gear.
Riven sat on the floor, sharpening a throwing knife with slow, deliberate strokes, the steel whispering against the whetstone. Across the room, Ash leaned against a beam, arms crossed, watching with a half-lidded stare.

Without looking up, Riven asked, “You good with crowds?”

Ash’s lip curled. “I like ‘em best when they’re running.”

Riven chuckled. “That’ll work. You make the noise, I’ll make the memories vanish.”

Ash tilted his head. “You're always this confident, or is that just a side effect of dressing like a scandal?”

Riven finally glanced up, flashing a roguish grin. “Scandal’s easier to pull off than trust. But both look good on me.”

Ash snorted. “Guess I’ll handle the smoke. You handle the mirrors.”

“Deal,” Riven said, flicking the knife into the air and catching it without looking. “Let’s give them a show they’ll never quite remember.”

Aldrek stood near the center mast, head bowed in silent prayer. One hand rested on the hilt of his mace, the other traced a slow circle over the ancient symbol carved into the post.

Aurelia's eyes drifted to the symbol briefly. She didn't recognize the order, but the geometry was old. Older than the building. When his eyes finally opened, she said softly, “You always start with prayer.”

He looked at her, eyes calm. "I speak to what strength remains. And it listens. Sometimes."

She offered a quiet smile. "We could use a little divine luck today."

"Luck, no. God favors the ready," Aldrek said, adjusting the straps on his armor. "We go into the unknown. Be steady. Be true as his guiding hand."

From behind them, Neavin cleared his throat. “You two always this ominous in the morning? Or’s this a special occasion?”
 
Aldrek gave a rare, faint smile. “Fear is not failure. Only fools walk into shadow without feeling its weight.”

Neavin let out a low groan. “Saints save me, you two are insufferable before breakfast.” He paused before he continued, "Rather have odds I can count. This city feels like walkin' into a problem someone already burned half of." 

Aurelia placed a hand gently on his shoulder. “Then solve it one piece at a time. You’re not alone in this.”

A faint clang of metal echoed from somewhere outside, distant but sharp enough to make all three glance toward the shutters. Neavin gave her a grateful, if uneasy, nod. “Right. One piece at a time.”

Aldrek grunted approvingly. “Good. Let’s be off. The day waits for no soul, nervous or otherwise.”

Aurelia froze mid-step, her eyes widening, not with fear, but clarity. “Wait,” she said, sharp enough to halt even Harmon at the door.

Everyone turned.

She stepped forward, her voice low but urgent. “We’ve all felt it. The city is… off. People are acting strangely, subdued, always watching. They’re walking someone else’s path entirely. Controlled.”

There was a ripple of agreement, some nods, a few stiffened spines.

Aurelia glanced at Harmon, then to each of the others. “We don’t know who’s behind it or how far the influence goes, but until we do, we shield our minds. Everyone. Mental shielding isn’t just for Yellow Adepts. You can do this. It isn’t instinct. It takes training, intention. It’s discipline itself. Most don’t know how. They never learn it unless someone teaches them.”

Weslin looked unsure. “I’ve only practiced with you a few times. I’m not…”

“You’re ready,” she said gently, stepping closer. “It’s not about brute strength. It’s about building a wall of intention.”

She turned back to the group. “Close your eyes. Center your breath. In. Out. Ground yourself, not in this city, not in fear, but in who you are. Your memories. The things that make you you. Hold to that.”

Some shifted uncomfortably. The others already followed. Ash cracked one eye open, muttering, “Feels like meditation with extra steps,” before Aurelia’s sharp look shut him up again.

“Now imagine a sphere around your mind, just above your skin, pulsing slightly with each breath. That’s your boundary. You don’t need to block thoughts. Only let nothing foreign pass that barrier. No whispers that aren’t your own. No suggestions that feel unnatural.”

She reached into her satchel and drew out a dull yellow shard, holding it aloft. “For Adepts, your shards will amplify this. Let the shard feed the shield. Don’t tap. Let it flow with your breath. The shard should move with you, not ahead of you.”

Aurelia turned, eyes briefly finding each face. “It will hold. If anything tries to push through, you’ll feel it. And if you do… don’t panic. Return to the wall. Breathe. Anchor.”

Dain cracked an eye open. “And if we start hearing voices?”

“Then you run,” Aurelia said flatly, “and you tell me. Immediately.”

Ash gave a low whistle. “City’s just getting better and better.”

Aurelia exhaled. “I’m not saying something will try to get in. But if something already has, we’ll be the first to know.”

Harmon gave a slow nod. “Good thinking, Mentalist. Consider it standard protocol for now.”

Nyssa opened one eye. “So we’re walking into a city of secrets, half a dozen lies, and now maybe mind control? When did we start living in a fairy story?”

Aurelia didn’t argue. “Just worry about keeping your mind your own.”

One by one, the buzz of voices in the loft gave way to motion. Armor was buckled, cloaks were thrown over shoulders, and weapons found their homes beneath fabric folds or in clever sheaths. Final glances were exchanged, some serious, some teasing, others wordless but heavy with shared purpose. 

The last of the city’s morning light filtered through the slats. The team split into pairs. As footsteps hit the stairs, “We move soft and wide,” Harmon said. “Too large a group becomes a target. Pairs blend. Crowds swallow them.” 

Dain and Nyssa went first, smooth and unhurried as if they did this every season. Aurelia and Weslin followed, her hand briefly on his arm. Aldrek needed no sending-off. Neavin muttered something about breakfast and descended. Riven flicked his collar up. Ash went after him like a shadow. Reau paused at the top of the ladder, looked back once at nothing in particular, and went. 

At the table, Harmon stood still, watching them vanish into the city one by one. Garr waited at the door.

Only the sound of the wind moved through the loft now. Garr moved to the peepholes with his arms folded across his chest. Morning light slanted through the loft rafters, dust catching golden in its beams. At the center table, Harmon adjusted the thick leather straps across his shoulder and reached for a cane resting against the wall, blackwood, knotted with age, a metal pommel carved into the shape of a closed fist.

He rarely used it. Garr turned from the window. His eyes went to the cane, stayed there a half second, and came back up without a word. Whatever question formed didn't make it out. “Didn’t know you had a limp,” Garr said. 

“I don’t,” Harmon replied, testing the cane’s balance in his palm. “But the streets ahead aren’t paved with courtesy. Sometimes, the cane’s not for walking.”

He gave Garr a sidelong glance, eyes steady. “We’re heading for the crater.”

Garr raised an eyebrow. “Crater?”

“There’s one detail all the refugees agreed on, that surfaced again and again in their accounts, even through the shock,” Harmon said, his tone even. “An explosion. Near the heart of the city. It’s the only thing I believe wasn’t a rumor.” 

Garr’s mouth tightened. “You think it was magical?”

“I think a lot of things. But I’d rather know.” Harmon stepped to the window beside him, adjusted his cloak. “Too many unknowns. Too many silences where answers should be.”

There was a long pause.

Then Garr asked quietly, “So why us?”

Harmon didn’t answer immediately. He tapped the tip of the cane gently against the floorboards, gazing far away for a breath, then locked eyes with him.

“Because I trust you,” he said. “And I need someone watching my blind side who still knows what it means to believe in something.”

Garr looked down at his hands, calloused from training and battle, then back up. “And what exactly are we believing in today?”

Harmon gave a soft, humorless chuckle. “That the truth still matters.”

That hung between them for a moment. “You think this,” Garr gestured toward the city beyond the walls, “ends with us walking out heroes? With flags and honor and a clean slate?”

“No,” Harmon said. “I think it ends with us bleeding in the dirt, or limping back with ghosts in our pockets. But if we’re lucky, someone else walks out clean because we didn’t.”

He looked at Garr, not as a commander but as a man.

“That’s what legacy means, Garr. Not what you keep… what you give.”

The younger man nodded, quiet for a moment, then offered a lopsided grin. “Sounds like something a mentor would say.”

Harmon smirked. “I’m too tired to be a mentor. Think of me as a very dangerous old man who’s been where you’re going.”

Garr gave a small laugh. “Fair enough. So… to the crater?”

“To the crater,” Harmon echoed, shouldering the cane and stepping toward the stairs. “Keep your eyes open. Ears sharper. And your blades close. The truth never comes quietly.”

Garr followed, slower, still chewing on Harmon’s words. He said nothing more, but the weight in his steps said he carried them with him.

The last team slipped into the winding lanes of Stormhaven, their shadows stretching long across the stone as morning gave way to noon. The safehouse door shut behind them with a final, weighted click.
