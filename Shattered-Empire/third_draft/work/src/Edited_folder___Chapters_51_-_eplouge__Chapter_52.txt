The frost-laced wind had lost the stench of the lizardmen by morning, replaced by the cleaner bite of pine and distant salt. Dain let his mount stretch into a steady lope, not quite a gallop but enough to put more miles behind them before midday. The fight still sat in his muscles like cold iron, a dull ache where adrenaline had burned through and left him hollow.

Riven rode to his right, back straight despite the shallow cut along his ribs. He kept scanning the ridgelines as if expecting another patrol to crest them at any moment. Nyssa was just ahead, her cloak snapping in the breeze, braid pulled tight against the wind. Every so often, she glanced over her shoulder, not to check the trail behind them, but to make sure the two men were still keeping pace.

The land here sloped down toward a narrow valley, the trees thinning enough to give them a view of the town tucked along the bend of a wide, steel-gray river. Chimney smoke smeared the pale sky; masts swayed in the harbor like thin black spears.

They slowed at the crest of a rise, allowing the horses to catch their breath. “There it is,” Dain said, lowering the map.

“Big enough to get lost in,” Riven muttered, eyeing the maze of streets visible even from here. “And big enough to have eyes we don’t want on us.”

“That’s why we split,” Dain said. He pointed with two fingers toward the dockside sprawl. “Two go into town, keep it casual. One rides north for an hour, sees if we cross paths with anyone moving inland.”

Nyssa adjusted her gloves, expression unreadable. “So who’s getting the cold ride?”

“You like cold rides?” Dain asked.

“Only if they end with something worth the trouble,” she shot back, and he thought, just for a heartbeat, she wasn’t talking about travel.

Riven cut in before Dain could answer. “I’ll take the city's main road. If there’s trouble, I will have backup.”

Dain nodded. “Nyssa, you take the docks. I will head north. Remember, we have two days, after that we cut, and hopefully they will be ok.”

They sat there a moment longer, letting the wind speak in the dry grass and the faint hammering of some unseen dockyard drift up from below. Then, wordless, they nudged their mounts apart, Nyssa angling downslope toward the docks, Riven toward the town, and Dain easing his horse into the track that skirted the town’s western edge, heading north.

Dain had been riding long enough for the cold to creep into his boots when he spotted movement on the road ahead, two riders, their silhouettes framed against the pale sweep of winter sky. Aymir and Taerel.

He slowed his gelding to a trot, letting the wind mask the sound of hooves while he studied them. The road was straight here, visibility wide, and a direct approach would have them spotting him half a mile off. Where was the fun in that?

A narrow deer track curved off into the scrub to his right, running parallel to the road for a short stretch before rejoining it. He took it, ducking low under the bare arms of an overhanging oak, and brought his horse out at an angle that would put him just behind their blind side.

When he was close enough to hear the jingle of tack and the creak of leather, he kicked forward. “Road tax,” he called in a sharp bark. “Pay up or turn back!”

Aymir jerked around, hand flying to the hilt at his belt. Taerel spun his mount halfway before his eyes registered who it was.

Dain grinned widely, reining in beside them. “By the Creator’s good humor, I must be the luckiest man alive. Barely half an hour’s ride from the port city, and I stumble right into you.”

Taerel exhaled hard through his nose, the corner of his mouth twitching. “That was reckless.”

“That,” Dain corrected, “was inspired. And it means we’re in no rush. I’ve got Nyssa and her brother Riven searching the city itself. We’re to meet at a point outside the walls in about an hour and a half.”

Aymir frowned. “Riven?”

“Nyssa’s brother,” Dain said, matter-of-fact.

Dain’s eyes swept the road ahead, taking in the two riders, then flicking past them, searching for the rest. “Where’s everyone else?” he asked.

Neither brother answered right away. That was when he saw it, the third horse trailing behind, riderless but burdened. The shape slumped over the saddle horn was still, wrapped in a makeshift shroud.

Dain’s stomach tightened. “Who? What happened?”

“Lysana,” Aymir said, the name alone weighted enough to dull the edge in his voice.

Taerel kept his gaze ahead. “She put an arrow through a joblin about to reach Luca. Another dropped from above, out of the beams, gods know how, straight down. Blade to the throat. Quick. We couldn’t get to her in time.”

Aymir said nothing after that. He didn't look at the covered shape behind them. He'd been doing that since the pass, Dain realized. Not looking at it directly. Like if he looked at it straight on, it became something he'd have to carry differently. 

Dain’s jaw worked. “And Luca?”

Aymir’s expression was grim. “Went dark. Killed every one of them. I mean, every living enemy that was still drawing breath. Slaughtered. Scariest thing I almost saw.”

Dain looked at him sharply. “Almost saw? You were there, right?”

“Oh, I was there,” Aymir said. “But this… darkness rolled in over the whole field. Couldn’t see the hand in front of my face. Just the sounds. Screams. Things breaking. It was over fast.”

“True,” Taerel added quietly. “And when it cleared, nothing moved but us.”

Dain let out a slow breath. “Luca… where is he now?”

“Gone,” Taerel said. “After he killed everything still standing. He walked away. Didn’t speak to us, didn’t stop. We’ve been moving without him since.”

Dain let that sit. He'd known there was more to Luca than the job. Standing next to him, you could feel it sometimes, like standing near something that had been still for too long and might not be anymore. He filed it without comment. Some things required a quieter moment than this. 

The cold wind carried a long stretch of silence between them before Dain finally asked, “What else?”

Aymir gave the bare bones of their last few days. No embellishment, no lingering on details.

When they finished, Taerel glanced over. “And you?”

"Where to begin?” said Dain, then he cleared his throat and just started. "The king changed our orders after what we saw had happened to Stormhaven,” Dain said. “We went from recon and information gathering to a straight rescue mission. We got a threat that they would be executed at the seventh bell yesterday. So we had to pull the governor’s family out before they could make good on said threat.”

Aymir gave a low whistle, his breath steaming in the cold. “And you made it clean?”

“Clean?” Dain’s tone carried the weight behind the words. “It cost us.” The silence that followed said the rest.

Taerel glanced over. "Who is Riven, exactly? You have mentioned him twice now.”

Dain exhaled through his nose, almost a laugh but not quite. “Riven’s a Silver Cloak. We needed one after Nyssa was recognized; half the country wants her head, so she couldn’t wear the hood anymore. We had to find another. The only answer was Riven.”

He glanced at the brothers, expression caught between wry and weary. “Getting him wasn’t simple. He was rotting in a Forge Hollow cell, and we had to break him out. But he’s worth it. Not just some cutpurse with a silver clasp, he’s Nyssa’s brother. Grew up alongside her in the same orphanage. Not blood, but he calls her sister, and she calls him the same. Closest thing to family either of them’s got.”

They let the horses carry them a while, the sound of hooves muffled in the hard-packed dirt. The conversation slid into safer waters, stray observations about the frost creeping over the roadside grasses, complaints about the worn state of their saddles, and an argument over which tavern in Opindale poured the strongest ale.

The meeting place came up faster than expected. Dain had been watching Taerel's riding since the last valley. The way he held himself was too still, the arm tucked slightly wrong. He filed it. First order of business when they stopped. 

The group reined in at the edge of the meeting ground, a shallow rise overlooking the faint sprawl of the port city. The air smelled faintly of salt, carried inland with the steady wind off the sea. Dain swung down first, boots crunching on gravel, and motioned the others to follow.

“Let’s stretch a bit,” he said, loosening the straps on his horse. “We’ll need the rest before the ride in.”

Taerel dismounted stiffly, holding his bad arm tight against his side. The bandage beneath his tunic was dark at the edges, the cloth clinging. Dain crouched beside him, pulling free the satchel of supplies Harmon had pressed into his hands before their split. The smell of poultice hit the air as he uncorked a jar, sharp and herbal, cutting against the tang of sweat and leather.

“This’ll sting,” Dain muttered.

Taerel grimaced but didn’t flinch as the ointment touched raw skin. A thin sheen of sweat broke across his forehead anyway. Dain’s hands were steady as he worked, unwrapping and rewrapping, tugging the fresh bandage snug before easing the arm back into the sling.

“There. Good as it’ll get until we’re somewhere with a proper healer.”

Taerel huffed out a breath, flexing his fingers once. “Feels better already.”

“Don’t lie to me,” Dain said, half a smile tugging at the corner of his mouth.

They shared a small laugh, the kind that loosened something in the chest without quite clearing it. Then they all settled in the grass, the weight of silence hanging for a moment before light talk filled it.

Aymir tipped his head toward the city below. “Hard to believe that place once looked bigger than the whole world. First time Father brought me here, I thought every house was a fortress.”

“Not me,” Taerel said, his tone dry. “I thought every tavern was a fortress.”

That drew a laugh from Dain, unexpected and grounding. They went back and forth for a while, 
childhood tales, half-serious arguments about which trade fair had the best food stalls, even a few barbs about Dain’s terrible sense of direction. The sort of talk meant to push the heavier things back for a while. 

The laughing had settled back into quiet, and the quiet had settled into waiting by the time Nyssa crested the road. Wind tugged at her cropped auburn hair, her eyes sharp as they swept the group. A heartbeat later, another rider emerged, darker clothes, lighter build, with an easy posture that made him look like he belonged nowhere and everywhere at once.

“Well,” Dain said, rising to his feet, “look what I found.” He pointed at the brothers beside him.

Nyssa slid from her saddle before the horse even stopped, her gaze locking on Aymir and Taerel. They rose, greeting her with clasped forearms before turning toward the second rider.

“You must be Riven,” Taerel said.

The man swung down and offered his hand without hesitation. “That’d be me. Good to finally meet you two.” His grin flashed quickly. “She tells me you’re part of the original crew. I got drafted in when things went sideways.”

“Drafted,” Dain snorted. “That’s one way to put a prison break.”

“Details,” Riven said, shrugging easily.

Introductions passed quickly and cordially. After everyone was acquainted and updated, Dain clapped his hands once. “All right. Let’s get this moving. I’ve got the coin, let’s get a boat,” said Dain.

The city closed around them in a crush of stone and timber, narrow streets spilling down toward the docks. The scents hit first, brine and fish guts, woodsmoke, pitch, and the faint sweetness of spilled ale. Dockhands shouted over one another, gulls wheeled and screamed above, and the steady thud of cargo hitting planks rang through the air. Horses stamped and shifted at the strange sounds, but the group pressed through, weaving between carts and barrels until the open piers came into sight.

They didn’t linger. The boat was chartered quickly. Dain returned to the others after speaking with him, adjusting the strap across his shoulder.

“Half an hour, maybe less,” he said. “They’ll prep the vessel, and they will load the mounts in a moment. Leave your reins with the deckhands, they’ll get them stowed.”

The brothers exchanged a glance, then Taerel spoke. “Leave the personal cargo to us. We will find something to eat before the boat is ready. Preferences?”

“Fruit,” Dain said without hesitation. “Something to keep the edge off.”

“Same,” Nyssa added. “Whatever you find is fine.”

Riven lifted a hand in a lazy wave. “Bread. Don’t care what kind.”

“Waters for all of us, too,” Dain said, passing over a few coins. “Make it enough to last the trip.”

The brothers nodded, their weapons still slung at their sides, and slipped away from the group. 

Aymir stopped one of the dockhands with two fingers on the man's arm. Quiet. Firm. The horse doesn't move. What's on it doesn't get touched. They'll be back. The man nodded once and found somewhere else to be. The brothers left together, walking toward the town without a word between them.

The brothers came back before anyone expected them, bread, fruit, and water, set on the dock rail with no ceremony. Then Aymir drifted to the far end of the pier, settling between two stacked crates where the torchlight didn't reach. Taerel took the walkway above the gangway, arm in the sling, rifle across his good shoulder. They didn't look at anyone. They just waited, quiet as men who have already decided what they will do when the time comes. 

The three who remained drifted to the edge of the pier. The river stretched out before them, iron-gray under the sinking sun. Salt stung the air, and the sound of waves slapping wood carried a strange steadiness, like a drumbeat waiting for something to march to.

Small talk picked up again, softer this time, threading between them as the bustle of the docks went on around. Riven told a half-serious story about his first botched pickpocket in Forge Hollow. Nyssa leaned against a piling, smirking and tossing back barbs. Dain listened more than he spoke, his eyes half on the water, half on the crowd. For the first time in days, the moment almost felt like peace.

Riven was telling the story with his hands by now, Nyssa making a face that was equal parts disbelief and delight. Dain leaned on the rail and let them have it. He'd been watching Riven since the prison break, the way he moved with Nyssa, easy and warm and utterly unguarded in a way that most people who'd survived what they'd survived never managed again. Some people came out of the dark intact. He filed that thought somewhere quiet and let the story play out. 

It didn’t last. A pair of dockhands, half-drunk and carrying coils of rope, stumbled past and clipped shoulders with Aymir. He muttered something sharp under his breath, but the men just laughed and kept walking, their boots clattering down the planks. The air smelled of brine, pitch, and sweat, gulls wheeling overhead with hungry cries.

Thirty minutes would come quickly enough, and with it the boat, the food, and the next step of their journey. For now, they had a thin hour of borrowed quiet to breathe, though even that seemed fragile.

The horses were nearly secured, the creak of rope and splash of water filling the air around the empty berth. Nyssa had slipped off, saying she’d find supplies before the boat was ready. That left Dain and Riven together, shoulders bent beneath the labor of hauling gear across planks slick with river mist.

Riven broke the silence, voice pitched low. “You care for her, don’t you?”

Dain’s hand stilled on the strap of a saddlebag. He glanced up, wary, but Riven’s expression was calm, serious, almost brotherly.

“She’s been through more than most know. I’ve spent my life watching her keep everyone at arm’s length. You’re the first she’s let that wall slip for.” He tilted his head, sharp eyes narrowing. “What are your intentions?”

Dain let out a rough laugh, rubbing at the back of his neck. “Intentions? I don’t know if I’m foolish enough to put it into words. I just… don’t want her carrying it all alone anymore.”

Riven studied him for a long moment, then nodded. “Fair. If this thing of yours is more than a one-time fire, maybe I’ll stick around. If there’s room for me in whatever you’re building.”

For once, Dain had the right answer. He knew exactly what he wanted to say. He felt it. A snap cut the air, crossbow string.

Riven jerked, a bolt driving into his shoulder, spinning him down onto the planks. Dain cursed, lunging toward him, only for pain to sear through his leg as another quarrel slammed into his thigh. His knee buckled, blood soaking fast through the cloth. His face met the planks. He tasted blood before he felt the pain.

Bootsteps. Heavy. Measured.

Out of the drifting fog, three figures advanced. Gray half-plate dulled by grime, no crest, no color. Helmets hid faces, except the man at their head. Bare-headed, hair cropped short and iron-gray, eyes flat as slate. His gait was steady, predatory. They looked like death itself walking the dock.
Captain Thane Rouric’s mouth curved in a humorless smile, the kind that promised nothing good.
“Lord Merrick keeps a list,” he said, his voice carrying like iron across the water. “And every name on it is his to collect. Property, gold, blood, it doesn’t matter. He always gets paid.” His blade lifted, the steel gleaming cold in the torchlight as he leveled it at Dain. “And lords with loud mouths? He likes them silenced. So ending you, Lord Alric Rendsdale, will be my pleasure.”
The name hit like a blow. Dain spat blood, forcing himself upright on his good leg, defiance flaring behind the pain.
Movement stirred at the edge of the dock. Rouric’s head tilted slightly, that grin widening, as though he’d been waiting. “Come out, little fox,” he called, voice cutting like a lash. “No sense hiding. I can smell fear better than smoke.”
Nyssa stepped into the open, boots dragging on the wet boards, her hands empty at her sides. Her daggers hung forgotten. Her face was pale, her eyes locked on the two men she couldn’t lose.
Rouric’s laughter was low and without warmth. “Ah. Just as I thought. Three names from Merrick’s list, all lined up for me. Fortune’s kind tonight.” He let the silence stretch, savoring it. “But me? I like to play with fortune.”
From the shadows, two more crossbowmen emerged, weapons trained on Dain and Riven. Four in total. No mistake. No escape.
Rouric’s voice dropped to a near-whisper, more terrible than a shout. “Let’s make it a game. You’ll pay Merrick back by choosing.” His eyes never left Nyssa as he raised his hand, fingers spread wide. “One of them lives. One dies. You decide.”
The two crossbows shifted, steady as executioners’ axes.
The river slapped against the pilings. The torches hissed and guttered. Nyssa’s stomach turned to water, the world narrowing to the dark holes of the crossbows aimed at the men who meant the world to her.
Rouric’s smile thinned, cruel and patient. “Choose.” His hand began to close, one finger folding down at a time, the count steady, inevitable.
“Five.”
“Don’t listen to him,” Riven snarled, forcing himself upright though blood seeped black into his tunic. His teeth were red, his face ashen, but his eyes burned. “He wants you broken, Nyssa. Don’t give him that.”
Nyssa’s head shook violently, tears already blinding her. “I—I can’t…”
“Four.”
Dain shifted, bracing on his good leg. His jaw tightened, but when his gaze found hers, his voice steadied. “Nyssa. Look at me.”
She did, and through the pain, he gave her a crooked half-smile. “If it has to be one of us, make it me. Let him live.”
Her breath hitched, breaking. “No…”
“Three.”

Rouric’s grin widened, wolfish. “Tick tock, little fox.”
Dain braced on his good leg and made his voice do what his body couldn't. “Nyssa.” Just her name. Then: “Let him live. I mean it.” 
Riven’s eyes never left hers. The trickster mask was gone. No bravado, no lies, only raw truth. His voice dropped to a whisper, steady as the river’s pull.

Riven moved. With a surge of strength no one expected, he shoved Dain sideways, stepping square into the crossbow’s line.

Nyssa’s scream tore free, raw and jagged, scraping her throat until it bled. Her knees buckled, crashing to the wet dock. Palms spread wide, she pressed to the wood as if she could anchor him, drag him back with nothing but will. Her vision blurred, her body shaking, the taste of rust and river water thick in her lungs.
But the current gave nothing back.

Her mind became her enemy as well. A hundred flickers of him at once: a grubby boy pressing half a stolen apple into her hand; a wiry shadow teaching her how to run rooftops faster than any city guard; a laugh, bright and reckless, echoing through the alleys of their youth. Nyssa’s body collapsed forward as though the strings had been cut. Her arms shook beneath her, scraping raw against the planks as she dragged herself toward him. Every inch closed between them felt like a mile. Her chest heaved, lungs burning, but she forced herself on, palms slipping in blood that was spreading wider with every heartbeat he spent on that dock.
Dain lay where they had dropped him like a broken doll, his face battered until he was barely recognizable. One eye swollen shut. His lip split to the bone. His jaw was slack and red with blood. His chest rose shallow, but every breath was wet, ragged, rattling with a sickening gargle that made her stomach turn to stone. He was drowning. Drowning in his blood.
“Dain…” The word tore from her, jagged, more sob than voice. She pressed her hands hard against the wound in his side, desperate, useless, the warmth of him spilling hot and steady through her fingers no matter how tightly she pressed.
His good eye blinked, searching, dragging open against swelling. Somehow, it found her. It clung to her with stubborn force, tethering her even as his body failed him.
“No. No, don’t you dare.” Tears blurred her vision, dripping onto his chest. She bent close, forehead pressed to his as if sheer will could chain him to the world. “You’ll live, Dain. You hear me? You’ll live. I won’t lose you, too.”
He tried to speak, but it came out broken, bubbling, just a rasp tangled with the gurgle of his blood. His lips shaped a word that never made it past his throat. A sound that should have been strength, defiance, but was only pain.
Her whole body trembled as she cupped his face with one slick hand, forcing him to see her even if his eye drifted shut. “Stay with me. Please. You’re all I have left. Don’t leave me, not you.”
She held him like that, knelt in blood and splinters, as the world tilted and the horror pressed down. His breath rattled, shallow, wet. Her sobs wracked her, but she whispered the words again and again like a prayer that the river, or the gods, or anything might hear:
“You’ll live.”
The brothers moved around them, Aymir tearing strips of cloth, Taerel scanning the shadowed docks for any sign of pursuit. But to Nyssa, their voices were muffled, drowned by the weight of her heartbeat and the river’s slow, relentless churn.

Her lips moved before her thoughts caught up. A prayer, raw and unpracticed, spilled out.

“Creator, if You see me now… if You hear me, then let justice fall upon them. On Merrick. On Rouric. On every soul who raised their hand tonight. Don’t let them walk free. Not after this. Not after him. Take whatever you require… just let justice be done.”

The air shifted, and it wasn't silence that followed. It was presence. The river’s roar dimmed, the ache in her chest stilled. A warmth pressed against her heart like sunlight breaking through storm clouds, steady and sure.

And then, clear as a bell, a voice touched her mind. ‘Contract granted.’

Nyssa froze, eyes wide. She looked up, but the brothers hadn’t stirred. The voice had been for her alone. But it hadn’t been cruel. It hadn’t been cold. It had been radiant, vast, and merciful, terrible in its certainty, but shining with the weight of something higher, something just.

Her fear tangled with awe, her grief edged with fire. She bowed her head again, and a tear slid down her cheek, not of despair, but of resolve. Justice had answered her. And justice was already moving.

The brothers were the first to move, breaking free of the shock that had frozen them all. Aymir shouldered beneath Dain’s arm, taking his weight, while Taerel steadied his wounded side. Between them, they half-lifted, half-dragged him from the dock, his boots leaving smears of blood across the planks.

Nyssa stayed close, blades still clenched, though her eyes never left the river. Every ripple, every swirl of foam was another chance, another hope, that Riven might break the surface. But the current rolled on indifferently, carrying him away.

Dockhands scrambled under shouted orders, hurrying the horses aboard. The boat rocked as ropes were loosed, the river’s pull already tugging at its hull. Amid the chaos, Taerel swung back to the last of the animals. The riderless mare that carried Lysana’s body shifted nervously at the gangway, the slumped form threatening to slip. Together, he and Aymir steadied her, then bound her carefully in place before leading the horse aboard. 

Only once she was secure did they step back, both men pausing, heads bowed, before following the others aboard.

Dain was lying on a narrow bunk below deck, blood and sweat shining on his face. Nyssa brushed his hair back with shaking hands before pulling away, unable to stay still. She rose to the rail again, searching the water even as the shoreline began to slip by.

The brothers lingered there too, silent, eyes fixed on the dark ribbon of river. None of them spoke the truth aloud, that Riven was gone, and Dain was holding on by a thread, but it hung between them heavier than a chain.

As the sun sank low, bleeding red across the current, the boat turned downstream. The city’s spires fell away behind them, swallowed by dusk. And still Nyssa stared into the water, lips moving in a prayer only she could finish.

The river carried them on.
