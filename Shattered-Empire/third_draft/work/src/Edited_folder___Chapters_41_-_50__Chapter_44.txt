The stone stairwell descended into a darkness that felt older than the city above it. The chill deepened with every step, not just cold, but ancient. It bit through their cloaks and wrapped their spines in a pressure that didn’t feel like air. Damp stone underfoot, the sharp tang of rust and mildew in the air, and the steady drip of unseen water amplified the silence. The flames of their torches seemed too small for the weight of the dark around them.

They emerged into a broad chamber at the base of the stairs, a vaulted cellar, wide and cold. Empty racks leaned against the walls, their wood rotted and bowed. Barrels lay split and scattered, their contents long gone or reduced to black rot. Mold climbed the walls, and thin white roots snaked through cracks in the stone.

Nyssa’s eyes scanned every surface, sharp and deliberate. She crouched low near a dust-draped crate, fingers brushing a faint scuff across the floor. It wasn’t old; it was purposeful.

“Someone’s been here,” she murmured, her voice low.

Aldrek stepped to her side, crouching carefully, his gloved hand hovering over a half-filled bootprint. “Not careless either,” he added quietly. “And not long ago.”

Ash slowed behind them, closing his eyes briefly. His breath fogged. “It’s not just cold. It’s wrong.”

It was true, it was not just mildew and damp, there was a deeper cold, a hollow chill, as if something below the stone drained heat from the space itself.

Dain’s fingers brushed the hilt of his rapier as he scanned the rows of crumbling racks. “Quiet doesn’t feel right,” he muttered. “Like the walls are holding their breath.”

They advanced slowly, every step deliberate. The shuffle of boots on stone seemed louder here, more intrusive. And then, movement.

Two figures emerged from a side passage at the far end of the chamber. Red Cloaks. Not the hollow, mindless shells they’d feared, but disciplined, controlled, careful. Swords already half-drawn, eyes sharp and alert. Soldiers.

Aldrek didn’t hesitate. His hand shot up, a signal.

The team froze.

And then it broke all at once.

Ash moved first, fast, slashing across the nearer guard’s sword arm. Steel rang on steel, a bright spray of sparks in the gloom.

Dain surged forward, ducking low beneath the second guard’s swing. He caught the man’s wrist, twisted, and drove a shoulder into his chest, slamming him back into the damp stone wall.

Aldrek advanced with brutal efficiency. His mace swept low, catching the first guard behind the knee with a wet crack. The man buckled with a sharp gasp before Aldrek followed through, rising fast and driving the mace head into the man’s ribs. The sound was sickening, bone and flesh yielding all at once.

Nyssa struck with precision, darting in as the first guard dropped to one knee. Her dagger found the exposed throat, clean and final. The man collapsed soundlessly.

The second Red Cloak wasn’t done. He surged forward, blade high, teeth bared, but Dain was already moving, rapier sweeping across in a tight arc. The edge caught the guard’s temple with enough force to draw blood and stagger him.

Aldrek stepped in, mace raised high, and brought it down in a single, decisive blow.

The fight was over almost before it began.

Ash wiped his blade clean on the Red Cloak’s tunic, face tight, taking labored breaths. “They weren’t… empty,” he muttered.

“No,” Dain agreed quietly, glancing at the bodies. “But they were careful. Trained. Whoever’s running this place isn’t just relying on those mindless puppets.”

They gathered quickly, stripping the fallen Red Cloaks of anything useful, pouches, blades, keys, and left the bodies where they fell. Silence settled again, heavier than before, as if the cellar itself disapproved of the interruption.

At the far wall, Nyssa paused before an iron door set low in the stone. Its surface was rusted, the handle worn, but it was too obvious. A door like this, in a place like this, was a lie.

She knelt, studying the seams around the frame. No hinge, no lock to pick.

Aldrek stepped beside her, brushing at the stone near the base, and there, nearly hidden, was a small recessed lever.

“Decoy,” Nyssa murmured. “Figures.”

She pressed it slowly.

The iron door didn’t budge, but behind them, a section of wall groaned, stone sliding on stone. A hidden panel swung outward, revealing a narrow passage cut into the rock, almost invisible until now.

A grim smile crossed Ash’s face. “Always something with dwarves.”

They slipped through into the hidden passage, torchlight casting long, wavering shadows across the stone. Here, the air changed again: no longer simply cold and damp, but carrying a faint, unsettling hum.

Ash slowed, his fingers brushing one of the stone blocks. Thin metallic rods were embedded here and there, set flush with the surface. They hummed faintly, a vibration felt more than heard.

Ash rubbed his temple absently. “Headache,” he muttered, quieter this time. “Behind the eyes. Started when we came through.”

The others didn’t answer.

Dain felt it too, not the ache, but the discomfort of the architecture itself. The symmetry was wrong. Angles were slightly off. Proportions unsettling, lines curving where they should have been straight. His instincts as a fighter, honed on awareness of geometry and movement, screamed at him that this place was designed to confuse. They advanced carefully.

Ahead, a stone stairwell rose into darkness. They began the climb. Ash paused first, torch raised high. The same moss patch. The same faint crack in the stone. The same iron nail juts from the wall.

Nyssa frowned. “We’re doubling back.”

Dain’s eyes narrowed. “It’s looping. Damn clever.”

Aldrek rested a gloved hand on the nearest wall. “A dwarf wouldn’t waste stone without reason.”

Nyssa dragged a bloody finger across the wall, then swore softly as they circled again to find her mark already there.

Then she spotted it: a nearly invisible depression in the stone near the bend. A catch. A switch. She pressed it.

The stairwell shuddered softly. Another seam appeared, and a second, narrower stair opened alongside the first, hidden in the thickness of the wall itself.

Colder still. Narrower. Real.

Ash rubbed his temple again, blinking against a sudden wave of pressure behind his eyes. “It’s… heavier,” he muttered.

Dain touched his shoulder lightly as they climbed. “Next chamber,” he said. “We’ll take stock there.”

Ash nodded, silent now.

They climbed carefully, the darkness around them deepening, but so too did the sense of being watched.

The climb continued, every footstep measured, every breath felt, the air thicker now with something none of them could name.

The narrow stairwell leveled out into a long, low corridor. Stone walls pressed close, the ceilings just high enough for Aldrek to pass without stooping. The air was no longer simply cold; it had weight. A dampness clung to their cloaks, but there was something else: a faint hum, like iron vibrating somewhere deep behind the walls.

Ash winced and pressed his palm to his temple again. The sensation behind his eyes was sharper now, not a headache, not pain exactly, but a pressure, as though something unseen was curling into the edges of his thoughts. He didn’t say anything yet.

Nyssa slowed, her sharp gaze catching the floor’s subtle scuff marks, fresh, deliberate, but not careless. “Someone’s passed through here,” she whispered. “Recently.”

Aldrek studied the stone, running a gloved hand along the wall. His fingers brushed one of the thin metallic rods embedded at intervals, silent and inert but humming faintly if you touched them directly. “The stone is speaking, but not to us,” he murmured.

Their torches barely pushed the darkness aside now. Every shadow felt heavier, every echo sharper.

As they moved deeper into the passage, they passed the first one, a man in a butcher’s apron standing behind a cracked, empty workbench. He was sharpening a cleaver, back and forth, back and forth, the blade glinting in the dim torchlight. His eyes never moved. He didn’t look at them as they passed, but his face was slack, expressionless.

A few paces later, a child sat on the floor, mending a fishing net with delicate fingers that never faltered, never slowed.

No voices. No reaction. Just the eerie precision of lives hollowed out.

Ash’s hand drifted to the hilt of his blade as he walked by, heart thudding louder than the footfalls around him. The pressure behind his eyes pulsed in time with it.

Then Nyssa stopped suddenly, holding up a hand.

Ahead, the corridor branched into two perfect, mirror-image hallways. Both identical: same cracked tiles, same faint marks on the walls, same dim, flickering torch brackets, but one wasn’t real.

Dain frowned and crouched, tracing his fingers along the seam where the right-side corridor met the floor. “Hollow,” he said quietly.

Aldrek moved to the left-side passage, where a heavy dwarf-forged metal door stood half-shrouded in shadow. Its face was smooth, seamless, marked only by a relief of a forge hammer and an anvil.

“It has no hinges,” Nyssa whispered.

“No lock,” Aldrek added.

Ash’s headache sharpened again as he stared at the metallic rods set into the walls nearby. Something about their hum seemed stronger here, more insistent, like a breath brushing the inside of his mind. He grimaced and looked away quickly.

Nyssa stepped closer to the dwarf door, tracing her gloved fingertips around the anvil relief. A groove, almost invisible, circled it.

“This is no ordinary door,” she murmured. “It’s a puzzle.”

Dain nodded, his instincts sharp. “They built it to slow anyone who wasn’t invited.”

Ash muttered under his breath as he watched them work, “Dwarves do love their damn puzzles.”

Ash reached out but pulled back when he felt the hum of unseen energy prickling his fingers. “Careful,” he said softly.

Aldrek moved forward with Nyssa, crouching beside her.

“The forge and anvil mean something,” Aldrek murmured. “Strength and craft. Together.”

Nyssa braced her hands on the carved plate, feeling the faint give as it began to rotate, but it resisted in strange ways: uneven, as if weighted incorrectly on purpose.

“Help me,” she said.

Aldrek joined her, adding steady force, letting the stone tell him when to press and when to ease back.

They turned the plate clockwise. It caught. Locked.

Nyssa swore under her breath. “Reset.”

She took the second attempt, slower and more deliberate.

“Hold tension,” Aldrek whispered. “Feel the shift…”

They adjusted their grip. They shifted together, turning the mechanism bit by bit. Each moment of pressure mattered, too much, it resisted; too little, it froze.

Ash turned to watch the corridor behind them, his palm lit faintly with controlled flame. He went still. 

There, back in the gloom, another civilian, a woman this time, stood perfectly still, broom in hand, facing the team but making no move, her blank face illuminated faintly by the flickering torchlight.

She stared without seeing, yet the atmosphere tightened nonetheless. The hum behind the walls deepened. Dain shifted uncomfortably, casting another glance back. “Hurry…”

Nyssa nodded, sweat dripping down her temple despite the cold. She twisted carefully, felt the shift, and with a final breathless effort, a deep metallic thud echoed through the stone.

The heavy dwarf-forged door slid inward with a slow groan, revealing a stairwell beyond, steep and narrow, cut perfectly into the mountain itself.

They stepped through quickly, but the pressure didn’t lift.

Instead, as they climbed, it worsened: a slow tightening inside their heads, a heaviness behind the eyes that made thoughts feel sticky and slow.

The pressure had changed. It wasn't behind his eyes anymore. It was inside them. The pain was keeping him from speaking, in fear of making it worse. 

They passed another mindless civilian, an old man kneeling on the stairwell, polishing an iron pot over and over again with a rag that was already threadbare.

No one spoke.

The stairwell twisted tighter, darker. At the next landing, a single Red Cloak stepped into view. This one moved differently. No wasted motion, sword already drawn, eyes locked on theirs from the first step. And this time it was ready. 

The Red Cloak at the landing moved with precision, not hesitation, his blade already raised before Dain’s foot struck the stone. His gaze locked on them, sharp and alive.

Dain surged forward without waiting for an order. His rapier flashed in the torchlight, angled low to catch the Red Cloak’s weapon, twisting it wide, forcing the man back. But the guard was trained; he dropped his shoulder, recovered balance, and countered in a smooth, disciplined motion.

Aldrek stepped into the fight, mace held low. He didn’t hesitate either. His first swing was a feint, but the second landed, a brutal, arcing strike that caught the guard’s sword arm mid-swing. Bone cracked audibly, and the blade fell from the man’s fingers.

Before he could cry out, Ash was there, fast, vicious, striking a deep cut across the guard’s upper chest. Blood splattered the stone.

But even as he staggered, the guard didn’t give up; he lunged with his bare hands, face pale, eyes fierce.

Nyssa moved next, low and swift. Her knife found the side of his neck with a clean thrust. The Red Cloak finally collapsed, his last breath wet and ragged.

The fight was fast, brutal, and ugly. No one said anything afterwards. That was new. 

Ash wiped his blade clean on the dead man’s tunic and didn’t speak.

They advanced quickly into Basement level four.

Here, the air smelled different: dry rot, dust, cracked leather. The old furniture level.

Rows of decaying chairs and overturned tables lined the stone walls. The ceiling hung lower here, the arching supports carved with dwarven motifs but softened by years of neglect.

Chairs stacked atop tables. Cabinets missing their doors. Dust inches thick in some corners. No footprints. No sounds but their boots.

Nyssa led the search, darting down one aisle and back, eyes sharp for any hidden threat, but this place was dead.

The tension still tightened with every step.

Ash slowed near a sagging armchair, brushing one gloved hand over the faded upholstery. His fingers froze as the subtle vibration in the walls pulsed again, crawling into his skull like a whisper.

He winced and kept walking.

Aldrek fell back slightly, his eyes scanning the carved beams overhead. He muttered something, a quiet prayer for the dead, but it felt thin here, as though even the Light itself didn’t want to linger.

The team crossed the length of the floor in silence.

Nothing. No ambush. No guards. Just dust and decay.

At the far stairwell, they ascended quickly into Basement Level three, where everything changed again. The rooms here were active, crates stacked neatly along the walls, tools laid out on benches, every surface in use.

And people.

Mindless civilians moved with eerie precision: stacking crates, sorting rusted tools, sweeping floors that did not need sweeping.

Their faces were slack. Their eyes were empty. They worked in perfect silence.

A blacksmith at a cold forge lifted his hammer, let it fall, lifted it again, a hollow ritual, never striking true.

An old woman folded cloths and placed them neatly in a chest, then unfolded them and folded them again.

Ash watched them as they passed, his lips pressed into a tight line. 

Aldrek slowed again, falling behind the others for a breath, watching intently.

“This isn’t obedience,” he murmured, voice heavy with something deeper. “It’s judgment, penance, and punishment.”

Dain glanced back at him, brows drawn tight. “How do you mean?”

Aldrek’s gaze stayed on a man polishing a table endlessly. “Look at them. Each is doing some mundane task… but it isn’t work. It’s a sentence. Like a purgatory where their souls are trapped, and forced to repeat their most hated labor, forever, until debts they can never pay are forgiven.”

His voice was grim, steady. “This isn’t control, it’s condemnation.” Aldrek was quiet for a long moment after that, eyes still on the folding woman. Then he turned and walked. The team said nothing in reply.

They pressed on, wary of every movement but never interrupted. The civilians didn’t block their way, didn’t speak, didn’t seem to notice them at all.

Until one man, a dockworker judging by his clothes, rag in hand, lifted his head as they passed.

He didn’t speak, didn’t move. But he began to follow. Step for step. Slow. Silent. Inevitable.

No sign of the family here. They made the climb to basement level two. So did the dockworker, following close behind.

This floor, the walls pressed closer, the oppressive pressure heavier again.

The metallic rods embedded in the stone were more frequent now, their faint hum an almost constant background note. Ash rubbed his temple again, his breath tight in his chest. The pulse behind his eyes was worse, a low, insistent throb that made thoughts feel sluggish.

Nyssa kept leading, swift and precise. They moved fast, hugging shadows and avoiding every civilian’s blank gaze, skirting crates and benches.

Finally, at the far wall, a heavy door, which gave the hint of a prison.

The gate was exactly where the plans had said it would be, massive, dwarf-forged, barred, and guarded. Red Cloaks stood beyond it, weapons ready, watching the hallway carefully.

But they never needed to test that door.

As Nyssa approached, she caught the telltale signs of something hidden. There was dust undisturbed at the threshold, hinges frozen by age. This entrance hadn’t been used in years.

She signaled them around.

A narrow service archway lay partly hidden behind old crates, just wide enough for them to slip through. It was cramped on the other side. There was a lock on the inner hatch, brittle with rust, but Nyssa made quick work of it. They didn’t speak as they passed through.

Inside was dead silent.

The prison was empty. Not empty as in “recently cleared,” empty as in forgotten.

Dust-covered floors. Cells standing open, their doors rusted and crooked. Chains trailing from walls.

A few bowls were scattered across the stone, dry, empty, cold.

The warden’s office stood at the far end, its interior bare save for a single stone table.
At its center, they found a polished silver dinner plate, perfectly clean, resting atop a folded napkin.

Dain squatted down beside it. “Someone was here… not that long ago.” He examined the plate and found no dust and no disturbance around it either. It felt wrong.

“Perfect,” Nyssa muttered.

Dain let out a bitter, humorless laugh. “Of course.”

Ash grimaced. “Creepy as hell.”

Aldrek stepped forward, touching the plate, rocking it gently until it clinked faintly on the stone surface.

“No one’s been here for a long time,” he murmured.

“Then what about the plate?” asked Dain.

“No idea,” Aldrek said, shaking his head in disbelief.

Before they could debate it further, from the shadows of the office doorway, a figure appeared.

The man in dockworker’s clothes, the silent follower from the lower level, had somehow made it in after them.

He stood just beyond the threshold, his eyes vacant and glassy.

And then he screamed, a sound without words, raw and jagged and endless. The noise shattered the stillness, echoing through every stone corridor of the prison floor.

Ash swore aloud. “We’re blown.”

From beyond the main corridor, where the guarded door had been, came the clatter of boots on stone, armor shifting, weapons drawn.
Ash’s curse was still hanging in the air as Nyssa and Dain threw their weight against the heavy dwarven door, slamming it shut just as boots crashed into it from the far side. The iron rang like a cracked bell, hinges groaning as it held.

“Find something to bar it!” Dain snapped.

Aldrek swept through the nearest pile and came up with a rusted iron rod. Dain and Aldrek wedged it through the door brackets just as another thundering blow landed. The team fell into place, weapons ready. Breath harsh, nerves tight.

And still… silence inside the prison.

“Back through the tunnel!” Dain snapped.

But Nyssa was already sprinting toward the service archway. She reached it, skidded to a stop, and cursed.

The opening they’d come through was sealed now. A heavy, dwarven-grade siege door, smooth and cold, no visible lock, no handle, just a single round impression in the metal, pulsing with a faint, steady hum.

She pressed her palm to it and felt it vibrate. A low, harmonic thrum, almost familiar.

Nyssa’s eyes widened. “It’s the same pattern… Reau’s collar. It’s a vibration lock, and we don’t have a key.”

Ash joined her, shoulder to shoulder. “Can you bypass it?”

“Not from this side,” she muttered. “It’s not meant to open inward, most likely. That’s the point.”

She stepped back, heart hammering, mind already hunting for alternatives.

“They secured it from the inside. So no one could escape that way.”

“Which means we’re sealed in,” Dain said grimly.

Nyssa and Dain turned together, just as the heavy dwarven door behind them shook from the first pounding impact of armored boots.

They all looked around. “That screamer didn't walk through the door we came through," Ash said. "So there's another way. Let's find it.” The others agreed.

Nyssa paused quickly and whispered, “That cell wasn’t open before…”

Her gaze fixed on an iron-barred door cracked open, dark inside. A second across the room, also ajar. Foul, musky air crept from both.

Something moved.

The stench grew worse: old blood, rot, iron, a wet animal smell that clung to their throats.

Then came the voice—low, guttural, heavy in their skulls, as if spoken through a vast loudspeaker but with no direction, no echo, just there inside them all at once:

“Ahhh… finally you arrive. And yet so slow. So tired already… I wonder… will you even amuse me?”

Two creatures crawled out of the cells, horrors, crawling horrors.

Insectoid yet somehow humanoid, long-limbed and jointed wrong, torsos emaciated, faces pale and eyeless. Their mouths were jagged circles of needle teeth. Their limbs ended in claws sharp enough to gouge stone, dripping black ichor that hissed as it struck the floor.

The team froze for a heartbeat, and the voice laughed again, curling coldly inside their skulls:

“Ah… my hounds stir, little mortals. Their hunger is endless… and your fear will season their feast.”

The first creature lunged, faster than any Red Cloak they’d fought. Ash met it halfway, shouting as he drew flame into his palm. A searing arc of fire lashed outward, lighting the room orange, but the creature twisted aside, the flame merely scoring its chitin.

It crashed into him with a sickening crunch. Ash staggered, wind-driven from his lungs, the stink of it overwhelming, wet rot and bile.

The second creature moved faster than thought.

Its limbs didn’t walk, they spidered, long, jointless things pulling its body across the stone with unnatural precision. It reached the dockworker before anyone could react. One moment, he stood there, mouth still open from the scream, and the next, he was lifted into the air like a puppet yanked by invisible strings.

The man showed no fear. He didn’t even resist. His mind was already gone. He just kept screaming… until he didn’t.

The creature’s face split open, revealing not teeth but writhing tendrils, slick with something that steamed in the cold. They burrowed into his skull, through ears, nostrils, and eyes. He didn’t even flinch. He just shuddered once, violently, then froze. A flicker of light left his eyes as his body went limp, limbs twitching with one last spasm.

Then the creature dropped him, lifeless and boneless, like a discarded rag soaked in blood and bile. Nobody moved for half a second. Long enough to know they'd all seen it. Short enough that none of them could afford to think about it yet. 

Aldrek stepped to the one near Ash, his white robes flaring as he swung his mace with two hands. The iron head smashed into the creature’s flank with a meaty crack, but the beast didn’t fall. Its claw lashed out, raking across Aldrek’s thigh. Cloth and leather tore; blood flowed warm down his leg.

The priest grunted, pain flashing bright, but stood fast.

Dain circled right, rapier flashing. His blade darted in, piercing a soft gap between armor plates. Black ichor sprayed his sleeve, hot, sticky, foul.

But the second creature was already on Nyssa. She ducked low, feeling air split where its claws slashed, then stabbed upward with both daggers. Metal struck flesh, but shallow. The thing shrieked in fury, its jaws snapping close enough to tug at her hair.

“So exquisitely breakable… your flesh slows, your minds stumble. You were never meant to endure me.”

Ash recovered, flames licking up his arms. He thrust one palm outward, fire lancing from his fingers. This time, the flame caught the first creature full in the chest. It screeched, an unholy noise that split their ears, and fell back, smoke rising from cracked chitin.

But not dead. It lunged again.

Aldrek swung low, aiming for its legs. The mace collided with a knee joint, splintering it, and the creature staggered. Aldrek raised the weapon again, pain in his leg sharp but ignorable, his breath ragged.

Nyssa rolled beneath the second creature’s outstretched limbs, slicing at its underbelly. Her blade tore a long gash, black ichor gushing, sizzling on the stone. The stench was unbearable.

“Yes… bleed… not for death, but for revelation. Your suffering will peel your minds open… and I will feast on what spills out.”

The first creature darted sideways, unexpectedly fast despite its injury, and slammed into Dain, claws raking his armored shoulder. He fell back, breath driven from him, but kept his rapier up, deflecting a killing strike.

Ash’s flames flared again. Sweat poured down his face as he drew deep on his Adept strength, fingers trembling from strain. Fire shot forth, catching the creature’s side, curling flames around its limbs.

Aldrek followed immediately, mace smashing down into its back. Bone cracked loud enough to echo in the chamber.

The first creature finally collapsed, smoke rising from its twitching limbs.

But the second was still alive.

It darted toward Aldrek as he turned and slashed across his arm. The priest cried out, blood streaking his robe’s white sleeve.

“Ahh… your healer bleeds,” the voice crooned. “You will fall… all of you…”

Ash turned, fire ready, but Nyssa was faster.

She leapt atop the creature’s back, driving both daggers deep into its exposed neck joint. The thing screamed, a noise that made the air feel thin, and writhed beneath her, claws scraping stone. It bucked violently.

Dain was there, stabbing low, driving steel through its abdomen.

Ash added flame, his hands alight as he poured burning heat into the wound.

The creature’s final shriek tore at their ears, and then it slumped, smoking, ichor pooling beneath it.

The voice faded with it, not gone, not defeated, but withdrawing.

“Bask in your small triumph… the mind is patient. I will unmake your thoughts slowly… piece by trembling piece… until even your screams serve my will.”

Aldrek staggered, bracing himself against the stone. Blood dripped from his arm and leg, but his eyes were steady. “I’ll manage,” he said, breath ragged, one eye squinting against the pain.

Ash wiped sweat from his brow, his limbs trembling with exertion. They gathered around Aldrek. The three standing took a knee to take a breath.

They had barely caught their breath when the door behind them shuddered, splintered, and crashed inward.

Red Cloaks poured into the room.

Six of them, disciplined, coordinated, fast.

And at their center, an Adept, robed in deep crimson, his fingers already tracing glowing runes into the air, magic building fast.

Ash looked at Nyssa. She looked back. The Adept's runes cast red light across the floor, reaching toward them like fingers. They had nowhere left to go but forward.
