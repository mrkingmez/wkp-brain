The tent was dimly lit only by a low lantern that cast golden light against the canvas walls. Harmon sat propped up on a bedroll piled with folded cloaks and a wool blanket. He looked pale, the lines on his face deeper than usual, but his eyes were sharp again. A wooden bowl of stew rested in his lap, steam rising slowly from it.

Weslin hovered nearby like a shadow, his worry etched plainly in the tight lines around his mouth. He’d helped Harmon sit up, fetched his stew, and fluffed the blanket twice. He didn’t know what else to do.

Aurelia sat cross-legged in the corner near the packs, quiet, but not distant. Her gaze drifted occasionally to Harmon, but mostly she kept her attention on Weslin, reading his energy as much as his words.

Garr stood off to the side, arms crossed over his chest, eyes flicking between Harmon and the flap of the tent. He looked like a man trying to remember how to breathe normally. Next to him, Dain leaned against the support pole, his face unreadable.

"I imagine I’ve missed quite a bit," Harmon said at last, spoon pausing halfway to his mouth. His voice was rough but steady. "Go on. Catch me up."

Dain was the first to speak. "We made camp a little after noon today. Found a good hollow, set camp. Nyssa and Neavin scouted the perimeter; no signs of pursuit. Ash has been keeping the rotation tight. No one’s slacking."

Garr added, "Brother Aldrek’s been tending the five we pulled from the cells. Minor injuries. Bruises, burns, and one fractured ankle. They're stable."

"And the five themselves?" Harmon asked, his tone even.

"Quiet," Dain said. "Still recovering, keeping to themselves mostly. One of them, Calen, has been keeping his distance. Another, Tarn, ran off early this evening, stealing a horse. The other three seem… thankful. Or at least not hostile."

Harmon nodded slowly, taking another bite of stew. After a moment, he glanced at the pile of folded parchments beside Weslin’s cot.

"That's all from Luca?"

Weslin shifted awkwardly. “Y-yes, sir. Reports, questions… mostly requests for guidance. They haven’t heard from us since the ride from Forgehallow.”

Harmon let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "Stack’s as tall as my thigh. Alright. I’ll get to them."

Before the boy moved, Harmon looked between Garr and Dain. "Before anything else." His voice dropped into something quieter than command. "Both of you. You kept it standing while I was down. I won't forget that."
Neither man answered. The silence did the work for them.
Weslin blinked, startled. "I’ll—uh—I’ll get you more stew."

"Thank you, Weslin," Harmon said gently, and the boy hurried out with the bowl.

For a moment, only the sound of the tent flaps rustling filled the silence. The lantern flickered softly, throwing a brief shadow over Harmon’s face as he adjusted his grip on the bowl.

Aurelia tilted her head. “And what about you? How long can you go on like this?”

Harmon didn’t answer right away. “Long enough.” 

Aurelia held his gaze a moment past the point of comfort. She didn't press it. But her expression made clear she had counted the days and done the math. "Long enough," she repeated, and left it there like a stone he would carry. 

He then leaned back, resting his head against the bedroll. He looked at Garr. "You did well. I know you didn’t ask for this, but you stepped up."

Garr didn’t reply right away. He shifted his weight, then spoke quietly. “I thought I’d have more time to learn from you.”

Harmon smiled faintly. "So did I."

Dain shifted from foot to foot. "We all did."

Harmon was quiet for a moment. Then, almost to himself: "The things worth learning don't fit in a drill." He looked at Garr directly. "Staying in the room when everything in you says to leave. You did that. You don't need me to teach you what you've already learned the hard way." 

Harmon looked between the two others. “It’s going to be a long road. But we’re still on it. Tomorrow, we start moving again, not far, not fast. Enough to stay ahead, enough to breathe. We’ve got the mission to finish, and maybe a new Silver Cloak to consider.”

He rubbed his temple, the weariness creeping back into his features. "I’ll speak to the others tonight. Meet the new blood. Sort what’s next."

Then, to both of them, he added, “Tell the others to get some rest tonight. Proper rest. I’ll speak to the new blood quickly, then I’m done. We need clear minds and strong legs in the morning.”

The two men nodded and stepped toward the flap. Harmon’s eyes closed. As the moments passed, he pushed himself up slightly, waving a hand. “Go. Let the others know we’re leaving at first light. Garr, stay a moment.”

The others exited, and Harmon’s expression grew distant as he waited for the flap to close.

“Thank you, again,” he said, voice low. “For stepping up.”

Garr shifted uncomfortably. “I didn’t have a choice.”

Harmon studied him for a long moment, the lines around his eyes tightening just slightly. “That’s exactly why it mattered.”

His attention shifted down to his hand, the ring dull and cold on his finger. “The ring carried me farther in that fight than I should’ve gone. I can feel it weakening.” His throat worked once. “And I can feel what it took out of me. It does not give without leaving a mark.”

Garr stepped forward. “Then take it off.”

Harmon gave him a tired smile. “I will not take this ring off again. Not while the road is under us and eyes are hunting. If it comes off, it is because I am done.” His gaze hardened. “And you don’t tell Weslin. Understood?”

“…Understood.” Garr's jaw worked once, quietly, the way a man swallows something he knows he'll carry alone. Then he nodded. 

Outside, the fire crackled low. The murmur of camp life was already winding down. Harmon let out a long breath and leaned back against the makeshift headrest.

“Tell them all to get sleep tonight,” he said. “They’ve earned it. Then start bringing in the new one for a quick chat, one by one.”

Garr nodded, then headed out of the tent to leave Harmon alone for a bit. 

The fire burned low outside the canvas, casting long shadows that flickered like ghosts. Inside the tent, Harmon sat with his hands folded. Garr stood just outside, pulling back the flap for the first of the rescued.

A thick-set man, grime still clinging to his clothes despite the wash, Torrek had the look of a boulder chipped from the mountains, hard edges, heavy shoulders, and sharp eyes. A cut above his brow hadn’t quite healed as he ducked into the tent.

“You Torrek Mull?” Harmon asked without rising.

“That’s right,” the man said, brushing ash from his arms. “Earthbound Adept. Miner for hire. Been digging holes since I was ten. Stone, ore, old city veins. If it was buried, I found it, mostly. I was one of the best… still am.”

“You worked for Merrick?”

“Not by choice. He bought my contract out from a trade baron. Figured I could find him buried treasure under Forgehallow and in the mountains nearby.”

“What do you want now?” Harmon asked.

“To disappear,” Torrek said bluntly. “Got family on the coast. Good people. They’ve got a farm. If they’re still breathin’, I’d like to see them again.”

Harmon gave a slow nod. “You’ll be given supplies, some coin, and a map. Hear me, you need to move like you are already being followed. Back roads, cold fires, no caravans, no open inns. If anyone asks who you are, you are someone else.”

Torrek’s brow lifted in surprise. "Just like that?" He shifted his stance, as if testing whether the ground beneath him was real. 

"Just like that. You walked into someone else's property. You walk out your own man. What you do with that is yours to decide.” Harmon said.

Torrek nodded once and stepped back into the night, thoughts filled his head.

Garr brought the next in. Haldrin Vance stepped carefully, robes trailing like a half-forgotten sermon. The scholar held a journal tight to his chest, eyes sunken but alert.

“Haldrin,” Harmon said. “Royal Archive, correct?”

“Was,” Haldrin answered softly. “Until I started asking the wrong questions.”

“What sort?” asks Harmon. 

Haldrin looked up at him, eyes bright. “The Pearl. Not the myths. The truth. What it was really meant for. What happened to it? What the pieces might still do.”

Harmon didn’t interrupt.

“I started digging through restricted shelves and asking about its construction, the materials, the magic. As I found some answers, that’s when they came, men with coin, calling themselves investors. More like thieves. Soon after, my research vanished. And so did I.”

“You still want answers?” Harmon asked, voice calm.

“I do,” answered Haldrin.

“Then find me in Opindale in three weeks, if the road allows it. If it does not, you wait two days and you vanish,” Harmon said. 

Haldrin blinked. “Truly?”

"We'll talk. Until then," Harmon said, "what do you need?" 

Haldrin hesitated, thumb brushing the spine of his journal as if reassuring himself it was still there.“I want to go home. To the Archive. Back to my work.”

“Done. You leave at first light with Torrek.”

Haldrin clutched the journal tighter and bowed, sincere. “Thank you.”

When he left, Garr stood aside for the next figure.

The tent dimmed. The next man stepped inside, slow and careful, cloak drawn tight. A short beard framed a jaw that once belonged to a younger, more idealistic version of himself. He said nothing as he stood before Harmon.

“Name?” Harmon asked, already knowing.

“Calen,” the man replied coolly.

Harmon studied him for only a moment before replying, “Calen… or should I call you Reau?” Harmon asked, his voice lowering, the name carrying more weight than the man himself. 

The man’s eyes narrowed slightly. “You knew?”

“How could I not?” Harmon said, his voice softer. “I was there the day you were born, held you in my arms. Arthur brought you around from time to time. Even though about your little...” He coughs. “Experiment.” He gave a knowing glance. Reau’s expression didn’t budge, stone cold.

“You were always a sharp one,” Harmon said with a faint, sad smile. “Your father and I… we were friends. Real ones. Not the kind that just share a tent and a mission. We believed in the same things. In doing what was right.” He paused, gaze sharpening. “What happened?”

“I was hunting slavers. East riverlands,” Reau said. “Tracked them too long. Got tired. Slipped up. They caught me in my sleep. Silver-laced collar, full binding rune. Been wearing it ever since.”

He tilted his chin to show the collar’s etched gleam. Harmon’s jaw tightened, just slightly, at the sight.

“And they took my sword,” Reau said, anger filling his voice. 

Harmon leaned back. “How are you holding up?”

Reau gave a small breath, not quite a laugh. “Between the new jewelry and the absence of my blade? A little hollow.”

“You know where it is?”

“I do not feel it, probably the collar. I looked through the prison as we made our way out. Nothing. But I might know where it ended up.” His voice turned cold. “Merrick’s estate. Stormhaven. He kept it. Said it was art. Put it on display. For all his lies, that was one of the few truths he told.”
The way he said it was flat. Not angry. Just finished. The tone of a man who had burned through rage long ago and arrived somewhere colder on the other side.
Harmon let it sit for a long moment before saying, “We’re heading east. Toward Stormhaven.”
Reau met his eyes. “Then I’ll come with you.” His gaze flicked once, hard. “If Stormhaven holds my sword, it holds answers too. I am done being caged.”

“You are welcome to,” Harmon said.
Harmon held his gaze a moment longer. "Keeping the name back in that cell. Operating blind with that collar on. That took something most men don't have." He paused. "Your father would have done the same." Reau said nothing. But something in him received it. He simply nodded his head. Harmon continued with his questioning. “Why the name change? I know you don’t hate your name,” Harmon said quietly.
“No,” Reau replied. His jaw tightened once. “Using it now would only hurt his legacy. I loved him, Harmon. Still do.”

“So do I. I miss him every day,” Harmon said with a bit of emotion in his voice. 

He didn't say anything else. He just pulled his collar straight, as if reminding himself it was still there, and walked out. 

It was barely a moment before the tent flap opened again, his last planned meeting. Riven Duskwind strolled in like he owned the place.

“Riven,” Harmon said without looking up.

“Was wondering when it would be my turn,” Riven said with a grin. “Thought you forgot me.”

“I waiting to see if you’d run. Like Tarn.” Harmon said. 

“Oh, I thought about it. But Nyssa would’ve hunted me down, and she can make the brave men break with that glare of hers. Besides…” He paused as he sat lazily, legs crossed, hands resting in his lap. “…you’re paying real coin. That’s worth staying for.”

“So you know what I'm about to ask you then?” asked Harmon.

“Do you think Nyssa could keep her mouth quiet on it for this long? I do give her credit; it took her a whole eight hours to tell me. That is some kind of record for her.”

“I bet. But I’m offering more than coin.”

Riven raised a brow.

“A full pardon,” Harmon said. “Carried by the weight of the crown, of Opindale. If anyone challenges it, they can challenge me. You are free of the chains.”

That sobered him. “You’re not joking,” Riven said.

“No,” was Harmon’s reply. A long silence stretched between them. Harmon looked him in the eyes and said, “You stayed. When Tarn rode out, you didn't. You could have. You had the horse and the dark, and nobody would have blamed you." He tilted his head slightly. "That tells me more than anything else you could have said." 
"A full pardon," he said again, slower, like he was checking whether the words were real. "Not just coin. Not just a job." He exhaled once. "Do you know how long I've been looking over my shoulder?"
"I have a guess," Harmon said.
Riven nodded. Then the grin came back, but slower than before. "Yeah. I'm in. What are you going to ask of me? What is my new position going to be?”
“You’re the faceman,” Harmon said. “ A Silver Cloak. You lie, you smile, you cheat, and people believe you.”

“I open doors,” Riven said with a smirk. “You just have to tell me which ones.”

“Then here’s the first,” Harmon said, leaning in slightly. “When we reach Stormhaven, we’ll need a base of operations. Somewhere that doesn't ask names and won't remember faces.”

Riven’s grin spread wide. “Stormhaven’s my city,” Riven said, and some of the grin fell away. “I know places that do not ask names twice, and alleys that forget faces if you pay them right.”

“Good. Now go get some sleep, we have some long days ahead of us.”

His hand rested against the canvas, fingers stilling as he exhaled. “You know…” Riven’s grin faded. “I didn’t join because of the pardon.”

“No?”

“I joined for her,” he said softly. “But now… Now I see the man you are and am all in.”

“Welcome to the team.” Harmon said with a grin.

Riven slipped into the night, a thousand thoughts racing behind that grin.
The tent had barely settled before Harmon's voice came again, quieter this time, meant only for one person. "Weslin."
The boy was already there. He didn't ask if Harmon needed rest or whether the messages could wait until morning. He just brought his ink and sat down beside the parchment stack without a word.
Harmon looked at him for a moment. "Good lad." They worked until the fire outside burned to coals.
The morning broke cool and quiet, the sky was a soft smear of pale gray and rose above the tree line. Harmon stepped out of his tent and paused.

The camp was already in motion. Tents were half-taken down, bedrolls rolled tight. Packs were being strapped to horses, and Garr was speaking in low tones to Torrek and Haldrin beside the gelding they’d been given. The two former prisoners moved with quiet purpose, securing what little they owned to the saddle.

But it wasn’t the bustle that caught attention. It was Harmon himself out a moving around. His posture was straight, and his movements were fluid. The weight he had worn at him, grief, exhaustion, fire, seemed to have slipped from his shoulders in the night. Even without reaching into the deeper pull of the ring on his finger, something in him had settled. Returned.

He looked like his old self. He moved like it, too. The pallor still clung if you stared long enough, and the lines had not vanished. His shaking was gone. The loose edge was gone. He walked over toward the pair of Torrek and Haldrin. 

Ash was nearby, cloak pulled tight, watching the old man move. He didn't say anything. He just exhaled slowly and looked away, the way people do when something they had been quietly dreading doesn't happen after all. 

Harmon just started talking to them as he got close. “Head east to Thornhaven,” he told them, his voice firm but calm. “Remember, keep to the back roads. Thornhaven’s a port town, and ports know how to look away when coin is quiet. There’s a junction just before the bend south of the Whitecap River. Use it. It will carry you downstream to Opindale. Change your look, sooner if you can.”

Torrek gave a grunt that might have meant agreement. Haldrin adjusted the satchel on his back and nodded.

“Eyes will be hunting for you. You can count there is a bounty already out for you,” came a voice behind them. Brother Aldrek stepped into view, freshly armored, his coat drawn tight against the breeze. He handed each man a small coin pouch and a compact supply roll, tied neatly and practically.“No caravans. No open inns. Make yourselves someone else.”

“Thanks for everything,” Haldrin murmured.

“Travel with the light, we will see you in Opindale,” Aldrek said, while making the hand symbol for a blessing of God the Creator towards them. 

Torrek swung up into the saddle first. Haldrin followed, less gracefully. Garr gave a parting tap to the flank of the horse, and they began to ride out.

A few of the others paused to watch them go. Weslin lifted a hand in a small, uncertain wave before letting it fall to his side. Nyssa leaned against a tree, arms crossed. Her expression was unreadable.

“They’ll make it,” Riven said, appearing beside her, sleeves pushed back to his elbows. “Torrek’s a rock. And Haldrin? He’s half-mad with purpose. That’s the dangerous kind.”

She didn’t respond. Her thumb brushed the hilt of her dagger, expression unreadable.

“You’re going to get a reputation,” he added with a sly grin. “Cold exterior. Kind heart. Team’s emotional anchor.”

Nyssa arched a brow. “If you keep talking, I’ll show you just how cold I can be.”

He held up both hands, mock-surrender. “There she is.”

She gave him a small grin.

As the departing pair disappeared into the morning mist, the camp shifted back into movement. Gear was folded and packed, armor strapped, and blades checked. No one needed to be told what to do.

Harmon watched for a moment, his gaze sweeping over the team. Then he turned toward his tent. He smiled faintly to himself.

No orders given. No maps laid out. And yet the camp broke itself like clockwork, quietly, carefully, as if they had been doing this for a lifetime and not for a handful of hard miles.

A soft breeze stirred the ash at his feet, carrying the faint scent of pine and cold earth. The last embers of the fire flickered behind him, thin tendrils of smoke curling up toward the lightening sky, still burning. Still defiant.

Weslin was already moving quickly, breaking down the central supports and rolling their shared sleeping mats. Harmon began to strap his armor piece by piece, methodically, before he finished packing his pack with the clothes of the past day.

They divided the gear between their packs and placed heavier pieces in the saddlebags. Weslin’s smaller pack was nearly full when Aurelia rode up beside them.

“You’re up early,” she said, smiling down at the boy.

“I—yeah,” Weslin said, nearly tripping over himself to stand upright.

“We’ll pick up your training again on the road,” she said, her voice gentle. “Only what you can hold. Controlled. Near the edge, and no farther. Not while we are moving.” She paused and grinned a bit, "And who knows." Her voice lifted slightly. "There may even be time to teach you an illusion or two." 

Weslin’s eyes lit up, his nerves briefly forgotten. He straightened and stood taller.

“Thank you,” he said, voice firmer than usual.

Aurelia’s smile softened. “My pleasure, little flame.”

Weslin drew in a steadying breath, shoulders squaring just slightly. They shared a nod, one of mutual understanding, teacher and student, and she turned her horse away to finish her preparations.

Soon, the last of the tents were down. The fire was ash. Tracks brushed away.

Nothing remained of the camp but footprints and silence.

Harmon looked out over the clearing one last time. What they’d built here, what had formed in the midst of prison escapes, broken orders, and impossible odds, was already gone.

But the fire still burned.

He swung up onto his horse.

“Let’s ride,” he said. “Quiet and tight. Eyes open.” They were off, heading northeast, toward Stormhaven, to answer the unknown.
