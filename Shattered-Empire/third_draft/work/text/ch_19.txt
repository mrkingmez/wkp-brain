Harmon returned to his makeshift command hub, which was dimly lit by amber-glow crystals humming inside wire-caged housings overhead. They cast a sickly light across a broad steel-plated table where maps, parchment scraps, half-burnt candles, and grease-marked mugs formed a chaotic landscape of their own. The stone walls wept condensation, and the forge heat below made the air humid and claustrophobic.

Harmon stood at the table’s head, arms folded tight across his chest, jaw locked.
The others returned not as a team, but as fragments. Shattered pieces of a plan, strained by time and pressure, and the first real cracks of something that had not had time to set. No one spoke at first, but the sound of bootsteps hammering up the stairs, fast and angry, warned Harmon before the first bodies even emerged.
Ash stormed in, jaw tight, eyes flaring. Behind him, Garr, face like a thunderhead, followed close. They were mid-argument and not bothering to lower their voices.
"I told you not to use your full flame in a paper-flooded archive," Garr snapped. "But sure. Scare the clerk half to death."
Ash spun on him. "And I told you if I didn't light up the back hall, we were walking into an ambush. Turns out I was right."
"You scorched half a map! I needed that corridor survey!"
"Then next time move faster and stop breathin' down my neck like a bookkeeper with a grudge."
They pushed into the room, still snapping. Harmon didn't speak. Not yet.
Ash threw down the folded blueprint hard enough to rattle a cup off the edge of the table.
"We got 'em. The library was slow, but Garr talked fast. I just glowered. Worked wonders."
"That's one way to put it," Garr muttered. "Glaring at a clerk like you was sizing him for a coffin."
Ash shot him a sideways look. "He didn't ask for a bribe. You're welcome."
"You elbowed the blueprint drawer off its hinge."
"Boys." Harmon's voice didn't rise. It didn't have to. "Do I look like I care about the drawer?"
Garr and Ash fell quiet. Not peacefully. The tension hung between them like steam off forge steel.
Then more footfalls. Louder. Faster. More than two sets.
Nyssa and Neavin came up the opposite stairwell at speed, boots striking stone too fast for anything but anger. The door burst open behind them hard enough to rattle the lamps in their cages.
"You don't do that in a city." Nyssa crossed the threshold with her hand cutting once through the air, sharp as a blade. "You don't freeze in the open without warning."
"I told you." Neavin turned on her before she'd finished. "I said the connection was slipping. I warned you. If I stop, it shows."
"You didn't stop," she said. "That's the whole problem. You kept walking. Straight into a watched lane like nothing in the world could touch you."
"Because something was there. I felt it."
Nyssa laughed. One short, hard sound with no humor in it at all. "You felt it. Right. While you were chasing ghosts through Veyrith's eyes, I was counting uniforms and doing the math on how fast we'd end up in a room we don't come out of."
"Wasn't your call to make."
"It became my call," she said, stepping closer, "the second your eyes went gray, and you stopped being a person and started being a body I had to keep upright in a crowd." Her voice was tight, controlled, but the edges were starting to fray. "Do you have any idea what that looked like? You were muttering. Your eyes were bleeding light. A child noticed you, Neavin. A child."
"Veyrith had eyes on the prison wall," he said, his voice going flat and low. "Every rooftop approach. Every guard rotation. What I was doin' was worth it."
"Worth it." She said the words slowly, like she was turning them over to find what was wrong with them. "I had to walk a patrol away from you with a smile on my face and a story about my brother who gets spells sometimes, God bless him, he doesn't mean nothin' by it." She shook her head. "Worth it. Sure."
"You covered it."
"I always cover it!" The control cracked. Just for a second. Something raw came through beneath, and she yanked it back hard, her jaw tightening. "That is the problem. I always cover it, and nobody ever once thinks to ask if I am all right after."
The room went very still.
Even Ash didn't move.
Neavin looked at her. Something shifted in his face, something that was not quite regret but was at least adjacent to it. Then it hardened back over.
"Next time I'll let you know," he said. "Before."
"Next time," Nyssa said, quiet now, which was somehow worse, "you tell me what you need and what to expect. Because if I don't know, I can't help you. And if I can't help you, we are both in a cell before sundown, wonderin' where it went wrong."
"Alright, that's enough." Garr stepped forward, broad shoulders filling the space between them, one hand angled toward each. "Y'all are burning rope we don't have."
Ash looked at the ceiling. "Nobody's wrong in here. That's genuinely the worst part."
That cracked something. Voices overlapped. Accusations bled into each other, each one feeding the next instead of burning out. Below, the forge hammers picked up their rhythm again, steady and low, pressing up through the floor like a heartbeat that had never once stopped for anyone. 
Harmon brought his fist down on the table. The sound was brutal. Final.
Everything stopped.
"I don't care who was right," he said. His voice wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. "Answer me one thing. Did you see a deal breaker?"
Neavin swallowed. "No."
"Good." Harmon didn't look at him anymore. "Then the mission stands. Sit down. If you brought something useful, put it on the table."
Neavin crossed the room and laid out his sketch. No defense. No commentary.
Garr sat first, slow and deliberate. Ash dropped into his chair. Nyssa took hers last, arms folded tight, jaw set, eyes still burning like something had been lit in them that wasn't going to go out on its own.
Neavin stayed standing. Breathing hard. Hands clenched at his sides like he didn't trust them not to shake.
Aurelia entered quietly. She moved the way she always moved in rooms like this, like she was passing through rather than joining. Weslin followed just behind her, hesitating in the doorway, pressing back against the frame. The air in the room had weight to it. He could feel it from the threshold.
Aurelia didn't go to the table. She moved to the far wall and turned slightly away, arms folded in against herself. Her fingers flexed once against her sleeve and then stilled, too deliberate to be calm.
"Report," Harmon said.
"Surface guards only." Aurelia did not turn. "Routine thoughts. No external pressure. Nothing sharp enough to linger."
"The Warden?"
"Didn't sense her."
"And the boy?"
"He observed."
Weslin took a step forward. "That was my instruction."
Harmon's gaze cut to him. "That was the plan."
Nyssa seized it. "So while the rest of us are bleeding through patrols and getting our cover stories straight, the two of you stood back and watched."
Aurelia turned then. Slowly. The way a person turns when they have decided to stop pretending something is not happening.
"Why," she said, her voice flat and precise as a ruled line, "are you talking to me?"
Not a question. Something colder than a question.
Nyssa blinked. "Excuse me?"
"I am not asking you to excuse anything." Aurelia's eyes moved across the room, taking in each face with an expression that contained nothing at all. "I am observing that you are all still here, breathing, intact, and apparently furious about it. Which is remarkable, given what could have gone wrong today." A pause. "You want credit for surviving your own morning. That is what this is."
The silence was the kind that happens right before something breaks.
"You want to talk to me about credit." Nyssa's voice had gone quiet. Precise. That was the danger sign. "You walked through a crowd reading minds and came home and you want to stand there and tell me what this is."
"I walked through a crowd doing my job," Aurelia said. "As did you. As did everyone in this room. And yet here we all are, performing our grievances for each other like it changes anything."
"Performing," Garr said the word carefully, like he was checking its weight.
"She ain't wrong," Ash said, not looking up. He sounded tired in a way that went deeper than the morning. "We're all right about everything, and we're all making it worse. That is a real accomplishment for one day."
"I don't need a eulogy, Ash," Nyssa said.
"Wasn't offering one." He finally looked up. "Just saying what everybody already knows."
Neavin's voice came in low and flat from the corner. "Better quiet than reckless. Half-formed visions in a tight street get people pulled in for questioning."
"And a man going blind in the open gets the rest of us flagged." Nyssa did not look at him. "So we're even."
"Are we."
"No." She looked at him then, direct and level. "We're not. But we're out of time to settle it, so it'll keep."
Aurelia had watched the exchange without moving. When the room went quiet again, she said, still in that flat even tone, "If the argument is finished, there is work to be done."
Weslin, still in the doorway, had not made a sound. He was watching Aurelia the way you watch someone you thought you knew do something you don't recognize. Without meaning to, he had taken a half step back into the hall. Not retreating. Just making room for something he didn't have a name for yet. 
Harmon stood. The scrape of his chair was the only sound.
"I am paying for results," he said, each word placed with the deliberate care of a man who does not intend to repeat himself. "This is not a result."
He looked at each of them. Long enough to mean it.
"You don't have to like each other. You don't have to agree. You don't have to trust the reasons behind every call that gets made in this room."
A pause.
"But you will respect that everyone here is good at what they do. Everyone of you. That includes the boy."
Another step forward.
"If you cannot stand next to someone long enough to finish the job," Harmon said, "Then just leave."
Silence. No one moved. No one spoke.
The silence that followed wasn't peace. It was the kind of quiet that happens when people are holding something back hard enough that the effort shows. Glances stayed sharp. Nothing was forgiven. Old grievances sat exactly where they had been dropped.
But the job was still on the table.
And in this room, that was the only thing that mattered.
Aurelia stood near the door through all of it. Hood up, arms folded, watching them with eyes that had gone somewhere else a long time ago and not fully come back. Thousands of thoughts pressing behind her face, all trying to come forward at once. She shook her head once, a small private motion, and when Weslin caught it and stepped toward her, hope already moving across his face, she turned and walked out without a word.
"Aurelia?"
She didn't stop.
He followed her into the outer hall, his voice quieter now, unsure of itself. "We were supposed to train again. Second lesson. You said after."
She stopped just before the stairs. Her back was to him.
"Go meditate," she said.
"What?"
She turned then. Slowly. And the face she turned with was not the one he knew from the morning session. There was nothing warm in it. Nothing patient. Nothing of the woman who had sat across from him with a candle between them and told him that listening was a choice.
"Close your eyes," she said. "Breathe. Maybe you'll become less of a burden while you're at it."
No heat in it. That was the thing that made it land the way it did. She said it the way you say something obvious. The way you say the floor is stone or the sky is gray. It was simply true, and she was tired of being the only one who knew it.
Then she descended the stairs. Footsteps silent. Gone.
Weslin stood in the hall and did not move.
Harmon had watched from the doorway. He waited one full minute. Then he turned and went after her.
He caught her in the corridor. His hand closed around her upper arm, not gently. "What the hell was that?"
She didn't pull away. Didn't tense. Just stood there the way something stands when it has stopped caring whether it is held or released.
"I told him what he needed," she said.
"You told a fourteen-year-old boy to be less of a burden."
"Yes."
Harmon stared at her. "Aurelia."
"He is not fragile," she said. "Treating him like he is does him no favors."
"That's not what that was, and you know it."
Something moved through her face. Fast. Gone before it finished arriving.
"I owe him nothing beyond what keeps him useful," she said.
"I'm not paying you to carve pieces off him," Harmon said. His voice had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with anger and everything to do with someone drawing a line. "I'm paying you to train him. That is the job. If you have something going on inside your head right now that makes that impossible, you tell me, and we figure it out. But you do not take it out on the boy."
"I'm fine."
"You're not."
Her jaw tightened.
"I'm not paying you to fall apart either," Harmon said. "Get it together. Do your job. If you can't, walk away now and save us both the time." He stepped closer, voice dropping. "You think Forgehollow is rough? Wait until you see what the wastes do to people who run out of people willing to stand next to them."
A long silence.
Then Aurelia nodded. Once. The motion precise and small and empty of everything except compliance.
"Understood," she said.
Her breath hitched. Just once. Something sharp caught beneath her ribs and then was gone, pressed back behind a stillness so practiced it had become indistinguishable from calm.
Harmon let go of her arm.
"Good," he said.
She disappeared into the shadows. He stood a moment in the corridor, the forge's heartbeat pressing up through the floor beneath him, slow and indifferent, and thought about what a woman who had lived that long and worn that many faces looked like when one of them finally started to slip. Then he turned and went back toward the tavern floor.
The main room had settled into the particular quiet that comes after the lunch crowd thins and before the evening crowd finds its courage. A few men at corner tables. A barmaid moving between them without hurry. The fire in the hearth had burned down to coals, and nobody had bothered to feed it.
Dain was at the bar. A glass in front of him that he hadn't touched, one elbow on the wood, watching the room the way men like him always watch rooms, cataloguing without appearing to. He glanced at Harmon when he approached, but said nothing. Just waited.
They stood together in the kind of silence that happens between people who have been in enough difficult rooms that they no longer need to fill the space before the information arrives.
Aldrek came through the door two minutes later. He didn't slow at the threshold. He crossed directly to them, reached into his coat without preamble, and set the folded parchment on the bar in front of Harmon the way a man sets down something he would rather not be carrying any longer.
The black wax seal had already been broken. The twin-tailed fox was still faintly visible where it had split.
Dain looked at it. Then at Aldrek. Said nothing.
Harmon unfolded the note. The words were short. Casual. Precise in the way only cruelty ever manages to be.
Would you be a good chap and let Nyssa know I never forget a debt. —M.
The parchment bent slightly under his fingers. A controlled motion. The kind of man makes when he is deciding how much of what he is feeling he can afford to show.
"He didn't guess," Harmon said quietly. "He has eyes on us. On her. Probably on all of us."
Dain's jaw tightened. He looked at the note without touching it, the way you look at something that has already decided what it is going to cost you. "How long do you think he's had them?"
"Long enough to connect her name to yours," Harmon said. "That's all that matters right now."
Aldrek had not moved from where he stood. His hands were flat on the bar, his eyes on the middle distance. "Merrick is not a man who sends a message like this and waits to see if it lands," he said. "He already knows it will. This is not a warning. It is a demonstration."
"He's telling us he can reach us," Dain said.
"He's telling us he already has," Aldrek said.
The fire in the hearth popped once. Nobody looked at it.
Harmon folded the note. Set it down flat. "We still have to get Riven tonight. That doesn't change."
Dain was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man raising something he already suspects will not land well. "We don't even know if he'll agree to come with us. Why risk everything for a man who might refuse?"
Harmon didn't answer right away. Not because he couldn't. Because the true answer sat in a place that was more personal than strategic, and saying it out loud in a tavern with a foreman's note on the bar between them would make it sound like sentiment rather than calculation, and he could not afford for it to sound like either. "His choice isn't the mission," he said finally. "Removing him from Merrick is."
Dain looked at him. Read the rest of it in his face. The part Harmon wasn't saying. He exhaled through his nose. "Then we get him," he said. "I'll take your word on it."
Aldrek bowed his head slightly. His eyes closed. He drew one slow breath, the kind a man draws when he is reaching for something from a depth he does not visit lightly. When he opened his eyes again, what had been doubt had settled into something quieter and harder and more durable.
"Right," he said. "Up we go."
Harmon handed the note back to Aldrek. The three of them moved toward the stairs without discussion. The decision made the way decisions get made between people who have each independently arrived at the same place and are simply acknowledging it.
The stairwell was narrow and damp, old condensation slicking the stone walls, a single sconce stuttering at the first landing. Harmon adjusted his collar. Dain cracked his knuckles without realizing he'd done it. Aldrek climbed with his hands loose at his sides and his eyes forward, the practiced ease of a man who has walked toward worse than a room full of arguing professionals.
Before they reached the top, the argument came down to meet them.
Raised voices. The sharp, particular edge of people who have been holding things in and run out of reasons to keep doing it. Ash's voice unmistakable. Garr answered with the rigid restraint of a man who has decided that if he raises his voice, something worse would follow.
Dain tilted his head slightly. "Sounds like we're late."
"Then we end it," Aldrek said and pushed the door open.
The room fell silent the way rooms do when the people in them realize someone has been watching.
Ash stood near the table, blueprint half-unrolled and forgotten at his elbow, soot still smudged across the back of his hand. Garr was by the hearth, arms crossed, the tension in his shoulders the kind that doesn't leave just because the shouting stopped. Weslin had pressed himself against the far wall, eyes narrow, taking everything in and giving nothing back. Nyssa stood rigid, arms folded tight across her chest, jaw set hard. Neavin sat at the corner of the table, fingers tapping an uneven rhythm against the wood, not looking at anyone.
Every head turned.
Dain stepped in first. Measured. The slight lift at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile and not quite anything else. "Did we interrupt something important?" he said. "Or is this the rehearsal before someone says something unforgivable?"
No one laughed.
Aldrek followed, drawing off his gloves. "We need to speak."
Ash snorted. "Perfect. That's exactly what this needs. A sermon."
Harmon stepped forward. He didn't raise his voice. He looked around the room once, long enough that each person in it understood they had been seen and accounted for, and then he unfolded the parchment and turned it so the broken seal caught the firelight.
"You're right," Aldrek said. "We're not steady. Trust is thin. Tempers are close to the edge." A pause. "But this changes the situation." Aldrek held the note up so the broken seal caught the light.  
Nyssa's eyes found the fox sigil and stayed there. The color left her face before she could stop it. The smirk she wore like a second skin, the one that had been there through every argument and every sharp word and every moment this morning she'd had to swallow something she didn't want, slipped. Just for a second. Just enough to show what was underneath it. Her hands had gone flat against the table's edge. Not gripping. Just resting there, like she needed to know something solid was under them.
Nobody mentioned it.
"It means someone is close enough to connect names," Aldrek said, his voice even and deliberate. "And willing to use them."
Dain moved a half step forward, eyes still on the seal. "I don't know Merrick well," he said. "But I know men like him. Men who send messages like this don't bluff." His jaw tightened slightly. "He's telling us to walk away from Riven. And if we don't, he intends to act."
The room took that in. Nobody moved. Nobody spoke.
Harmon let the silence settle just long enough to mean something. Then he cut through it. "If anyone wants out," he said, "that moment has passed."
Nothing.
"Good," he said. "Then we stop burning time."
He looked around the room one more time. The same faces. The same bruises underneath them. "Whatever eyes are on the street right now will only sharpen if we hesitate. That means we move tonight."
The tension didn't disappear.
It aligned.
The difference was small, and it mattered more than anything else in the room.
Neavin leaned forward first. His fingers stopped their rhythm against the wood. "Outer patrols thin just after the second bell," he said. "Not on paper. The guards rotate inward for meal breaks. It's by habit, not orders. Which means it'll hold."
Nyssa's eyes moved to him. Not warm. Not hostile. The look of someone listening to information they need from a source they are not finished being angry at. "West alley watch goes lax when the food carts come through," she said. "I saw it twice. Everyone's eyes follow the bread."
They did not look at each other when she finished confirming his count. They didn't need to. The information fit together, and they both knew it, and neither one of them was going to say so out loud. A few of the others almost smiled. Almost.
Aldrek spoke next. "I walked the perimeter at dusk. There's a stretch on the north face where the guards are running on instinct more than attention. They've done the same route long enough that they've stopped seeing it. That's where they'll be softest."
Weslin had been quiet through all of it, back against the wall, watching. He swallowed once. Then he straightened slightly, the way someone does when they have decided to say a thing and are committing to it before the nerve runs out.
"The bakery deliveries don't just pull the guards," he said. "They pull everyone. Vendors, porters, message runners. The whole street thickens." He glanced up, more confident now. "If we move inside that, we're not two people walking toward a prison. We're just part of the crowd."
No one told him he was wrong.
Harmon leaned over the blueprint. The access corridor ran behind the loading bay on the west side, wide enough that two men carrying something heavy between them wouldn't draw a second look from anyone who wasn't specifically looking for it. Twelve minutes between the rotation and the next watch. Thirteen if the bread cart ran late, which, in Harmon's experience, it usually did.
The door from the stairwell creaked. Aurelia stepped back into the room. She didn't announce herself. Didn't look at anyone directly. Just moved to stand near the door the way she had stood near it before, and the room absorbed her the way it absorbs a drop of cold water, a small shift in temperature that everyone notices and nobody names.
The silence stretched one beat longer than it should have. "Crowds make minds noisy," she said quietly. "That works in our favor."
Weslin was looking at her. She didn't look back. Whatever had happened in the hall was still sitting between them, not resolved, not addressed, just present the way certain things are present in rooms when the people who share them have run out of time to deal with them properly.
Garr straightened from the hearth. "If it breaks," he said, simple and flat, "I'll hold the corridor."
Harmon watched the last piece settle. No posturing. No performance. Just people who were very good at staying alive in bad situations doing the work of staying alive in one more bad situation, and carrying everything that had happened this morning inside them like stones in a coat pocket, heavy and unspoken and not going anywhere.
"Alright," Harmon said. "That's enough to work with." He stepped to the table and rolled the blueprint fully open. The paper was worn at the folds, the corridors marked in two different hands from two different sources, and the west loading bay was circled twice.
"We go in fast," he said. "Clean if we can manage it. Loud if we can't. Either way, we don't linger. We get Riven out, and we're gone before anyone thinks to ask the right questions." He looked around the room. At each of them. "We came here for him. We're not leaving without him."
No one answered with words.
They leaned in over the blueprint, shoulders almost touching, the amber crystals buzzing overhead, and below them, the forge hammers kept their rhythm, steady and indifferent, the way the work always is, not caring who picks it up, only that it gets done.
