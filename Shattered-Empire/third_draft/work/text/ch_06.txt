Weslin sat stirring his stew, his grip tight on the spoon, the broth gone lukewarm. From his place on the Grand Balcony, he had a clear view of the long tables stretching down the hall. Banners of Aldoria, Opindale, and Moradaer draped high from the rafters, their silks gleaming in torchlight, the weight of nations on full display. Yesterday he would have pressed his face to the railing to see it better. Tonight the whole display felt like it was happening to someone else.
He shifted in his seat, the wooden chair creaking under him. His stomach was a knot, food forgotten. Harmon had given him one task. Watch. Listen. Come back with something useful. It had seemed simple enough in the corridor when Harmon's hand was on his shoulder and the instruction was clear. Up here, surrounded by pages and body servants taking their breaks between duties, it felt considerably less simple.
He had tried to join a conversation near the railing. Two older pages had looked at him the way people look at someone they do not recognize and gone back to talking. A kitchen girl had moved away when he sat near her. He did not know any of them. He was Weslin from the west docks, the baker's son who had been a page for less than a day, wearing a new cloak and a pin he did not fully understand. He might as well have been invisible.
Which was exactly the problem. The balcony was supposed to be the advantage. Pages and body servants heard everything, not the speeches and toasts the nobles performed for each other, but the real things, the whispered things, the things said in corridors and behind hands. Harmon had to have known that. That had to be why he sent Weslin up here instead of keeping him on the floor. But knowing it and actually hearing it were two very different things when no one on the balcony wanted to talk to him.
He wiped his palms on his tunic. He had heard nothing useful. Nothing at all. Just the noise of the hall below and the low murmur of conversations he was not part of.
He set his spoon down. Closed his eyes.
He was not sure why. It was not a plan. It was the thing you do when you are trying harder than trying seems to be working, when you press your hands over your ears to hear better, when you hold your breath to see further. He told himself he was focusing. He told himself that if he could just shut out the noise of the hall for one moment, he might catch something.
He caught something.
Not a sound. Not exactly. More like the edge of a sound, the feeling of words pressed through a wall. Close. Closer than the hall below. He opened his eyes.
The servants near him had not moved. Their mouths were still. The kitchen girl was refilling a goblet three seats down, her eyes on the work, her face showing nothing. Two pages were watching the musicians below. No one was speaking.
But something was.
She smiled at me when I brought the second platter. She has never done that before.
It arrived so clearly that Weslin turned to find who had spoken. The page nearest him was staring straight at the hall below, his jaw set, his lips perfectly still. His face was the face of someone performing indifference very carefully. He had not said a word.
Weslin looked back at the kitchen girl. She was still pouring wine, completely unaware.
He pressed back against his chair. His heart was going faster than it should. He looked away, looked at the banners, looked at the floor, looked at anything that was not the people sitting near him.
Another one came through.
If Aldric folds before the third hand, I will have his boots by midnight.
Weslin blinked. Across the balcony, three pages were gathered near the back wall, not talking, all of them watching the hall below with the studied innocence of boys waiting for something. One of them was turning a playing card over and over in his fingers, slow and absent, like a man who does not know his hands are moving.
Now is the time.
That one was different. Heavier. It did not feel like a page thinking about cards or a boy thinking about a girl. It felt like something with a purpose behind it, a thought attached to a decision already made. Weslin looked down at the hall below, scanning the tables, but he could not tell where it had come from or who had sent it.
More came, and they did not wait for him to be ready.
The grey cloak. Do not look at the grey cloak.
She knows. She has always known. I will deal with it after.
My feet are killing me. Four more hours of this.
He asked me to prepare the east wing guest room. Third door. Told me to tell no one, not even the head steward. Gave me a coin to keep my mouth shut. I kept it.
I should have eaten before the shift.
If the envoy leaves early, it means the letter did not reach him. If he stays, it means it did.
They came faster now, none of them waiting, none of them announcing themselves, a page worrying about his aching back arriving in the same breath as something that felt like treason and something that felt like a boy deciding whether to apologize to his sister. Weslin could not sort them. He could not slow them. He could not tell which ones mattered and which ones were just the noise inside someone's head at the end of a long night, because they all felt equally loud and equally real, and none of them were his.
He gripped the edge of the table. His spoon rattled in the bowl.
The thoughts tightened around him, not painful yet, but pressing, the way a crowd presses when it gets too close, when there is no longer enough air. Someone's guilt. Someone's hunger. Someone's cold, specific dread belonged to a face he could not find in the hall below. A flash of something that might have been grief or might have been fury and was gone before he could tell the difference.
He turned his head, slow and deliberate, and looked at the servants again. Still silent. Still moving. Not one of them was looking at him. Not one of them was aware that anything had changed.
His palms were soaking through his tunic.
Whatever this is, he thought, stop.
It did not stop. It pulled back slightly, the way a tide pulls back before it decides what to do next. He sat very still and breathed and told himself he had imagined it. He had been trying so hard to hear something useful that he had started inventing voices. That was what had happened. That was the only thing that made sense.
He almost had himself convinced when a familiar voice broke through everything.
"You look like you swallowed a loaf and regretted it," Allison said.
Weslin turned to see Allison sliding onto the bench beside him, quick as a sparrow. She snatched a roll from his plate, tearing into it with a grin.
“That’s the face you’re making,” she added between bites. “Somewhere between terrified and trapped.”
Weslin groaned. “I’m fine… aren’t you supposed to be in bed?” 
“Of course. And I’m the Queen of Opindale,” she said, nudging his arm. “Snuck out. So? How does it feel, Sir Squire?”
He nearly choked. “How… how did that get to you?”
Allison’s grin widened. “Pages see everything. Even from the shadows.”
For a moment, her smile faltered, a quick flicker that Weslin almost missed. There was something beneath it, envy, maybe sadness—but she buried it fast behind another teasing smirk.
“You idiot,” her voice softened, yanking him into a hug. “Look at you, I’m glad it was you who got it.”
The words eased something in his chest. Weslin hugged her back, relief softening his nerves. “Thanks,” he whispered.
She pulled away, ruffling his hair. “Still an idiot. And now you’re Harmon’s idiot. That’s worse.”
Weslin chuckled, shaking his head. “Don’t worry. I’m not about to forget where I came from.”
“Good,” she said with mock sternness, giving him a playful shove. “Then eat your stew before you pass out in those fancy boots.”
Weslin smiled, the weight of his task easing for a moment. Some of the pressure on his chest lifted.
A shadow blotted out the torchlight. Weslin did not need to turn around. His body knew before he did, the shoulders tightening, the jaw setting, the particular cold that had nothing to do with the hall's temperature. 
“Well, look who crawled up into the balcony,” Braden Marloch drawled with a mocking tone, his smirk returned, deeper. “The little sparrow himself. Thought you could flap your way above us?”
Weslin’s stomach knotted hard. Years of sneers, shoves, and whispered humiliations pressed down all at once. He had prayed Braden wouldn’t notice him here, a foolish hope.
“You always find me, don’t you?” Weslin muttered, his voice sharper than he intended.
Braden leaned close, the swagger of a guildmaster’s son dripping from him. Joran and Malric, his ever-present shadows, chuckled behind. “Someone is bold tonight, and look at his new cloak and pin. A baker’s brat dressed up in borrowed feathers. Do you really think anyone here believes you belong?”
The words cut sharply, just as they always had. But this time Weslin forced himself to look up, to meet Braden’s eyes. Fear in his heart, but courage in his voice, he said, “Better a baker’s son than a parasite clinging to his father’s name.”
Joran’s laugh cut off. Braden’s grin twitched.
“Careful,” Braden said softly, then shoved him back against the bench, hard enough to rattle the spoon in his bowl. “You think Harmon will keep you long? You’re a page, nothing more. He’ll toss you aside like the scraps from your father’s oven.”
The venom hit deep. Weslin’s throat burned, but he spat back, “At least scraps have worth. They feed people. What do you feed, Braden, besides your own pride?”
For a heartbeat, the sneer faltered. Then Braden’s hand clamped Weslin’s shoulder, grinding down. “You’ll regret this,” Braden said. “I should’ve broken you when I had the chance. Maybe then you’d know your place.”
The pressure wasn’t just on Weslin’s shoulder. It pressed into his head, heavy and invasive. Thoughts not his own clawed at his mind. ‘I should be the squire. Not him. He doesn’t deserve it. He never deserved anything. I’ll break him.’
Weslin gasped, clutching the table’s edge. Allison stood abruptly, eyes sharp. “Enough, Braden,” Allison said, stepping between them. “You’ve already proven what you are.”
He shot her a look, cruel and mocking. “Stay out of this, stable-warmer. Or maybe I will remind you too where you belong.” He made a rude gesture with his other hand. Joran shoved her down. The three bullies laughed. Braden continued, “Stay down there, everyone knows you're used to it.” 
Something in Weslin snapped. The years of dread, the years of humiliation, the heat building in his chest, he could not cage it anymore.
Burn! The word wasn’t spoken. It exploded out of him.
Braden looked down at his hands and screamed. That scream came out of him like something tearing loose. He stumbled back, clawing at his own arms, his chest, ripping at his collar. "I'm burning," he screamed. "I'm burning, I'm on fire, get it off, get it off." He spun, slapping at flames that no one standing near him could see. His skin was unmarked. His clothes were whole. Nothing in the room had changed.
Only Braden, on his knees now, is still trying to put out a fire that was not there
Weslin felt it, the raw connection, Braden’s pain searing through his own skull. For a moment, he thought it would tear him apart, then it vanished, leaving silence.
Braden collapsed in a heap, gasping, trembling. He choked out in a weak voice, "It's inside me, I'm burning, make it stop." 
Weslin didn’t wait. He bolted, boots hammering stone, heart slamming in his chest. Don’t stop. Don’t look back. Find Harmon. Allison hurried back to her feet and ran after him. 
Weslin ran for the stairs, turning right into the Grand Hall. He had barely taken two steps when pain struck like a hammer to the skull. His knees buckled. He crashed to the marble floor, clutching his head as the world spun. It was not just pain. It was a flood. A thousand voices poured into him at once, shouting, whispering, snarling secrets they thought no one else could hear.
He is too young for her.
The Governor will be moved soon. He is too weak.
Can’t trust Ayhmeri, not after the last war.
If the Moradaer envoy knew what I sold, I’d be dead by morning.
My debts will ruin me before winter comes.
Weslin gasped, his breath ragged. The thoughts tore through him like shards of glass, overlapping until he could not tell one from another. He squeezed his eyes shut, palms grinding against his temples as if he could press the noise out.
“Weslin!” Allison was suddenly at his side, dropping to her knees, hands gripping his shoulders. Wes." Her voice dropped to something tight and quiet, the teasing completely gone. "Talk to me. Right now. What is it?" 
“I… I don’t know,” he choked. “There are too many.”
Allison glanced around. Nobles at nearby tables began to turn, their conversations faltering as eyes drifted toward the disturbance. A murmur rippled outward. No one rose. No one called for aid. A few leaned closer to their companions, voices lowered behind lifted goblets, watching without appearing to watch. A few exchanged passing glances, more irritated than concerned, as if the moment were an inconvenience rather than an interruption. Servants continued their routes with practiced precision, though their steps slowed just enough to betray their awareness.
Allison, knowing the wrong eyes were on them, tried to get him off the floor. “Come on, get up,” she urged, hooking an arm under his and hauling him up with surprising strength. “We need to go,” Allison said urgently. “Before anyone starts asking questions.”
Across the hall, Harmon had been deep in talk with Lord Alric Valmere when he saw Weslin collapse. His jaw tightened. He had known. He had known, and he had moved too fast again. He bowed his head briefly to Valmere. “If you would excuse me, my lord, there is something urgent I must see to.” Valmere started to protest, then followed Harmon’s gaze and fell silent, giving a stiff nod.
By the time Harmon reached them, Allison had Weslin half-stumbling toward the side corridor. Harmon caught the boy’s arm, steadying him. Harmon’s grip tightened slightly. “Breathe, lad. Just breathe.” His voice was firm, grounding Weslin against the storm.
They found a bench in the corridor where Weslin collapsed, shaking. The voices were already fading, but they left him drained, his breathing ragged. He clung to Allison and Harmon both, wide-eyed.
Harmon knelt in front of him. "I moved too fast." He said it quietly, the way he said things he meant completely. "That is mine to carry, not yours." 
Weslin slumped on the bench, chest heaving, hands still trembling. He tried to speak when Harmon asked what happened, but his throat caught, words spilling out in gasps.
Allison leaned forward quickly, her voice firm though her eyes were wide with worry. “He collapsed in the hall,” Allison said quickly, “Came down from the balcony. Braden had been cornering him up there. Then one second, he was moving, the next, he was on the floor, clutching his head like something was tearing him apart. Everyone saw it.” Her fear showed in the pace at which she was talking. 
Weslin forced out a ragged whisper. “Voices… too many. Everywhere. They won’t stop.”
Allison squeezed his hand tighter, looking to Harmon with frightened confusion. “You know something. What is happening to him? What can I do?”
Harmon’s gaze lingered on the pin at Weslin’s collar, the faint gleam of the yellow shard catching the light. He drew a slow breath. “That confirms it.”
Allison straightened. “What? Tell me.”
Harmon met her stare, then turned back to Weslin. “You are an Adept, Weslin.” He let the words settle. “A Yellow. I had my suspicion when we first met. I ran a test. That,” he pointed to the pin Weslin was wearing. “The pin. I placed a slice of a yellow shard. If I were right, it could have helped you tonight. I misjudged the timing. You were not ready. I thought the feast would work in our favor. Enough noise, enough minds, enough to blunt the first opening. I did not account for how strong the first opening would be.”
Weslin blinked, his eyes wide and wet. “An… Adept?”
“Yes,” Harmon said gently. “You are my squire now, and as part of your training, you will learn to master this gift as surely as you will learn to wield a blade. Don’t worry about it now.”
Allison pulled Weslin against her side, holding him close as though she could shield him. “So all of that… it was real?”
“Yes,” Harmon admitted. 
Weslin pressed both hands to his face, his body shaking with relief and fear mingled. He whispered through his palms. “I didn’t want to hurt him, I don’t want to hurt anyone.”
Weslin drew a few more uneven breaths, then lowered his hands. Harmon gave him a nod. “Now, tell me what happened. As much as you can remember.”
Welsin haltingly recounted Braden’s taunts, the shoves. He tells these boys about his past encounters with him. Then the shove Allison, causing a sudden rush of anger that flared into something more. His words shook, but the truth spilled out. “I thought it. I wanted him to burn. And he did. I felt it in my head, like the thought left me and struck him. I never wanted… I never meant…” His voice broke, shame choking the last words.
"I know," he said. "That is why I am going to teach you." Harmon was still for a moment. He looked at Weslin with an expression that had no name for it exactly, not pity, not pride, something older than both. Then he exhaled once, slow and quiet, the way a man exhales when he has heard a true thing and is deciding how to carry it. He nodded once. Harmon leaned in, eyes intent. “That was projection, Weslin. “You did it in the grip of raw emotion. That is why it was so dangerous. I will teach you to control it and not hurt anyone,” Harmon said, steady as iron. “My promise.” His words softened the blow. 
Allison gave his hand one last squeeze, then stood reluctantly. “I should go. If I do not, my mother will send the guard to drag me back home. She thinks I am still sick.” She bent down and hugged him tightly. “Be careful, Wes. Please.”
“I will,” he whispered.
She offered Harmon a small bow. “Thank you for helping him.” She stood. Looked at Harmon for a moment with eyes that were doing several things at once. "Keep him in one piece," she said. "He is the only friend I have who does not bore me." Then she pulled her cloak straight and walked back toward the noise like someone who had never been anywhere else. 
Harmon gave her a small smile, “He is in good hands, I promise.”
Allison gave them both one last glance, then hurried down the corridor and disappeared into the hall. The silence she left behind seemed heavier. Harmon rose to his feet. “We cannot linger. The King has given us leave. We ride before dawn. Tonight, we make ready.”
Weslin, still unsteady but determined, pushed himself upright. His legs wobbled. His breath hitched, then leveled. “Where do we go first…?”
Harmon placed a steadying hand on his shoulder. “First, we will go get changed. There is a meeting we must attend.”
Weslin met his eyes, fear and resolve mingling in equal measure. Harmon gave him a firm nod, then turned toward the corridor’s shadows.
“Come, lad. It is time.”
And together, they walked into the night.
