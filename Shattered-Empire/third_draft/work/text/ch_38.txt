Governor Meridian Drake stood at the window of his guest chambers and watched the feast burn below him.

Torchlight blurred against the frost-glazed glass, glinting across the golden gowns and deep violet banners. Every courtyard glowed with lanterns strung high in curling threads of firelight. Minstrels played. Nobles laughed, a thousand lives carried on in choreographed joy.

And he hated them for it.

This was supposed to be a triumph. The summit reaffirmed the Accord. A celebration of peace, prosperity, and shared strength. Ten days of culture, song, and open tables, a living reminder that the fractured kingdoms of man could still come together.

Instead, for him, it had become a funeral stretched over nights.

He hadn’t eaten since yesterday morning and hadn’t spoken more than a handful of words aloud. Even the simplest acts, such as shaving, buttoning his tunic, walking down the hall to retrieve wine, had become hollow routines, performed like a ghost replaying its death.

The cold helped. It kept his hands from shaking too visibly. He pressed his fingertips to the window’s edge, feeling the pane’s bite. From this vantage, Opindale’s southern towers stretched like cathedral pillars into the dark. The city looked invincible. Ancient. Eternal.
But he knew better now. He knew how fragile it all truly was. He knew what they wanted him to break. The dagger sat behind him, in a violet-lined box on his writing table. He hadn’t touched it tonight. He didn’t need to. Its presence saturated the air.
He could still feel it, like a second heartbeat, off-tempo, intrusive, marking the seconds he had left. He still didn’t know what it truly was. He only knew what it meant. He had opened the box two nights ago. It had arrived sometime after the fifth day of the feast, after the warning. There had been no crest, no markings, just a note tucked beneath the silk.
‘To aid you in your task.’

At first, he thought it a mistake. A cruel jest. But then Colton came. No guards. No bluster. Just a folded slip of parchment in one gloved hand… and a lock of his daughter’s hair in the other. The precise honey-brown she braided every morning.

That had been four nights ago. Colton hadn’t needed to say the rest. The steward’s voice had been calm, pleasant, almost warm as he detailed the terms.

“If the king still draws breath when the final bell tolls on the tenth night… the family will be processed. Efficiently. Irreversibly.”

Not exile. Not imprisonment. Not even reeducation. Elimination. All of them. His bloodline, cut at the root. Drake turned from the window and looked at himself in the tall mirror near the chamber’s hearth.

He looked like a man carved from fatigue. The silk robe hung loose on one shoulder. His jaw had stubble. His eyes were hollow. There was blood at the corner of one fingernail, torn earlier from a reflexive clench. He didn’t remember doing it.

He didn’t remember sitting down, either. But there he was, lowered onto the edge of the settee, hands folded in his lap like a child awaiting punishment.

“This was supposed to be a celebration,” he whispered to the room.

His voice didn’t echo. He blinked slowly and looked again at the box on the desk. The dagger lay on violet silk. Thin. Almost delicate. The hilt was plain except for the shimmer, a faint pulse of color that moved like something alive and slow. Nothing about it looked like a weapon. That was the worst part. 

The dagger didn’t glow. It didn’t whisper. It didn’t move. And yet, it demanded more from him than any throne, any enemy, any oath he had ever made. He had once withstood a siege without flinching. Had argued down two belligerent kings in the Accord’s founding summit. But now? Now he could barely draw breath.

A knock. Soft. Polite. Final. The door creaked open before he could rise. Colton entered. Impeccable. Unhurried. Not a crease out of place. The Head Steward of the palace in Opindale closed the door with silent precision and moved across the room with the easy grace of a man walking into a library rather than a battlefield.

“Governor Drake,” he said smoothly. “Forgive the hour.”

Drake didn’t answer.

Colton smiled, as if that was expected. He stopped before the desk and looked down at the open box. The dagger rested inside, untouched.

“I see it’s still there.”

Drake’s voice, when it came, was hoarse. “I wanted to know what it does.”

Colton gave a soft, approving nod. “Curiosity is a noble instinct. Most would fear to ask.”

"Does it kill?" Drake asked. "Or… does it do something worse?" 

Colton didn’t blink. “It does not kill, Governor. It replaces.”

Drake’s breath caught.

Colton continued, tone gentle as snowfall.

“It swaps the soul of the one it pierces with a prepared vessel. Crafted, tuned, and aligned to the exact magical signature of the target. The bloodline does not notice. The wards do not notice. The magic itself accepts the new occupant as the rightful king.”

Drake felt his stomach turn.

“Edric would die,” he whispered.

“And the world would never know,” Colton said softly. “To the Accord, to the realm, to the gods themselves, he would still reign. But every word from his mouth… would be ours.”

The fire hissed low in the hearth.

“And if I don’t?” Drake asked, not looking at him.

Colton’s voice didn’t change. “You already know the answer, Governor. Do you really want me to say it aloud?”

Silence.

“You don’t need to decide now,” Colton said, turning toward the door. “Only before the tenth flame dies.”

He opened the door partway, pausing in the threshold.

“For what it’s worth,” he added, with a faint smile, “this is history, Governor. You should feel honored to be at its fulcrum.”

Drake flinched as the door latched shut, not from fear, but from how quiet it was. He remained seated, staring at nothing. The dagger waited behind him. And in the dark glass of the window, his reflection no longer looked like a governor. It looked like a ghost, one that hadn’t yet decided who to haunt.

Drake soon left his quarters. He closed the door behind him and paused just outside it. The click of the latch echoed like a warning. The halls of Opindale’s upper palace were quiet this time of night. The kind of stillness that gnawed at the corners of a man’s thoughts.

He stood there for a breath, then another, adjusting the cuffs of his formal coat with a precision that masked the tremble beneath his fingers. The weight of the dagger, Whisperfang, rested inside the lining, light as a whisper, heavier than guilt.

The walk to the ballroom stretched before him like a corridor through his conscience.

Torches flickered along the polished stone walls, casting long, distorted shadows that followed him whether he walked toward the light or away from it. The opulence of the corridor, with its ornate columns and marble reliefs of past Accord victories, only pressed harder on the part of him that still remembered what he was supposed to be. He had walked this path a dozen times before, sometimes beside the King, a few times ahead of honored guests, never like this.

Never with the world teetering just behind his ribs.

He passed the statue of Veydras the Peacemaker and slowed, not in tribute, but in dread. His legs didn’t want to go any further. His knees ached beneath his state robes. His heart refused to beat. Even his breath was careful now, as if any misstep might trigger something final.

Once, he might have paused to admire the view from the open gallery, the garden spires lit with enchantment, the bridge of nations draped with silks bearing the sigils of three proud kingdoms. But now it all looked hollow. A stage for dancers who didn't know the play was ending.

He descended the final staircase slowly, boots measured on the velvet runner.

Then, with a final breath, he stepped into the light.

The ballroom shimmered with warmth and polished grandeur, a jewel of light and pageantry set against the cold political games of the day. Candlelight flickered through crystal fixtures, and laughter curled like smoke between gilded balconies. Nobles from three nations mingled in sweeping arcs, like stars in a slow, deadly constellation.

But Drake saw none of it.

He moved like a man walking through a painting, part of it, yet somehow not. Murmurs greeted him. Bowed heads. Raised glasses. But every gesture, every voice, passed through him.

Ten nights of peace. Once, that would have meant something. A promise to future generations, forged by sacrifice and held through blood and diplomacy alike. It should have been a joyful occasion. Instead, it was a funeral in slow motion.

He was late, as most of the lords had already found their places among the colonnades and crystal fountains. He made his way to the high table and took his seat.
Music swirled from the dais. Gowned nobles glided through courtly waltzes beneath chandeliers strung with lightglass orbs. Diplomats from across the continent laughed with small glasses in hand, each gesture precise, rehearsed, sharpened by years of performance.

And there, at the heart of it all, stood King Edric Wyman and Queen Mirelle.

Drake’s breath caught before he could stop it. They weren’t just regal, they were… alive. Their hands found each other easily, naturally, as the gravity of affection pulled them together. His thumb brushed over hers mid-step. Her eyes softened with the touch. Not a performance. A marriage of equals. The sight burned. If he went through with it, he wouldn’t be ending a symbol. He’d be extinguishing a man and destroying the woman beside him.

What a celebration! What a renewal! Ten days of torture. They saw it as a time to gather allies, settle rivalries, remember the long wars, and raise a glass to their absence. He thought, 'Once I would have raised a glass with them. That man is dead.’ The weight on Drake’s shoulders dragged him further with every breath.

He took in his surroundings, looking for a way out. He saw Lord Ferric of Varendor, jaw locked in fury as he debated an Opindale port official about grain routing agreements through Aldoria. Ferric's voice cut through the noise. "You gave them favorable rates. You starved our southern lines." Then it blended in with the music, the chatter, all the different sounds pounded in his head.

He moved, needing to escape. Not far from them, three Moradaer spellwrights stood under a silver-braided banner, dressed in robes the color of extinguished coal. They moved like a single organism. One, Lady Veysa, tapped a finger to her temple and muttered, “If you let them into the Accord, you dilute it. It becomes a market, not a pact.”

Drake passed through them like a ghost. Mouths moved around him. He stopped hearing the words. Too many titles. Too many smiles. Too many handshakes meant knives.

Their whispers followed him. As he made his way to the balcony to take in a deep breath, that was when the tears started to fall. He stood there until he was empty and then until he was decided. A long breath. The cold air on his face. He turned, wearing a smile that didn't belong to him. Whatever it took. Whatever it cost. He would not let them die. 

As he made his way back to his table, a voice caught him. “The Northern Pass resurfaced, with whispers of reopening it and lifting travel restrictions for those with the means or a cause. Imagine the profit.” Lord Carven of Velmor was saying. He was a silver-bearded and loud-lunged businessman who offered expedition shares to anyone who would “clear the first ridge.” Most dismissed it as suicide, but too many leaned in, already counting what they'd make if the numbers held.

‘If they only knew.’ He slowed at the fountain terrace. He caught the end of a conversation between an Opindale trade minister and a merchant from Dornmere. “Stormglass,” the Dornman said, “is the future of locomotion and lighting both. If you want a share, speak now.” The minister smiled, but his eyes flicked to the King’s dais, as if weighing how long Edric might still be king.

Even among allies, the air bristled.

Everyone played their game. Everyone reached. Drake used to admire it. Now he only saw a table set with poisoned fruit.

He pressed a hand to one of the marble columns, steadying himself. A servant passed, and Drake waved him away before he could speak. He didn’t need wine. He needed silence.

He found it in the reflection of the window.

His face looked pale, the dark shadows beneath his eyes now permanent. His posture was still stately, but it was only maintained by years of training and the last remnant of discipline. He ran a gloved hand down his jaw.

Five days since the message. One left.

And still… no answer.

The knife was there beneath his coat. A thin, impossible thing with a violet shimmer at the hilt. Whisperfang. They had told him that if he used it on the king before the tenth day’s end, then his family would be spared and not reeducated. Not executed. Spared. Moved to safety. Respected for their station.

He wanted to believe it.

He couldn’t.

Above him, on the roofline of the eastern tower, a shadow perched motionless among the gargoyle spires.

The figure said nothing at first. Then, very softly, he spoke.

“Two done.”

He tilted his head toward the ballroom below.

“One to go.”

He watched the Queen from above, Mirelle’s silhouette radiant in the light. She smiled easily, unaware that a gaze held her with the weight of obsession. The shadow’s fingers hovered over his hip, twitching slightly, like they ached to touch her, or end her.

“No one can see it coming.”

He chuckled to himself, then whispered again, though no one answered.

Looking back on the floor, he saw Drake’s steps slow as he caught a moment at the high table.
The King had leaned toward Mirelle again, brushing a stray curl behind her ear. She caught his hand before it could fall away. Their fingers wove together.

This brought a smile to the figure's face. “She does have a lovely smile.”

He continued to watch them. Not with envy. Not with admiration. Just watched. That intimacy… was real, he thought.

He turned away.

On the floor below, Drake recoiled from the smell of stormfruit glaze on a passing servant's tray. 

He moved through the crowd like he was the only one who could see the cage. He saw them all as wild animals in a menagerie. In this crowd, an assassin waited. Somewhere in this theatre of power and opulence, the third victim's breath still filled their lungs.

The applause in the ballroom thundered yet fell on the deaf ears of Meridian Drake. He stood apart, his back to a marble pillar beneath the high banners of Aldoria, watching a sea of light and silk blur around the king and queen. Laughter chimed from clustered nobles. Toasts rang out in a dozen dialects. Plates gleamed. Wine spilled like ritual blood.

He was inside the heart of the Accord, surrounded by celebration, and he had never felt more alone.

A steward passed close, murmuring something in polite tones. Drake did not respond. He hadn’t heard the words. His hand was inside his coat again, fingers closed around the lacquered hilt of the dagger. Whisperfang. The blade that promised peace by way of betrayal.

He had counted the days. Nine since the warning. Eight since the second note arrived. Two since his knees buckled in the dark of his chamber, alone with the weight of their lives on his shoulders. And now, the ninth night.

His wife. His son. His daughter. His sister-in-law. All still alive, for now. He had received no message. No proof of life since the lock of his daughter’s hair was delivered in silence. No assurance, only threat.

‘Kill the king before the tenth night ends. Or we’ll cleanse what remains.’

The blade felt warmer now. Was that its nature, or just the heat of his guilt, trapped under layers of fine cloth?

He watched Queen Mirelle touch King Edric’s arm, and the gesture, so simple, slashed at him harder than any blade.

They looked at each other like they still remembered who they were before power. Not puppets. Not icons. Just people. A shared smile. A private joke. She leaned into him as he whispered something. They were lovers still.

Gods, forgive him. He couldn’t even remember the last time Karethiel looked at him that way.

His stomach clenched. ‘If I do it, I become the butcher of kings. If I don’t, I become the executioner of my own children.’

Drake pressed his palm flat against the wall beside him. The stone was cool. Anchoring. He swallowed hard and tried to push the noise away.

Someone bumped into him, a young noble, drunk on power or wine. The man gave a lazy apology and moved on.

He didn’t even recognize Drake for who he was.

Good.

The music changed, a softer melody. A slow-dance lull meant to evoke a sentimental mood in the room.

King Edric offered his hand to Queen Mirelle. She took it with a graceful smile, and together they stepped onto the center floor. The new song was the beat of a slow waltz, one meant for dancing with a partner. Their movements were quiet, precise, unrehearsed, but deeply familiar.

They danced not for performance, but for each other.

A page stepped up near the musicians and called out, his voice clear and youthful. “By His Majesty’s kindness, those who wish to join the king and queen on the floor, you are most welcome.”

That single invitation brought warmth to many faces. To be invited, by the Crown, into their dance, it was more than an honor. It was a memory made real. Dozens of couples moved forward. Old soldiers with stiff knees. Young nobles with eager steps. Foreign delegates were curious to participate. For a moment, the hall truly felt like a dream given breath.

But not for Drake.

He looked up toward the balcony overlooking the courtyard. Torches shimmered against the stained-glass windows. Past that, the tower spire rose like a blade in moonlight.

Harmon’s team had to be out there. Somewhere. Moving in silence. He prayed they were cutting toward the chamber… Perhaps they’ll find the cell. Maybe they’ll stop this before I…

He couldn’t finish the thought.

Drake shifted his weight and leaned harder into the pillar. His vision blurred. ‘Let them save her. Let them save them all. Please, God. If you're watching, free me from this burden. I don't want to wear this crown of blood. But I will. If that is what it takes, I will.’ The thought died as a hush swept the upper tier.

A presence moved above him, silent and cold. A glass clinked behind him, but it wasn’t that. It was something colder. Something watching. He didn’t look. He already knew.

Eyes like knives. A weight that didn’t belong. He moved toward the edge of the ballroom, every motion controlled, practiced. He nodded to a few dignitaries as he passed, smiling without meaning it, just another governor making his rounds.

The shadows near the arch were empty when he glanced back.

The dagger felt heavier now, pulling at him like a chain. He needed space. Distance. Anything but another step in that hall.

One more night. That’s all, just one more.

If Harmon’s team succeeded, none of this would matter. The blade would be returned in the box. His hands would be clean. His children would still have a father. He would still be a husband.

He knew they would not hold up their promise, or everyone would have seen his actions. No, he was already dead, whether by their blade or his own guilt. All that remained was which ghost he’d become.

His head shook in resolve. He would trade his soul for theirs. He would die in silence, hated, scorned, and they would live. That would be enough. But not tonight.

Tonight, he was still a man walking the line. Governor Meridian Drake slipped through the archway and disappeared into the dark. Behind him, laughter bloomed like flowers across the ballroom, and no one noticed the world held its breath.
