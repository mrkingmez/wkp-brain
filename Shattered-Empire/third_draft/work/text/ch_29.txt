The frost bit deeper up here. The horse’s hooves crunched in rhythm, slow and precise, as Taerel guided it up the winding trail. No birdsong. No steady wind. Just the heavy silence of the upper ridges, where snow lay thick, and breath vanished the second it left your lungs.

A scarf was pulled high across the lower half of his face, wound tightly beneath his hood. His eyes, sharp, calculating, were the only part of him visible. The mask beneath the scarf was thin, magical, unseen to the naked eye. It filtered the cold, kept his breath from fogging the scarf, and hid the faint shimmer of power pulsing across his cheekbones. From a distance, he was just another cloaked shadow in the snow.

But shadows didn’t ride like this.

He’d been on the move since before dawn. His horse, trained for silence and strength over speed, moved as if it understood urgency. They both did. The siege at the pass wasn’t breaking anytime soon. The civilian quarter, boxed in behind the fortress walls, was running out of everything: bandages, tonics, fire-starters, food. Luca’s team was working to find a back-door tunnel into the siege line, but without supplies, it would be a death sentence for the people inside.

And that meant this ride wasn’t just a scouting run. It was a lifeline. If I don’t bring something back... they bleed out. They starve. The tunnel would mean nothing.

He clenched the reins tighter.

This wasn’t a mission for glory. It wasn’t for a mark on a report or some command chain recognition. Not for glory. Not for orders. For the particular people behind those particular walls, whose names he did not know but whose odds he had already run, and found wanting. 

Death, he could calculate. Failure to the people waiting inside those walls was a different kind of loss entirely, one that didn't sit well with him. 

The trail knifed through a natural cut in the ridge, tight, uneven stone slicked with hard-packed snow. Taerel dismounted without a sound, looping the reins around his wrist. His breath misted faintly against the inside of the scarf, but his body stayed still, honed and practiced.

The horse hesitated as the walls narrowed. Good instincts. Taerel stepped forward and tested the ground. Loose shale shifted underfoot.

Too late. His boot slid. He caught himself, slamming a gloved hand against the cliff wall, but the twist came sharp and fast, a brutal snap of tension in the same damn ankle he’d hurt a few nights ago at the other orc camp.

The ankle screamed, and for a heartbeat, the ridge tilted. He planted anyway, then eased the foot down with care, letting bone and tendon settle into a lie that would hold for a few more minutes. It would not hold long. So he kept everything deliberate. Small steps. No leaps. No wasted turns.

He dropped low, gasping once, sharply, through clenched teeth, and crouched, already stripping the wrap from a side pouch. Snow dug into his knees as he bound the joint. Tight. Secure. Pain flickered through him with every movement.

“Bad timing,” he muttered through clenched teeth. “Not a sign.”  A faint crack echoed somewhere deeper in the ridge, sharp enough to snap him alert again.

The horse nudged his shoulder lightly, as if sensing the shift.

“Still on track,” he said softly, more to himself than the horse. And they moved again. He reached the ridge’s edge just past the sun directly above.

Crouching low, he peered over the granite rise, letting the cold seep into his thighs. Far below, half-shrouded by frost-laced evergreens, a bandit caravan crept along the lower slope. Seven riders. Two packhorses. They looked like merchant horses: saddlebags and bundled rope satchels swaying with the gait of their mounts. One man laughed, the sound echoing up the stone like it didn’t belong.

They moved without discipline, too relaxed. But not green. Not anymore. They were returning from something, not heading out. Their gear sagged under weight, not anticipation.
One of the riders glanced uphill, frowning at the empty ridge as if something in the silence bothered him.

He took in the layout like a surgeon preparing for a cut, timing the rotation, noting every gap in their awareness. Six-second coverage blind spot on their left. No rear scout on the high ridge. No vanguard watching upward. Their biggest mistake?

They thought the snowfall would protect them.

He pulled back, eyes scanning the slope ahead. If he moved fast, he could intercept at the next switchback near the creek. “Supplies are the priority,” he told himself, and still his hand did not loosen on the rifle. Seven men meant seven mouths and seven blades, and every one of them could strip the packs and vanish into the white. If he let them pass, there would be nothing left. This could be the only shot to get back quickly. The people were what drove him.  So the switchback became his only choice.

He reached the switchback just ahead of them.

The slope dropped in a shallow curve to the left, funneled by frozen brush and a line of broken stones. Water trickled faintly beneath a thin sheet of ice, the only sound beyond the creak of his boots in the snow. This was the place. No escape routes. No line of sight for half a mile. If he moved quickly, with enough noise, he could force them into a moment of confusion. And a moment was all he needed.

He moved down and across as a cold gust knifed across the rocks, sharp enough to raise the hairs on his neck. He had picked the exact spot to set up his trap, a rounded rock near the trail’s edge, half buried in frost. He slid a wedge beneath it, then coiled the twine. It should make enough noise for them to check out.

He dropped low again, retreating to cover with practiced speed. Less than a minute passed before he heard it, the hollow clatter of stone against ice, the quick exhale of startled breath. Someone shouted. Two of them dismounted, footsteps crunching toward the slope just below him.

One carried a short sword. The other had a handaxe and no helmet. Both looked annoyed, not alert. Good.

Taerel struck from the ridge.

The rifle coughed once, and the wind stole the sound and carried it sideways along the rock face, away from the trail. Up here, the storm did strange things to noise. A shout could die at your boots, and a whisper could travel a mile. He did not wait to learn which it would be today. The first man dropped without a sound, legs twitching in the frost. The other turned too slowly.

Taerel was already on him.

Steel met steel, his rapier finding the man’s side just above the hip. The bandit stumbled, grunting in pain, and brought the axe around in a wild arc. Taerel ducked low, swept behind him, and drove the hilt into the base of his skull.

The body hit the ground hard and didn’t rise.

Taerel didn’t wait. He cut the reins from the nearest packhorse and yanked the heavy saddle bags free, flipping the flaps as he moved.

The first bag sat heavy and dense, nothing shifting, nothing hollow. The weight of things packed to last. He kept it. 

The second was a treasure trove, jerky and tonics, bandages and sealed vials, all of it packed tight as someone had planned for misery. He slung it beside his own without stopping to count. The third was more of the same, dried fruit and field oil, bundles of cloth wrapped clean. He did not need names for it. He knew what it meant.

One more pouch, thicker, heavier, looked like firestarter bricks. He pocketed it. Voices rose above him, sharper now. No questions. Orders. Someone had found the bodies.

He grabbed the last bag, slung it onto his horse’s harness, and swung into the saddle.

It came from the ridge above, one of the bandits, faster than the rest, crouched behind a tree stump, crossbow already cocked again. The bolt missed his heart, but not by much. It tore through the side of his coat and across his upper arm, ripping a red line across his shoulder and collarbone. His vision flashed white.

He gasped, half from pain, half from shock, and kicked the horse into motion.

The animal bolted forward, hooves digging deep into the slope, scattering snow behind them. Another bolt snapped past his head. A third thunked harmlessly into a tree behind.

He didn’t fire back. No time. No clean shot. Just move.

Blood soaked into the inside of his coat. Warmth fled fast, leaving only the dull pull of it. He forced his breath into a narrow rhythm and kept moving. Every jolt of the saddle drove pain through his ankle, which was now nearly locked in place with swelling. 

The ridgeline blurred past him in streaks of gray and white. He ducked low, one arm tight against his chest, reins pulled one-handed as they veered through the narrow pass. The trees leaned too close. He clipped one with his knee. Didn’t matter.

Behind him, he heard the bandits shouting. No pursuit. No horses on that slope, and the ground was a treacherous tilt of ice and stone that punished haste. They shouted instead, because shouting cost nothing.

He risked one glance over his shoulder. Only the wind chased him, hissing through the rocks.

The ridge broke open to the west.

Taerel's horse stumbled over the rise, foam streaking its neck, steam pouring from its muzzle like smoke from a battlefield. The trail curved beneath him, half-visible through the blur of wind and blood in his eyes. His shoulder burned, hot and bright, and every heartbeat echoed in his temple like a drumbeat warning of collapse.

Just ahead, the treeline split along the overlook. A shelf of dark stone jutted out into the open air, offering a panoramic view of the valley below.

And the orc camp.

Fires flickered in pits dug into the snow, tucked beneath a bend in the ridge where wind and sound both broke. The camp sat low, eyes on the valley, not the heights. They did not look up. They never had. As far as they knew, the ridge was empty. Dead land. That’s what the team had counted on.

A figure stood on the edge of the overlook, cloaked and steady, his rifle leveled across the valley. The scope glinted for a half-second, catching the faintest light.

Then the figure moved.

Fast. Efficient. The rifle turned, pivoting toward the sudden sound of hooves behind him, toward Taerel. Toward the shape slumped low in the saddle, blood soaking his right side, eyes barely open.

The man didn’t fire.

The rifle dropped. Not lowered, dropped. The muzzle fell with a sharp jerk, the weight of realization moving faster than instinct.

“Taerel?” The name came out strained, half question, half prayer.

The lookout sprinted forward, boots hammering frozen dirt, scarf pulled down as he closed the distance. Same dark hair. Same build. A mirror grown under different skies.

His brother.

“Whoa, whoa, hey, hey,” he said quickly, both hands catching the reins before the horse could pitch sideways on the slope. “I’ve got you. You’re alright. I’ve got you.”

Taerel didn’t answer. Couldn’t. His lips were cracked, and his breath was shallow. He was half-folded over the horn of the saddle, one hand still gripping the closest saddlebag like it might disappear if he let go.

Aymir pulled him upright as gently as he could. Blood stained his gloves. “By God’s bread, you’re hit. What happened… No, don’t talk. Just hold on.” He turned the horse carefully and started leading it, step by agonizing step, toward the hiding spot. He took it slow, down the ridge path toward the new campsite, tucked beneath a curtain of snow-laden evergreens. Hidden. Safe. For now.

Every few paces, Aymir glanced back at his brother to make sure he was still upright. “You stubborn bastard,” he muttered. “You always do this.” A sharp breath caught in his throat as he steadied Taerel’s slipping weight.

Taerel made a sound, half a cough, half a laugh. It hurt. But it was real. Aymir looked up sharply. “Don’t. Don’t do that,” he said tightly. “Don’t be clever right now. Just stay awake. You got the supplies?”

Taerel nodded once, slow and stiff. “They’re going to lose their minds,” Aymir said quietly. “You pulled it off.”

Another glance down the slope. Just ahead now. He whistled once, a low-pitched signal. Footsteps crunched below. Someone called out. Aymir guided the horse the final stretch, then reached up and caught Taerel under the arm as he slipped from the saddle at last, more collapse than dismount. He caught him and hauled him close.

Taerel’s head hung forward, one arm around Aymir’s shoulder, his breathing ragged.

“I got what I could,” Taerel whispered, the words rough.

“I know,” Aymir said, his voice cracking. “I know.”

Behind them, the fire was already being stoked. A lean-to was cleared. Gloves thrown aside, salves and water drawn. Aymir lowered him slowly to a roll of blankets, gloves already smeared with half-frozen blood, and pressed a hand to his neck. Pulse still strong. Still there. 

Taerel’s packs hit the ground. The gear was dirty, bloodstained, and half-frozen, but when Aymir opened the side flap of one of them, the first thing he saw was a bundle of wrapped cloth and tightly sealed vials. The vials were wrapped in cloth and tucked into a hard leather sleeve, packed like someone expected the world to try to break them.

Aymir looked up. “It’s good,” he said. “It’s a blessing.”

Taerel sat back, finally, eyes closed for a heartbeat. For the first time since the snow began falling, the tension in the camp broke, not fully, not loudly, but just enough for them to breathe.

Taerel, half-conscious, finally letting the weight go. He didn’t speak again. He didn’t need to. The ridge held. The fire burned.
