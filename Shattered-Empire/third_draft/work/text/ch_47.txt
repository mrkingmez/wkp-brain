The stairwell to the twenty-eighth floor was narrow and sweltering, carved into the side of the tower like a last-minute afterthought by architects who clearly never intended for anyone to use it during a siege. The stone steps were slick with condensation, the air thick with the sharp bite of burned oil, old blood, and the bitter aftertaste of wards that had long since discharged. Shadows clawed the walls from their torch crystals, all flickering blue and orange as the group ascended.

Ash was the first to collapse at the landing.

“Two hundred and thirty-eight,” he groaned, flopping back against the curved wall, one hand bracing his side. “I counted. Twice. That’s more stairs than I’ve climbed in my entire life.”

Dain slumped beside him, panting, his tunic damp with sweat. “You only counted twice because you lost track halfway.”

“Still counts,” Ash muttered. “My lungs are writing their last rites.”

Brother Aldrek stood near the stairwell door, upright and silent, his mace resting against his shoulder. Though his breath was steady, a thin gash still leaked down the side of his face, dried blood painting a dark streak beneath one eye. His robes were torn along one arm, scorched near the hem. He made no complaint.

Nyssa hadn’t sat. She stood near the stairwell door, eyes narrowed, adjusting the strap on her vambrace in small, precise motions. She hadn’t spoken in the last fifty steps, but the tension in her shoulders said plenty.

A distant groan rolled through the stone.

Not a creak. Not settling. A low, drawn-out moan from deep within the tower’s bones, like the echo of something ancient shifting in its sleep. The sound rattled behind the eyes, deep in the skull.

Ash looked up slowly. “What in the gods’ hollow teeth was that?”

“The tower,” Aldrek said. “It hurts.”

Dain ran a hand down his face. “Let’s get it over with.”

Ash pushed off the wall with a grunt. Aldrek pulled the door open.

The twenty-eighth floor opened like a preserved memory. Twin doors loomed ahead, tall enough to shame giants, each slab engraved with deep-relief carvings that glinted softly beneath the amber light. Long-bearded kings stood locked in eternal vigil across their surface, hands clasped over rune-etched axes, while stylized beasts curled and twisted around their feet, basilisks, thunder wolves, deep wyrms, all icons of dwarven legend. Every figure had been carved by hand, worn smooth in places by reverent touch or the slow march of centuries.
Dain pressed his shoulder against the left door. The hinges resisted with a groan like slumbering stone, then gave way. A rush of colder air whispered past them as the door yawned open.
They stepped into silence.
The throne room was cavernous, more a sanctum than a room, carved from reverence and memory. Its vaulted ceiling soared above, ribbed in dark granite and inset with crystal panels that caught and fractured the low light into a thousand shimmering fragments. The effect was like standing beneath a sky of slow-turning stars.
Pillars marched down the chamber’s sides, each massive and ornately cut, bearing the etched names of clans long vanished or exiled. Most were unreadable now, their lines dulled with time, but here and there a sigil still caught the light: crossed pickaxes, rising anvils, the coiled rune-drake of the Hollow Spine.
Heavy banners hung between the columns, some moth-eaten and torn, others miraculously intact. Deep crimsons, faded indigos, the colors of old dwarven bloodlines. Threads of gold still clung to a few, defiant in the face of dust and disuse.
“It’s not just abandoned,” Nyssa murmured, voice low. “It’s preserved.”
Aldrek nodded once. “This place wasn’t forgotten. It was kept, still used, sparingly. The governors hold ceremonies. The chair still matters.”
And there it was.
At the far end of the hall, under the tallest arch, the throne waited. A slab of blackstone, unadorned except for the intricate mithril veins that wrapped its base like living silver. Crimson velvet lined the seat, not newly sewn but clearly maintained, as if someone still came here from time to time just to clean, just to remember.
It was not the throne of a king born to rule. It was the seat of one who had taken power and held it against all things. Solid. Unyielding. Rooted.
Ash tilted his head as he took it in. “Big chair. Bet the view was great.”
“Depends on what you were willing to ignore,” Aldrek muttered, stepping forward.
They moved slowly, drawn deeper into the hush. Even their breaths seemed subdued now, caught under the weight of a thousand oaths and accusations that still clung to the stone.
Dain let his fingers brush one of the banners as they passed, and the fabric crumbled slightly at the edge. He pulled back quickly, muttering an apology, though no one answered.
Dain didn't move immediately. His eyes went to the pillar inscriptions, the clan names in stone, the crossed tools and coiled drakes of houses that no longer existed except in this room. There was a kind of dignity in being remembered in stone even when you were gone. He wondered what his own house would leave behind. Whether the name Dain had spent his whole life protecting would merit anything as permanent as a sigil carved into good granite.
He caught Ash watching him. "We're moving," he said and walked on.
Beyond the throne, side corridors led into the Thane’s private quarters. Alcoves stood behind thick velvet curtains, dusty now, their gold trim dulled. Ash peeked into one and found an old war map still mounted on the wall, its pins rusted, the lines drawn in faded ink. Another held a bedframe of solid ironwood, stripped bare but unbroken. A ceremonial forge sat cold in the corner, its bellows sealed with wax.
“These rooms... so well preserved,” Nyssa observed. “They were left. Like a tomb someone still visits.”
“Because they do,” Aldrek said. “In times of great need, the governor retreats here. The last three used this floor during major crises. One even signed the Stormhaven Armistice from that throne.”
“And now?” Dain asked, stepping out of a bedchamber whose walls were lined with ancient dwarven poetry. “It is empty.”
“Yes, mostly memories,” Aldrek said.
They fell into silence again. Not one of dread, but of respect. Even Ash didn’t break it.
It took time to find the stairwell. Unlike the narrow shaft they'd climbed to reach the floor below, this one was tucked behind a slatted bronze gate in the southeast wing, half-obscured by a collapsed tapestry. It was smaller, almost hidden by design, meant for dignitaries or nobles wishing to pass unseen between the royal and servant levels.
Dain reached for the latch. “Let’s hope the next floor’s quieter.”
Ash smirked. “You just jinxed it.”
The latch clanged open, and they moved into darkness once more.
The stairs to the next floor wound tightly, but had room to walk two by two. They climbed in silence. Gone were the marble panels and golden sconces.
The twenty-ninth floor bore none of the majesty of the Thane’s hall below. Here, the tower narrowed into function, shaped by calloused hands and sleepless necessity. Stone replaced luxury, bareness, grayness, and utilitarianism. The walls closed in. The ceiling hung lower. Iron sconces jutted from support beams, many of their lamps long extinguished. The few still lit cast a wan, cold-blue shimmer that flickered like breath on the edge of dying coals.
It was warmer here, but not comfortably so, stifling, thick with the scent of old oil and sealed dust. As they stepped from the stairwell landing, the air shifted again. Beneath the stale heat clung something else: dried herbs, faint soapstone, lavender, a ghost of domesticity.
“Something’s wrong,” Aldrek muttered, low.
He was right.
The corridor stretched ahead, uneven in pitch, floorboards creaking in sections where stone gave way to patched slats. Doorways lined both sides, rows of servant quarters, most left open like mouths mid-sentence. Each room was small, just large enough for a narrow bedframe, a basin, and a wall shelf. Most shelves were bare. Some beds remained made, others half-unraveled, blankets turned down as if someone had only stepped away for a moment.
But no one was here.
No bodies. No blood. No clear signs of struggle. Just… stillness. Like the occupants had vanished between one breath and the next.
Ash stepped into a room with a small pair of boots neatly aligned at the threshold. He bent slowly, staring at them.
"Someone left in a hurry, but not in panic," he murmured. “These aren’t tossed. They’re… placed.” He touched the heel lightly, showing no dust. “Too clean. Like someone wanted us to see them.”
Nyssa swept a slow arc with her gaze, moving past the ajar doors, her voice low. “It feels like they were taken all at once. Like the whole floor exhaled and everyone went with it.”
“Or turned to vapor,” Ash said, forcing a smirk. It didn’t land.
They moved deeper. The silence clung heavily here. Not like a quiet room, but like the air was pressing against them, listening.
The corridor opened into a wider chamber halfway down the hall. Not a throne room, not a gathering space, just a storage concourse ringed with benches and half-empty racks. And in the center, crates. Three of them.
Ash slowed as they stepped forward. The crates were unmissable, each the size of a hay wagon, dark-wood slats reinforced with black-iron banding, bolted directly to the floor. A canvas tarp half-pulled away revealed cushioned compartments within, nestled recesses shaped for cargo they could only guess at.
Nyssa’s brow creased. “Why store something like that here?”
Aldrek’s hand brushed the side of one crate. “Not stored. Secured.”
“They’re recent,” Dain said, crouching near the base. “No dust buildup along the latches. No signs of rot.”
Ash circled one slowly, laying his palm along the wood. “They’re too big to have come up the stairs. Even if you broke them down, there’s no way they fit through that last hall.”
“No pulley rigs either,” Nyssa added, scanning the walls. “No recessed platforms. No loading slots. Just stone and pipe.”
“Then they were brought here another way,” Aldrek said, finally. “A different lift. Hidden. Something meant for the tower’s upper floors.”
Ash looked to the ceiling, then back toward the wall, eyes narrowing. “That, or they came from above?”
Nyssa glanced at the nearest door. “We don’t have time to solve this.”
“Still,” Ash said, reluctant. “Feels like we just walked through someone’s secret.” He'd touched the arcane shard briefly when they were close to the crates, half expecting something, a heat signature, a residual ward, anything a trained hand could read. What he got was the sensation of reaching into water and finding the water had been replaced with something that only looked like water.
"Someone's secret," he said again. He kept moving. He kept the part about the shard to himself.
A bell reached them through the stone. After hearing that first bell, Dain started to count them. By the count of the third one, he walked faster. The count ended at five.
They pressed on, boots tapping lightly on the stone as they moved past the crates. The corridor narrowed again, turning at a slanted angle where the masonry changed. It took them longer than expected to find the next stairwell, tucked behind a false alcove door.
Nyssa found the latch first. It opened with a reluctant click, revealing another narrow flight spiraling upward into more shadow.
“Guard house, should be above,” Dain said, glancing up the stairwell. “Everyone ready?”
Ash cracked his knuckles and rolled his shoulder. “No. But we go anyway.”
They continued up the stairwell toward the Thirtieth floor. Behind them, the crates loomed in the shadows, silent, massive, unexplained. Ash cast one last glance back as the turn of the stairwell swallowed them.
The passage narrowed again, walls pressing in like the throat of something ancient and slow to wake. The air thickened, hot and metallic, the scent of scorched iron and dry stone rising with every step. Their boots echoed with a strange hollowness, as if the space ahead had already been emptied of sound.
Dain reached the landing first, pausing at the door. He tilted his head, listening.
Nothing.
He pushed it open.
The heavy door groaned like a wounded thing, revealing a chamber cloaked in stillness. The barracks level. But there were no voices. No movement. No breath.
Instead, there were piles.
Piles of gear. Not scattered or discarded, but arranged. Each one exactly where a man might’ve stood. Boots paired neatly. Greaves aligned over leggings. Chestplates stacked, weapons leaned gently against them. Even helmets rested atop, their visors shut as if watching the door.
Aldrek stepped through and halted. “Saints preserve…”
It looked like fifty men had simply vanished mid-march. As if they’d melted straight out of their armor, their equipment falling to the ground in perfect order.
Nyssa moved low and slow, blade drawn. “What the hell is this?”
Ash crouched beside one of the sets. He didn’t touch it. “These aren’t damaged. No signs of a fight. No scorch marks. No blood.”
“There’s no dust,” Dain added. “This didn’t happen days ago. This is recent.”
Brother Aldrek moved between the piles like a man in a graveyard. His voice dropped to a rasp. “Their gear’s warm. Not hours old. Minutes.”
Nyssa stiffened. “Warm?”
Aldrek nodded, jaw set. “Like it was just worn.” One boot in the nearest pile was still tied. A double knot, the kind a man who had learned the hard way tied every morning without thinking about it. The kind you did not stop to undo in a hurry, the kind you never expected to be the last time.
Aldrek looked at it for a moment. Then he moved on.
Ash exhaled, low and shaking. “I’m really hating this place.”
The Thirtieth floor had no guards. No bodies. Just the armor. Rows of empty armor.
They advanced carefully, past the first line, then the second. Every pile was identical. Every piece was precisely arranged. It wasn’t tossed, nor looted, nor aligned. As if the men had evaporated and something had set their gear down in their place. Ash muttered something under his breath that no one caught.
The far end of the barracks opened into a broad assembly hall, once meant for morning briefings. Here too, rows of armor. And in the center of the room, directly opposite the next stairwell…. A trail.
Small, wet footprints. Child-sized. Not smudged or scattered, centered. As if the child walked with purpose, undisturbed. Leading into one of the side rooms.
None of them spoke. None of them followed. “Why aren’t we following that?” asked Dain while pointing out the tracks. 
Aldrek answered, “Because some paths are meant to be walked only once.”
Dain held on to Aldrek's words for one beat, “Stairs. Go.”
The exit was across the chamber, an open stone archway with another door waiting beyond. Nyssa took point, stepping lightly through the eerie silence. Ash followed, his eyes everywhere but his feet. Aldrek gripped his mace tighter, eyes flitting to the rafters. Dain brought up the rear.
The stairwell swallowed them one by one, it curving tighter as the walls shifted from bare stone to the soft gold hues of noble stonework, leaning to the Thirty-First Floor. Velvet-lined sconces gave off a warm, amber glow, casting rich shadows that danced with returning grace. The scent changed as they got closer to the Thirty-First floor, no longer stone and sweat, but polished cedar, lilac oil, and the faintest whiff of ink and parchment.
Dain reached the landing and paused, eyes sweeping across the hall. It was pristine.
“Feels like stepping into someone else’s life,” Ash murmured.
“Someone very rich,” Nyssa added, eyes flitting to the gilded trim and marble busts.
Carpets padded the floors, thick and embroidered with Aldorian royal weave. Arched ceilings gave way to grand chandeliers of crystal and brass, dimmed but clean. Doors stood evenly spaced along the corridor, each etched with silver calligraphy and inlaid with house crests.
Ash exhaled slowly, eyes roving. "And not a damn speck of dust. Either ghosts clean in their spare time or someone’s been keeping this up."
Aldrek stepped in behind him, expression unreadable. "The king’s family used these chambers during their stays. You can feel the legacy in the walls. The Governor uses it for the same as well as special guests. This tower is still active, not only as a widow to the past but as a fort for the city in times of war. People are here every day to maintain these floors. But, I can see why you would think this would just be an old house."
Nyssa's fist went up, and the room stopped. One beat. Then two fingers forward, a command in a language older than words. She was already past them before they'd fully processed it. 
They moved in silence, blades drawn, shadows to shadows. Down the main corridor, they passed four rooms, empty but immaculate. The fifth, a lounge of sorts, had light leaking through the seams of the closed door.
Nyssa peered in.
Two guards.
Not Red Cloaks. Regular palace men, young, tired-looking, their leather armor worn smooth from years of use rather than battle. They stood at the edge of the corridor, guarding the passage that led to the prisoners, those scheduled for execution within the hour.
One sat slumped against the wall, lazily eating from a tin bowl, steam curling into the still air. The other leaned near a shuttered window, stretching his arms and rotating his neck with the sluggishness of a man who hadn’t seen real combat in months.
Nyssa watched them from the shadows, her breath low and even.
She gave the signal.
Dain veered left, silent as dusk. Aldrek ghosted behind Nyssa, mace in hand. Ash melted to the far wall, eyes sharp.
It was over in less than ten seconds.
Dain took the first without a sound, hand over mouth, blade finding the place between the ribs that ends things quickly. Aldrek took the second the way a man drives a nail, one motion, no hesitation, the mace landing where it needed to and not where it looked impressive. 
Dain wiped his blade on the man’s tunic. “They weren’t ready. Just men doing a job.”
“Wrong job,” Nyssa said under her breath. “Let’s move.”
At the corridor’s end, a heavy arch framed another stairwell, this one wider and far older, its stone steps worn smooth by centuries of use.
Nyssa ascended first, slow and silent. The wood-and-iron banister creaked beneath her glove, a rhythm like a taut bowstring. She reached the top, crouched low, and peeked just enough to see the floor beyond.
The Thirty-Second Floor was long; at the end of the hall, it opened into a ceremonial chamber.
Grand. Circular. Vaulted high with marble ribs that caught the light like skeletal wings. Once-beautiful wall hangings were gone, only dust outlines and empty brackets remained. Sconces burned low. No sound. No motion.
And yet, at the room’s heart, stood a long, stone table, carved from dark granite and chained to the ground by all four legs. Thick links. Tarnished iron. Built to anchor decisions, or prisoners. Spaced out around the room were doors, used by diplomats upon their visit to their quarters for their stay.
Three throne-like chairs stood along the far end.
And seated in the center…
A woman.
She wore layered robes of deep burgundy and gold, woven with heavy silk and trimmed in pale white fur. Her sash bore the emblem of the Governor’s line, two mirrored stags beneath a radiant sunburst. Her collar stood tall behind her head, a symbol of courtly station. Her fingers rested delicately in her lap, her chin lifted just slightly, her posture poised in perfect dignity.
Still. Unmoving. Regal. But something in her stillness was wrong. Nyssa didn’t blink. Didn’t breathe. Then she slipped back down the stairs.
“She’s there,” she said softly. “The governor’s sister-in-law. I saw her once, “About a year back. Riven and I were running a ring-swap con. She was playing hostess for some charity banquet. She looks the same now as she did then. Same formal robes. The dress is right. Governor's seal, fur collar, the whole presentation. She looks like she belongs here." 
Ash’s fingers twitched, the faint glow already building beneath his skin.
Dain looked at Aldrek, who simply nodded once, face hard.
Aldrek’s voice was quiet, but resolute. “Then we go. One floor left.”
They went up without discussion.
The stairs curved tighter near the top, and the air changed before the floor did, cedar and old parchment and the specific stillness of a space that had been waiting. Dain registered it as he climbed. Not the absence of people. The presence of quiet. Those were different things, and this was the second one.
The 32nd floor was wide. Amber-lit. The kind of space built to make people feel the weight of what happened inside it. Marble columns lined the corridor, sconces burning at intervals, warm light falling across stonework that generations of ceremony had polished smooth.
Ash took in the ceiling first, the columns second, the shadows third. His eyes were doing the inventory his hands did in a fight: what was where, what could move, what already had.
Aldrek came up last and stopped at the landing for a half-second longer than the others. Something moved across his face that was not readable. Then he followed.
The corridor curved ahead. At its end, through a carved stone archway, candlelight warmed a wider space. They could see the edge of a chair. The edge of a woman's robe. Burgundy caught the light along its hem.
No one spoke. Nyssa had already done that work.
They moved forward in the particular silence of people who had agreed without saying so to be ready for whatever the next thirty seconds held. Dain's hand was at his blade. Ash's skin held a faint warmth at the palm that he was not fully releasing. Aldrek's lips moved once, private and brief.
The archway opened. They stepped through.
She was exactly where Nyssa had said. Seated. Still. The governor's seal at her breast. Her hands folded with the practiced dignity of a woman who had spent her life being looked at in rooms like this one. Her chin held the precise angle of composure under pressure.
The wrongness came a half-second later. Not from her. From the room itself, from the specific quality of the silence around her, the way the candlelight moved and nothing else did, the sense of a space that had been configured rather than inhabited.
Ash felt it in the shard before he named it.
Nobody moved for a count of three. Then Nyssa pulled back from the archway and led them down the corridor, past two more closed doors, into a chamber wider than the rest. The ceiling arched overhead, ribbed with white-painted beams and set with hanging crystal orbs. A long ceremonial table sat in the center, fixed directly to the floor with iron pins and bolted legs, made to hold peace treaties and royal guests, not to be moved.
But Nyssa barely glanced at the table. Her eyes were on the far alcove. “She was over there," she said, pointing.
A small receiving room adjoined the larger space, oval-shaped, draped in emerald green curtains. Candlelight glowed behind the silk folds. The team fanned out again, clearing the corners, checking each side room. Empty beds. Sitting nooks. Folded towels and clean water basins, untouched.
Aldrek tested one of the doors. “Still warm. This place was used recently.”
They returned to the main chamber and crept forward toward the room Nyssa had marked.
And there they were.
Five figures sat in silence behind a lattice divider. It shielded them partially, more for ceremony than security, but through the open space between panels, the team could make them out.
The woman in the center wore layered silks of burgundy and soft gray, cinched with a golden sash and high fur collar. Her shoulders were square, posture precise. A clasp bearing the seal of the governor’s house gleamed at her breast. Her hair was braided in the old noble style, dignified, formal.
Nyssa nodded. “That’s her. The Governor’s sister-in-law.”
Two children sat to her right. A boy and a girl, maybe eight and ten. Wide-eyed, still, clinging to each other but saying nothing.
Also with them was a woman in a fine dress, plainer than the others but still regal. The governor’s wife.
The fifth figure was a man. Silent. Broad. Wrapped in a servant’s cloak with his face turned away, crouched slightly in the corner. He didn’t speak. Didn’t move.
Aldrek lowered his mace. “We’ll clear the rest of the rooms. Quietly. Then we get them out.”
They spread out again, Dain and Nyssa sweeping left, Ash and Aldrek right. Nothing. No other guards. No Red Cloaks. Just stillness.
When they regrouped, Dain nodded. “Clear.”
Ash approached the family slowly. “It’s safe. We’re here to get you out.”
They moved quickly but with care, blades flashing only long enough to cut the bindings. Ash kept his head low as Nyssa led the way to the prisoners, her dagger working cleanly through each rope.
The children were first. They didn’t cry, didn’t speak. The boy looked down at his wrists as though unsure they were still his. The girl clutched the front of Nyssa’s coat without a word. The governor’s wife sat upright with perfect posture, trembling only in her fingertips. Beside her, the sister-in-law didn’t move at all, just stared into nothing with an expression caught between grace and terror. And then there was the fifth figure.
He was slumped against a pillar, face hooded beneath a thick cloak, posture oddly still. Broad across the shoulders. Easily mistaken for a guard or a man in shock.
Ash glanced at him, but Nyssa cut in front of him.
"Start on the others," she whispered. "I’ve got this one."
Ash moved without answering, crouching beside the governor's wife first, working the rope at her wrists while Nyssa moved to the fifth figure. Steel whispered through rope as bindings fell away. Ash felt sweat prick under his collar, not from effort, but unease. The silence in the room wasn’t normal. The walls seemed to breathe around them, stone holding its breath.
The girl clung to Nyssa’s leg as she rose. “It’s okay now,” Nyssa whispered. “We’re getting you out.”
Ash stepped back to the fifth figure. The others had been lifted. Guided to their feet. He remained motionless.
Too motionless. 
Ash crouched beside him. “Hey,” he whispered. “You alright?”
No response.
Ash stepped forward slowly, his breath shallow. The hooded figure sat at the edge of the group, slumped, heavy, oddly still. Something about the way it breathed, or didn’t, unsettled him. It was too still. Too deliberate.
He reached out, hesitant, fingers brushing the edge of the thick cloak.
It slipped off without resistance.
And what waited beneath wasn’t human.
The skin was stretched wrong. Pale, bloated, veined with purple threadwork and black suture lines. Its torso was too wide, patchwork muscles bound with strips of discolored flesh and old uniform cloth. One eye was stitched shut. The other stared, lidless and milky. Its chest heaved with no breath. Its arms, those were the worst. Thick as tree trunks, ending in hands the size of hammers, fingers swollen and twitching. It smelled like copper and rotted marrow.
Ash stumbled back, heat flaring in his palms. “Flesh golem.”
His voice was barely above a whisper, but it was enough.
The thing stood.
Nyssa turned toward the mother, already reaching for her shoulder when the woman began to weep, softly at first. Then her sobs turned into choking gasps. Black ichor streamed down from her eyes like ink bleeding through porcelain. She looked at Nyssa with terrible clarity.
The terrible part was that nothing in her face was wrong. Not her eyes. Not the set of her jaw. Not the exact quality of dignity that Nyssa had seen at the banquet a year ago and recognized on the stairs an hour ago. All of it exactly right. All of it empty.  
“I’m sorry,” she whispered. “But you shouldn’t have come.” She was out of the chair before the sentence finished, closing the distance in a way that did not account for how far the chair was from Nyssa. 
The child beside her moved in the same instant, her tiny mouth distending with impossible width, strands of viscous goo stretching between jagged, uneven teeth. Her eyes turned obsidian. The boy followed, grinning, mouth tearing wider as his jaw unhinged.
Dain shouted, blade already up. “It was a trap!”
The mimics came at them together, which meant there had never been a family here at all. The laughter came from the walls. Low. Unhurried. The kind of sound a thing makes when it has arranged something carefully and is watching the arrangement fulfill its purpose. None of the creatures in the room was making it. That was the worst part. 
Steel clashed against shrieking claws.
Nyssa didn't hesitate. That was what she told herself in the instant it took her hands to move. She didn't hesitate. The thing in front of her was not a child. It had been dressed as one. That was different. Her hands disagreed by about a quarter-second.
Then her dagger moved. Nyssa ducked, twisted, and drove a dagger into the side of the mimic-child’s neck. It shrieked, gurgled, and collapsed into black tar. “They’re not human!” she snapped. “These are some kind of mimic, nothing I have ever seen.”
Ash threw fire, but it sputtered; his shard burned the wrong hue. Weak. Mismatched.
“Dain! On the big one!” he yelled.
The flesh golem barreled forward, swinging an arm like a siege club. It smashed into a wooden table, turning it into wood chips and shrapnel. Aldrek raised his mace, the warding prayer on his lips, but the thing didn’t stop. It barely slowed.
Ash ducked behind a pillar and flung a narrow arc of fire. The golem’s shoulder caught flame, but it didn’t scream. It didn’t even notice.
“Too stupid to feel pain!” Ash growled. “Too strong to ignore!”
Dain charged the side, slashing deep across its ribs, but the golem backhanded him, sending him skidding across the floor. Nyssa leapt over a mimic, rolled, and slashed the leg of another, turning it to goo. She stabbed downward again and again until it stopped moving.
Aldrek moved to intercept, his mace glowing with holy fire. “In the Light’s name. Back! Beast!”
He struck the creature’s knee with a prayer-enhanced blow. The golem stumbled, dropped to one knee, then roared and slammed both fists into the floor. The impact cracked the stone beneath them.
Ash gritted his teeth and circled, looking for an opening. “Dain!”
“Still breathing!” Dain snarled, wiping blood from his lip. “We need to drop this thing fast!”
They rotated, Aldrek holding its attention with pulsing white light, Ash flanking, Nyssa clearing the last mimic off the wife, and Dain closing in with a flurry of rapid slashes that tore through the golem’s back. Still, it fought.
Ash finally planted both feet and shouted, “Move!”
The others scattered.
A column of fire erupted from his hands, washing over the golem’s upper half. Its flesh boiled and blistered, but even then, it stood.
Dain sprinted in, leapt onto its back, and drove his blade deep into the neck seam where several corpses had been stitched together.
The golem thrashed once, its head was severed halfway, then it hit the ground like a felled boulder. Ash went to one knee beside it, panting. “Dead. Tell me it’s dead.” Aldrek crouched over the creature briefly. “As dead as something like this gets.”
The room held its breath for two full seconds. Then the door they had come through buckled and buckled again. Then something hit it from the other side with enough force to crack the frame, and the sound it made was not human at all. Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. Dain's eyes went to the door, then to the stairwell above them, then to the door again. Two options. One of them led up toward what they had come for. One of them was currently deciding whether to come through the frame or through the wall.
Dain looked at the others. "Up," he said.
