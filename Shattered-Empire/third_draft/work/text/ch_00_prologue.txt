The clatter of dice and the soft scrape of cards on wood echoed faintly atop the wall. Three guards hunched over a battered little table, uniforms loose, helms pushed back, laughter kept low like children sneaking sweets. The pass yawned behind them, black and cold and entirely unobserved. 

The voice cut through their mirth and left nothing. “What in the blue hells are you three doing?”

The cards froze in mid-play. All three guards looked up sharply, their faces draining when they saw who stood before them. Sergeant of the Guard Corren Vey loomed in the torchlight, broad-shouldered, scar-lined, his expression carrying the particular stillness of a man who has decided what he is going to do before he says a word.

They scrambled to their feet, snapping to attention. The Sergeant’s steps boomed as he stalked closer, towering over the little table as though it were an altar of shame.

“I asked a question,” Vey barked, the kind of voice men learned to move their feet at before thinking.

“Sir,” stammered Alix Sentel, the youngest of the three, his hand trembling at his side. “We were just… playing cards to pass the time. These shifts get long, and we—”

“Just killing time,” Vey repeated, his voice suddenly calm, almost soft. That softness was worse than a shout.

The woman across from Alix, Ashlyh Shaed, swallowed hard and found her courage. “Yes, sir. Only that. No gambling, no harm. Nothing ever happens up here. Not in years. Some say there’s nothing even left out there.”

Vey’s head turned slowly until his eyes locked on her. “Shaed, is it?”

“Ashlyh Shaed, sir.”

“Well, Ashlyh,” he said, and without warning, he seized the flimsy table with one hand and hurled it over the battlements. It clattered down the stone face, vanishing into the black maw of the pass below. His voice roared after it. “It is not your job to think. It is not your place to listen to rumors. You are the watch on this wall, the eyes that keep the rest of us breathing! Do you understand?”

The three snapped in unison, “Yes, sir!”

Corren Vey strode to the edge of the wall, looking out across the frontier. The wind tugged at his cloak, carrying the scents of pine and frost and something else, faint, bitter, like ash. He had smelled that on the wind before. The last time, three men had died before morning.
“You think it’s quiet because the nightmares are gone?” His voice dropped to a low growl. “No. They’re patient. They wait. We used to know that. We used to take recruits out beyond the walls, make them see what we fought. Some never came back. But those who did… they were soldiers, forged by fire.”

He tugged off his helm, and the torchlight caught the long scar running from the corner of his left eye down to his jaw.

“You three will have wall duty for a week. Midday to midnight. Dinner and washroom breaks only. If I hear one whisper of idleness, I’ll throw you over myself. If I catch you again, I’ll hang you for dereliction of duty.”

“Yes, sir!”

Vey’s glare softened, only slightly. “You’re lucky it was me. The new lord arrives today, and from what I hear, he’s stricter still.”

That brought their heads up. Ashlyh blinked. “New lord, sir?”

Vey nodded. “Aye. The king cycles them every three years. Sir Jorah is returning to his keep. His replacement is on the road even now. Sir Evens Garkins of Woford.”

All three guards froze. Alix breathed the name like a prayer. “The Evens Garkins? The one who led the coastal defense, who crushed the eastern rebellion?”

“And some say,” Ashlyh added, her voice hushed, “that he’s a Ringbearer. That he’s to rebuild and take command of a new Brotherhood, to march against the great evils when they return.”

The third guard, silent until now, leaned forward. “Is it true, Sergeant? Will he lead us to reclaim the frontier? To rebuild the Shared Empire?”

Vey snorted. “Children’s tales. I don’t know if the rings are real, nor do you. He comes to hold this pass, to keep the lands of men safe. That is all.”

The third guard straightened. There was a strange light in his eyes. “The rings are real." He said it without heat, without investment, the way you say a thing you know and have known a long time. “No man chooses them. They choose their bearer.”

Vey’s brow furrowed. Something about the man’s tone scraped across his instincts like a whetstone. He stepped closer, squinting. “Who are you again? I thought I knew every man on this wall.”

Something in the space between his question and the answer told him more than the answer would. His body knew it first. His hand was already moving to his hilt when the guard finally smiled. It was wrong, too slow, too deliberate. With each step, he seemed to grow, his shadow stretching, the torchlight bending oddly around him.

The other two turned. Alix's hand went to his sword without touching it. Ashlyh said nothing. Her face said everything.

The false guard’s helm hit the stones with a hollow clang. His face melted pale, features smoothing into something inhuman. The nose was gone, the flesh too perfect, his eyes black and flat and lit from somewhere that wasn't light.

“You were right, Sergeant,” the thing said, its voice warped, too calm. “We are the nightmares. And tonight we become yours again.”

Alix staggered back, breath broken. “What are you?”

The creature smiled. “You can call me Dar. It was a good game, Alix. You too, Ashlyh. But all games end.” He drew a dagger, its blade catching no light. “We will find the rings. And Lucent bids me tell you something first.”

Vey’s sword rasped free. “Lucent?”

Dar’s grin widened. “Very good. His message is simple.”

An arrow hissed from the dark and buried itself in Alix’s throat. He collapsed, choking, blood pooling fast. A second shaft slammed into Ashlyh’s eye. She fell straight, the way bodies do.

Vey roared. His sword was in his hand. His feet found the stance before he told them to. “Come then!”

Dar blurred forward, faster than a striking viper. His dagger punched into Vey’s gut, the force lifting the Sergeant off his feet before he was slammed down beside the corpses of his guards.

Pinned, gasping, Vey spat blood and clutched weakly at the blade.

Dar bent low, his voice intimate, his breath cold. “The time of man is over.”

He wrenched the dagger sideways, then yanked it free, leaving Vey writhing. From the shadows, two green-skinned creatures scuttled forward, Goblins twisted by some darker hand, their eyes gleaming sickly yellow.

“Take positions,” Dar ordered. “Loose arrows until their screams drown the bells. I’ll open the gate.”

As Vey tried to drag himself upright, the screaming had already started below. He knew the voices. Some of them, he knew. Dar crouched beside him once more. “No, no. Stay alive, old soldier. Bleed, but not too much. You’ll live long enough to watch us gut your keep from within. You’ll hear your men die and know it was your failure that let it happen.”

Corren Vey lay on the stones, breath ragged, hot blood pooling under him. He reached for his sword, but his fingers were too slick to grip it.

Dar crouched low, pale face inches from his. The creature’s voice dropped to a whisper, each word cold as river ice.

“You’ll live, Sergeant. That’s your curse. You’ll wake with this wound, with the memory of your men’s screams, with their blood on your boots. And you will carry my master’s words to every corner of your kingdom.”

Vey snarled, but it came out wet, a bubbling growl.

Dar smiled wider, dagger still slick with his blood. “Lucent says the time of man is over. Remember that. Speak it. Spread it. Let them know their walls are dust and their lords are shadows clinging to dying light.”

He twisted the dagger once more, not to kill, but to sear the memory into nerve and bone, then ripped it free. Vey arched in agony, teeth gritted against the scream that tore from him anyway.

The Goblins cackled from the parapet as they loosed their first volley, arrows raining down into the courtyard. Shouts erupted, the clang of steel, the crack of alarm bells.

Dar stood and looked down at him one last time. “Bleed. Crawl. Live. That is your task tonight, Sergeant. A witness is worth more than a corpse.”

Then, like smoke, he slipped away toward the gatehouse, his shadow melting into the night.

Corren Vey clutched his side, the world swimming. Every breath was agony, but his eyes stayed open, fixed on the bodies of Alix and Ashlyh, on their faces slack in death. He forced the sight into his mind, knowing he would never escape it.

He wanted to close his eyes. To surrender. But the thought of Dar’s grin, of Lucent’s name, kept him awake. If his men’s lives were the price, then his duty now was to bear witness. He got his elbow under him. An inch. Then another. To crawl, to live, and to tell the tale of the night the nightmares returned.
