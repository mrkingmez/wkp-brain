The boat cut through the still waters of the Andralis River in the late morning, like a slow arrow, its hull creaking as oars dipped rhythmically on either side, nudging the vessel into the wide bay near the western docks of Rellisford. They had traveled about six hours upriver from Opindale. The sun sat warm and high above, casting a golden glow across the river's surface that shimmered like hammered bronze. The air smelled of water and weathered rope, mingled with the dry musk of cargo crates and the sharp tang of pickled fish baking under dockside tarps.
Weslin stood at the bow of the boat, boots planted wide on the damp, sun-warmed planks, the river wind tugging gently at the ends of his coat. The spray along the bow had gone brackish as they neared land, and the current slowed to a lazy pull, the kind that seemed to tug at thought as well as hull. His hands rested on the railing, calloused fingers curling around the wood while his eyes stayed fixed ahead, Rellisford. It wasn’t grand. It wasn’t regal. But it lived. This was the first place Weslin had been other than Opindale, so he studied it like a schoolboy. 
The town unfolded along the southern bend of the river like a patchwork cloak stitched from timber, stone, and sweat. It was layered and working, less a monument and more a machine. The docks came first, jutting out from the muddy bank like thick, gnarled fingers reaching to catch them. Heavy ropes swayed from tall pulleys, creaking where they connected to a towering iron winch mid-haul on a barge stacked with barrels of plum wine and bolts of sun-dyed fabric. Dockhands swarmed like ants, muscled and shirtless, shouting in coarse harmony as they hefted crates and barked orders. One man shoved a cart forward with his shoulder while another cursed the stubborn anchor chain they were trying to reset. The air was thick with brine, river mud, and the sharper tang of iron and tar. 
Above it all, gulls wheeled and screamed, and black river crows perched on the tops of mooring posts, heads twitching for any sign of spillage. One dove down and wrestled a half-eaten fig from a pile of straw, only to be chased off by a younger boy swinging a wet rope like a whip. 
Behind the chaos of the waterfront, stone-and-timber warehouses rose in rows, stacked two or even three stories high in places. The old sigils painted on their exteriors had faded with age: a red door, a coil of rope, a bundle of crowned wheat. Each one whispered of merchant families, of distant cities, of lineage and silent deals made in smoke-filled chambers. One warehouse sagged near its foundation. Weslin guessed that was Rustside, the name he'd heard muttered by some of the crew, a crumbling, neglected structure with mold licking up from its base and a visible lean to its frame. Even from here, he could catch the scent wafting out of its shaded maw: mildew, aged grain, and the ghosts of long-forgotten shipments.
But it was the town beyond the warehouses that truly caught him. His eyes followed the slope of the cobbled main road, where shops pressed close, their trade clear without signs: herbs drying in bundles, a forge glowing with copper pans, bright cloth rippling like prayer flags. A butcher argued with a woman in a headwrap, cleaver resting on a stump, while cages rattled nearby with restless poultry. Four inns stood along the bend like weary guardians, one spilling laughter from open windows, another gleaming with white pillars and polished glass.
Between it all, life churned. Though this place paled beside Opindale in size, the life here resonated with Weslin in a way the city rarely did.
Children raced through alleys with chalk-dusted hands and bare feet. A fox darted from the reeds along the riverside, startled by the slow grind of their boat’s hull scraping against the shallow bank. Dragonflies skimmed the water, luminous in the sunlight. A woman paused to sweep the bakery's stoop, her sleeves rolled and her cheeks flushed, while beside her a man adjusted a painted sign outside his narrow storefront, arguing with himself about whether it should face east or west.
The closer they got, the deeper the rhythm settled into Weslin’s chest. Rellisford breathed, not in the frantic pulse of a city but in the long, steady inhale of a town that knew its place in the world. It had its feet in the mud and its hands in the coin-pouch. Every shout, every clang of metal, every flutter of bird or banner felt like a note in some old song that only Rellisford remembered how to hum.
It was the scent that hit him next, bitter ale and smoke, sweet bread and tannins from the dyehouse he hadn’t even seen yet, layered over the wet rot of the dock edge and the coppery tang of freshly caught riverfish.
Weslin lingered a moment longer at the bow. Then, all at once, he turned and jogged toward the stern, weaving between crew, crates, and coiled rope.
At the stern of the boat, Harmon sat hunched over his journal, his writing hand steady despite the gentle roll of the current beneath him. His fingers were ink-stained, his brow furrowed in concentration. The page before him was nearly filled, thoughts, numbers, and half-finished plans all tangled together in his looping script. 
He paused as the dock came into clear view, the crunch and clang of the riverfront swelling in his ears. The journey had been long, six hours of drifting, rowing, planning, and silence, but as he capped his inkwell and closed the journal, a quiet satisfaction settled over him. The boat shifted slightly beneath him, the groan of wood on water marking the end of the ride. He slid the journal into his satchel and looked up.
Around him, the crew stirred with purpose. Someone was adjusting saddle gear, checking each buckle with methodical precision. Boots thudded lightly on the deck. Quiet banter flowed between a few who leaned lazily over the rail, watching the dockhands below work like ants in a hive.
The first ropes were thrown out. With practiced ease, the sailors moved into place, shouting terse commands and tying off the vessel to the thick, moss-covered posts of the dock.
Harmon stood, brushing off the front of his tunic. “All right,” he said, voice clear but not raised. “Let’s get the teams up. I want a word before we unload.”
“Yes, my Lord,” Weslin replied, already hurrying off.
He walked toward the center of the deck, gathering his thoughts as boots and orders echoed around him. Horses neighed from the rear cargo hold, eager for the river rocking to end. The scent of hay and sweat wafted up from below, mixing with the sun-warmed wood and distant aroma of roasted nuts drifting in from the market square.
It didn’t take long for Weslin to get everyone together, as most were close to stern, where Harmon wanted to meet. As they gathered around, Harmon took in everyone. In the light, they all looked a bit different from how they had on the water. He noted it. Or perhaps it is my eyes. 
“We are docked.” He let that settle. “Time to split up. Listen.” Harmon’s gaze moved like a tally. “Aurelia. Neavin. Weslin. You are with me.” Then, without pause, “Ash. Aldrek. Dain. Nyssa. Garr. Stormhaven. You get off here, Saddles first. No wandering.” He turned his chin toward Luca. “Lysana. Aymir. Taeral. Luca. The pass.” He called out. “Garr.” 
Garr turned to look at Harmon. “Here’s coin,” Harmon said, flicking the purse toward him. “Food, tack. Other needs. Be quick, we leave before the next bell.”
For a beat, nobody moved. Too many eyes, too many new streets, the dock noise pressing in. Harmon let the hesitation live just long enough to sour. Then his tone cut clean.
“Weslin.”
“Sir. Harmon pointed once, not looking away from the group. “Your pack. Here. Now.” Weslin was already moving.
“While he is gone, you four gather around.” The group drew a little tighter around Harmon. Harmon hands a purse to Luca now. “Here is the same thing for you. This is for you to get what you need when you stop at Barrowmead. Your mission is to head to the pass and report back to me on what you see and what is happening. If at all possible, try not to get discovered. But if you see something you can help with, you have full autonomy to take action. Please keep me updated so I can relay it back to the crown. Any questions?”
Aymir speaks up first, “So what are our rules of engagement?” Aymir asked. “Shadow work, yes. But if a patrol gets too close? Can we take them down?”
“If you cannot give me a clean report, you use judgment.” He let his eyes settle on each of them, one by one. “Remember this. A missing patrol becomes a story. Stories grow legs. Protect the mission. Protect each other.” A smaller pause. Less comfort in it. “You are not fools. Let's not start acting like you are. Questions?”
There was a long silence until Luca spoke up, “How are we to report back to you? We will be hundreds of miles away.”
Harmon just nodded and looked around the group to see if Weslin was on his way back or not. Hurrying up the way was Weslin with his whole pack on his back. Harmon gave a little smile at the effort this young man was giving. 
“Back, Sir Harmon,” Weslin said, breathless.
“As I see, hand me the pack, please.”
Weslin did so with no hesitation. “What are you looking for? I might be able to help you find it.”
Harmon didn’t answer Weslin. He just rummaged around in the pack until he found a small black bag. He handed Weslin his pack back. “Thank you. Just wait there.” He said, not looking at Weslin at all, opening the bag and retrieving the contents took up his complete attention. He removed a shiny silver bracelet and handed it to Luca, “This is how we communicate.” Turning and handing the second one to Weslin. “Put it out, both of you.”
Both Luca and Weslin did as they were ordered and placed the bracelet on their left wrist. Harmon waited until each bracelet was secured. He did not rush them. “Listen carefully,” he said, raising his wrist slightly. “This allows direct thought communication. It is not magic shouting. It is a focused transmission.”
His eyes settled on Luca. “Choose Weslin in your mind. Form the message. Send it. Do not speak.” Luca concentrated.
Weslin stiffened, eyes widening. “He says I need new clothes.” A flicker of restrained amusement crossed a few faces. Harmon did not indulge it. “Good. That confirms the connection.” His tone shifted into doctrine. “You do not broadcast. You select the mind. You send once. Weslin relays. That is the chain.”
His gaze hardened slightly. “Misuse it, and you create confusion. Use it correctly, and you remove distance. Understood?”
Luca just nodded his head. “Good, now go with honor,” Harmon said, stepping back from the group. “Weslin, go unload your horse from the boat and prepare to ride.”
“Yes, Sir” was the simple reply from Weslin. The docked boat groaned of old wood and iron as they made their way off, ropes slapping against waterlogged posts. Harmon and Weslin were the last to disembark, each leading a saddled horse down the narrow ramp as the rest of the team disappeared into the bustling streets ahead. By the time Weslin mounted his horse, Luca's team was already at the road's fork. Weslin caught a last glimpse of them turning north, Luca at the front of the boat, Lysana just behind him, and then the bend took them, and they were gone. Harmon was already riding east. 
 The air in Rellisford was thick with the scent of river silt, charcoal smoke, cured meat, and spiced bread. Merchants shouted over each other in the upper rows while children darted barefoot between barrels and crates. Harmon gave a short whistle to signal their exit, but no one looked back.
The two of them paused just long enough to adjust saddle bags and check the contents of a wrapped package Harmon had taken from the ship’s captain. “Weslin, put this somewhere safe. It is delicate.” Weslin grabbed the bundle and put it in a saddle bag. Being extra careful, he put his blanket around it to give it some more padding. 
As they mounted, Weslin glanced toward the stone archway that led deeper into the city. “This place smells like a forge, and a bakery had a brawl,” he muttered. Harmon smirked but said nothing, guiding his horse toward the stables just off the main square where they were supposed to meet the others. 
They moved through the press of carts and crates, the scent of brine giving way to horses and frying fish. Aurelia was not hard to find. She had positioned herself at the stable entrance where the lane opened onto the square, her horse quiet beside her, her pale violet eyes moving across the market in small, deliberate arcs. She had likely been aware of Harmon for the last two minutes. When Weslin spotted her, his face brightened. She gave him one calm look that took his measure without making a performance of it. She rode with graceful flow, her earthy-toned robes draped over her mount. He took notice of a Mindstone pendant that rested just below her throat. The Yellow light from it was dancing in the early morning sun.
"Aurelia," Harmon greeted. "Glad I caught up with you before we headed out."
"Really? What do you need, sir?"
He dismounted and walked closer to her. "I want you with me,” Harmon said. “Not just as a Yellow Adept. I need you handling communications.” 
Her eyes narrowed slightly. "How wide a range are we talking?"
"What can you manage?"
"Five miles is my clean range, direct link, surface thoughts, soft sends. I can push further... astral projection, if I’ve linked with the mind before. But that drains me, and it will drain a shard as well."
"We'll try to keep it under five then. But if we split teams, I’ll need you to bridge the distance."
She nodded slowly. "I can do that."
He motioned to Weslin, who was sitting in the saddle quietly behind him, eyes scanning the square. “This is Weslin. He’ll be your support. Whatever you need, shard prep, notes, meditation blocking, he’s yours to command.”
He paused, voice lowering just a bit. “And there’s something you need to know about him...”
Aurelia leaned in to hear.
“He’s a Yellow."
Aurelia blinked, caught off guard. Her eyes flicked over him, then softened. "You’re sure?"
"He was never tested," Harmon said. "Not a late bloomer. His dad let him slip through. I suspected something when we met, but it was confirmed at the feast." 
Weslin sat straighter now, hands clutching the reins. Harmon continued, voice low but steady.
“He held a shard, and the voices flooded in, dozens at once. Then his rival cornered him, a bully who wouldn’t let him pass. Weslin panicked, wanted him to burn, and the boy thought his skin was on fire. Screamed like it was real.”
Aurelia inhaled, visibly shaken. "That’s an instinctual projection. Raw talent. Dangerous without guidance."
"Exactly. I want you to train him. On the road, while we work. I know it’s not proper, but he can’t wait."
She looked to Weslin, who met her gaze with hope and just a trace of fear. He nodded earnestly.
"I won’t let you down," he said, almost whispering.
Aurelia gave a small smile, placing her hand gently over her pendant. "Then I won’t let you go untrained."
“Thank you, I will owe you for this.” 
With that, she turned and led her mount toward the outer gate. Weslin watched her go. "She said yes." He said it once to confirm it was real, then immediately: "What if I cannot learn fast enough? What if I do something wrong and hurt someone again?" He looked at Harmon. "What happens then?" 
Harmon looked at him the way he looked at things. He was still assessing. "Then we deal with it." No softening in it, but no edge either. "You are not the first person to carry something dangerous they did not ask for. You will not be the last." He moved his horse forward. "You will not be doing this without guidance. That is why she said yes." A beat. "What happens next is yours to earn." He did not wait for an answer. 
They turned back toward the heart of town, where raised voices and a tightening crowd had begun to draw attention.
At the corner of a cart vendor’s stall, Nyssa stood stiffly, arms folded, face casual, but Weslin could see the tension behind her eyes. Across from her, a squat man with a nose like a hawk and bright, accusing eyes was jabbing a finger at her.
"That’s her!” The man’s voice cracked like a whip. “I never forget a face.” He jabbed two fingers at his own temple. “Steel trap.” He started to move around his booth, “You lifted two gold bars. Rings, off my cart."
Nyssa did not step back. She blinked once, slow, offended on purpose. “Sir, you are mistaken. I have not set foot in Emberfast. You must have me confused with another customer you tried to cheat. I’ve got one of those faces,” she said with a thin smile.
“Liar.” Spit flashed in his beard when he said it. “Same hair. Same eyes. Same smug mouth.”
Nyssa’s smile stayed thin. Her eyes did not. “Then you should call a guard, not a crowd.”
The nearest onlookers leaned in anyway. The merchant tasted that attention and swelled with it.
“As you wish,” he snapped, his voice carrying. He took a moment to draw in a breath, making a show of it for his new onlookers. Then he let out a bellow, “GUARDS!” 
Harmon cursed under his breath. He was already moving, stepping between them, palms open in a calming gesture. "Let’s not make this a scene," Harmon said. He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. "Maybe we can sort this out like neighbors."
"She’s a thief! And now you’re harboring her. I want justice!" The man blew a whistle, sharp and loud. The man blew a whistle, sharp and loud. This time, Harmon did not curse. He simply moved, faster than the situation technically required. 
"Nyssa, go. Now!" She didn’t argue. She melted into the crowd like smoke, disappearing down the side street.
Harmon turned, scanning the crowd. Already, he could see heads turning, whispers starting. He moved quickly, rounding up the others. 
Dain came out with bags in his hands. “Why is someone blowing that damned whistle?” He looked around, dumbfounded.
Harmon spotted Dain and rode towards him, “We have worn out our welcome here. Go. Tell everyone you see, we meet outside of town.”
Dain just nods his head, quickly handing off the bags to Harmon and Weslin.
Within minutes, they were all back on horseback, riding hard down the trade road out of Rellisford.
When they’d finally slowed to a trot outside the town’s edge, all of them grouped back up, getting into two columns. Everyone but Harmon was checking behind them. They were not followed. 
The dirt road curved gently into the open countryside, the sounds of Rellisford fading behind them into birdsong and the distant hiss of wind brushing across wheat fields. The team rode in staggered pairs, some in conversation, others quiet. Nyssa trailed just slightly behind the main cluster, her shoulders hunched and lips moving in a low mutter.
Pathetic." She said it like a verdict. "One day." The words came out clipped and sharp. "I could not last one day without ruining it.”
She didn’t notice when Harmon pulled up beside her. He didn’t get her attention until his shadow fell across her saddle.
Harmon didn’t look at her. His gaze stayed fixed ahead, scanning the horizon. He said nothing.
Nyssa’s jaw worked once. “I know. I ruined it.” Her voice tried to be careless and failed. “You do not have to say it. I know.”
Harmon just rode beside her and let the road speak first. Nyssa could not stand the quiet. “There is too much I cannot explain. I do not even know what else can come back on us.”
Harmon gave a small nod, eyes forward. When he spoke, it was flat, professional. Worse than anger. “All right. This changes the plan.”
Nyssa flinched like he had touched a bruise. “No. Harmon, do not.” The words came too fast. “Do not toss me aside. Do not cut me loose.”
“Enough.” One word. Clean. He kept his gaze on the horizon. “I am not cutting you loose.” He swallowed before delivering the blade of it without heat. “I cannot use you as I intended. Your face is known here now. I need someone who already works in the dark.”
Nyssa managed a small, wounded exhale. “Oh.” She stared at the road until her breathing returned, and so did her poise. 
"Now,” Harmon said. “Tell me more about this Riven. Will he help?"
She nodded, still quiet.
"We are going after him. With your face soon to be plastered everywhere. I need someone who already works in the dark around here."
He spurred his horse forward, the plan shifting with each mile. Behind him, Weslin glanced back at Rellisford one last time. The town was already going small in the haze, the docks and the warehouses and the bakery woman and the fox in the reeds all folding together into distance. He had been there less than two hours. It already felt like something he would remember.
He faced forward and rode.
