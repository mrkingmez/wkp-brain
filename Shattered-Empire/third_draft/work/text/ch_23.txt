Aldrek’s knee kept him pinned to the floor. Dain felt discomfort in his ribs. He groaned, low and annoyed. “Off me, brother,” Dain said evenly.

His lip was split, a bruise already purpling under his eye, but his eyes were alert, and that familiar half-smirk tugged at the corner of his mouth. “Good hit, brother,” he said, coughing.

Aldrek grunted and rose, yanking him upright with one firm pull.

“You disguised yourself as a prison guard?” the priest said, unimpressed.

“Improvisation,” Dain replied, brushing soot from his borrowed uniform. “Your tackle was appreciated. Brought the role together.”

Nyssa crossed her arms. “What are you doing here?”

Dain tapped the insignia stitched above his chest, the rank sigil of a senior escort officer. “I’m here to get you out.”

From inside the sealed cell behind them, Riven’s voice drifted through the slit in the door, dry as old parchment. “Charming plan so far. Do I get to be rescued before or after the punchlines?”

“We’re working on it,” Nyssa called back.

“What happened to the drainage tunnel?” Aldrek asked, ignoring the sarcasm.

“Scrapped,” Dain said. “Blocked by twelve guards standing watch over the vault corridor. They're not forged, but they’re elite. Professional. Won’t move. Won’t blink. We never would’ve made it through.”

Weslin stepped up, brows knitting. “But... the plan—”

Dain fixed Weslin with a look. “Rule one, my young man, expect the plan to break.”

“You already told me rule one was keeping my eyes open,” Weslin shot back.

Dain shook his head, “They are all rule one,” He tried a smile. The pain in his ribs killed it before it finished. He breathed out carefully and continued. 

“Harmon knew it might fall apart,” Dain said. “Once we saw the link was dead, we sealed it. No runners. No signals. If that spread, the whole descent burned.”

Nyssa’s eyes narrowed. “So he didn’t trust us to let us know about the backup?”

Dain shook his head. “It wasn’t about trust. It was about containment.” 

From behind the door, Riven muttered, “Sounds like your boss is paranoid. I like him already.”

A moment later, another boom shook the walls, causing dust to trickle from the stone ceiling. Followed by another crash, steel on stone, or maybe stone melting. The faint roar of fire echoed through the vents. Dain gave the group a questioning look.

“Ash,” Nyssa said under her breath.

Aldrek murmured a prayer, fingers brushing the symbol carved into the haft of his mace. “Let the fire purify what must burn.”

Dain glanced upward, unfazed. “He’s doing his part. Time for us to do ours.”

Nyssa reached for the locking mechanism, fingers brushing the cold steel.

“Wait,” Weslin hissed, stepping forward. Everyone froze.

Weslin leaned in close, eyes scanning the frame. “There’s a ward concealed under the locking. Let me look.” He examined for a few moments. “Definitely a ward,” Weslin said. “Once it’s tripped, it runs to completion. There’s no cutting it short.”

Nyssa’s hand hovered, now still. “How do you know?” she asked.

“I heard it, and when I went to look away, I saw the smallest flicker.” He didn’t look up. “Aurelia taught me to listen to the magic’s hum, not the way it feels. This one’s tight, like pulled thread. Looking at it, this one is delicate; it could go off if done wrong.”

“Good catch, pup,” said Nyssa

“You can disarm it?” Dain asked.

“I’ve never done one alone,” Weslin admitted, voice quiet. “But I’ve practiced enough to try.”

Nyssa crouched beside him. “Do what you were trained to do, pup. I’m right here.” She gave a reassuring squeeze to the soldier, and he blushed a bit. 

Weslin nodded. He pulled the same silver chisels and an etching needle and got to work. He switched the smallest blade, barely longer than a fingernail, and pressed it gently to the first rune.

The ward buzzed.

He inhaled, then began to move, not fast, but with a steadiness that came from knowing the room had no margin for error. One rune at a time. He was missing the connection with Aurelia, yet he went on. He nicked and scraped, deforming curves and slicing along precise axis lines, turning active glyphs into inert scars.

When the final rune bent under his chisel, the magic dispersed with a soft hiss, like breath escaping from a glass lung.

The lock gave a quiet click. No fire.

The cell inside was cold by design. The walls were bare blackstone, sealed smooth, with embedded ward lines dimmed from lack of light. The glowstones overhead were dulled and crusted with age, casting the cell in a bluish haze that left most of it in shadow.

All three men were chained in place, seated on low stone benches, iron cuffs bolted to the floor. The chains gave each of them just enough room to sleep on the floor. 

The first was tall, lean in the shoulders, but broad at the chest. His skin was sun-worn, olive-toned, with dark hair and the sharp eyes of someone who measured every person in the room within seconds. His boots were still polished. That was the first giveaway, soldier.

Riven stood the moment the door opened, chains clinking at his ankles, a slow grin spreading across his face.

“Nyssa Quill,” he said, voice low and warm. “I was starting to think you’d left me here on purpose.”
She stepped into the cell without hesitation. He moved to her, and they embraced, tight, fierce, and wordless. For a moment, the prison fell away. Her arms wrapped around him with an urgency that betrayed more than friendship, and he buried his face in the crook of her neck like someone breathing freely for the first time in days. 

From the threshold, Weslin caught Dain’s expression shift as the moment passed, just a flash. Something in it that Weslin could not name. He looked away first. Some things were not his to hold onto. 

“So…” Dain said, clearing his throat. “Are you going to introduce us, Nyssa? Or should we just assume this is the charming one?”

Nyssa stepped back, her hand still on Riven’s chest, steady and familiar. “This is my brother,” she said smoothly. “Riven.”

“Guilty.” Riven’s smile was easy, confident. He laughed and turned, gesturing casually to the other two. “And these two fine gents are Haldrin, the never-stopping lecturer. And Torrek doesn’t talk unless the walls start caving in.”

The second man, Haldrin, gave a stiff nod. He had graying brown hair tied back with a scrap of parchment, spectacles resting on his nose despite a bruised cheek. His robe, though singed at the hem, still bore the crest of the Althrun Collegium. Books had shaped his posture, back straight and chin elevated.

The third, Torrek Mull, didn’t bother standing. He was massive, arms corded with muscle and old scars. His clothes were thick canvas, tailored for movement, dirt still crusted into the folds. A wide iron collar ringed his throat, runes flickering faintly along its edge. He lifted his gaze slowly, like stone grinding against stone.

“Don’t talk much,” Riven said. “But when he does, it’s usually to say something like, ‘Move,’ or ‘Duck.’”

A distant boom shuddered through the floor. Dust sprinkled from the stone ceiling. Somewhere far above, Ash was still fighting, and the outcome was still unknown.

Nyssa started to work on each of their chains, popping them one by one. 

Aldrek stepped forward, eyes scanning each of them. “Any injuries?” he asked, voice low but firm. “Tell me now.” His hand hovered near the symbol on his mace, a faint glow building at his fingertips.

Riven shook his head. “Bruised pride, maybe. But we’re good.”

Haldrin winced and touched his side. “A cracked rib, I think. Nothing serious.”

Aldrek stepped to him, placed a steady hand over the injury. Soft white light pulsed from his palm, and Haldrin exhaled slowly as the pain dulled.

“Better?”

“Yes,” the scholar replied, surprised. “Much.”

Torrek didn’t speak but nodded once. Aldrek didn’t press him.

Dain stepped forward and tossed down a wrapped bundle. “Time to change. We’re walking you out like you belong here.”

Aldrek caught one of the sets midair. The robes were stiff, formal, the kind worn by court scholars or traveling magistrates. Deep chestnut brown with embroidered crimson edging, the fabric was clean, if plain, and unmistakably official. Matching sigil pins gleamed from the inside fold, forged insignias from a sanctioned research guild.

“Get dressed,” Dain said. “We’ve got a window, and it’s not going to stay open forever.”

A knock rang out from another cell.

Riven turned. “That must be the new guy. He was meant to be with us when we went on his lordship's little job.”

They moved to the door, footsteps cautious. Another weak knock, then silence. The cell was darker than the rest, its glowstones nearly dead. Only the faint gleam of iron reflected through the slit.

Two men stood within. One was pressed close to the door, beard wiry and unkempt, his eyes sunken but alert. The other hung back, his frame larger, shoulders broad despite the grime clinging to his skin. Both looked like they hadn’t eaten a proper meal in weeks, their clothes ragged, cheeks hollow, skin pale under a layer of prison dust.

“We’re getting you out,” Nyssa said softly, already examining the lock. Nyssa’s eyes flicked to the corridor, then back to the lock. “We move now,” she said. “You keep pace, or you don’t come.”

Weslin stepped up and froze. His brow creased. “Wait.” He swallowed. “Water ward.”

Dain looked sharply at him. “Serious?”

Weslin nodded. “Water-bound,” he murmured. “Different weave, I can handle it.” His words were more confident than his body language as he got to work.

Nyssa leaned close. “You’ve got this, pup. Focus.”

Weslin dove right to work, moving with efficiency. When he finished the final stroke, a hiss released from the wall, joining him, letting out a breath he didn’t know he was holding. The ward faded. The lock clicked. The door opened.

The two men stepped forward slowly, blinking against the light.

Nyssa scanned them both. “Names?”

The taller one coughed and said, “Calen.” He rubbed his wrists as the chains fell away.

The other hesitated. Then, with a dry smile that didn’t reach his eyes, he muttered, “Tarn.” His gaze lingered on Dain a moment too long before he looked away. 

Dain raised an eyebrow but said nothing. He tossed each of them a folded magistrates’ robe.

“Put these on. You’re the inspection detail now. Try to look like you belong. You two are good to go, right?”

They obeyed without comment and nodded their heads in confirmation. As they pulled the clothes on, Dain added, “We’re leaving as the delegation Harmon and Neavin used on their way in. They left under the first distraction. You’re filling the numbers.”

“Harmon’s waiting?” Calen asked.

“Yes,” Dain said quickly, stepping away.

Tarn’s face darkened. He muttered nothing, just clenched his jaw as if trying to grind the name from his memory. His hands trembled slightly as he fastened the robe’s front clasp.

A dull boom shook the floor again.

The group pressed upward.

“That didn’t sound good,” said Aldrek.

They arrived back on the third level, and what they saw shocked them.

Ash sat propped against a scorched wall, his coat burned at the sleeve. The body of the Forged brute lay face down beside him, arms sprawled out, steam curling from his tunic.

Weslin gasped, wide-eyed. “Is he dead?”

Ash cracked one eye open. “Not last time I checked. He’s just taking a long nap.”

He winced as Aldrek knelt beside him and pressed a hand to his chest. Light spilled through the contact, warm and quiet. Ash hissed again and rolled his shoulder.

“Little tougher than I thought,” he muttered. “But hey... I needed the practice.”

Dain tossed him a robe. “Put this on. You’re part of the escort team now.”

Ash blinked in surprise. “Dain?”

“Surprise,” Dain said with a smirk. “Let’s get moving.”

They climbed up the next stairwell, the scent of smoke thickening as they rose.

Walls blackened, guard towers half gone, smoke veined the pre-dawn sky. Rubble and fire blurred the yard into a battlefield still breathing. Ash peered around the smoke. “That... could’ve gone worse.”

Nyssa coughed into her sleeve. “Next time, maybe 50% less.”

Dain straightened his posture, and the group followed suit. They moved as a column, formal, organized. Nyssa led with authority, Aldrek beside her, his eyes downcast and his expression passive. Weslin clutched a thick ledger stolen from one of the desk drawers. The two new prisoners walked stiffly behind, robed and silent.

“We are the inspection,” Dain said, each word flat and final. “The fire will give us our cover. Eyes forward. Nobody speaks unless I do.” The group made its way fully into the courtyard. The guard’s attention slid past them, split between smoke, flame, and three competing commands. 

Dain pointed to a litter and barked in a clipped accent, “Need more help near the breach!”

One of the guards waved them on without even glancing. “Go, go! South wall’s still hot!”

They slipped around a broken siege cart, ducked under a cracked arch, and passed through the side gate into a narrow alley.

Neavin popped up from behind a scorched stone fountain. He brushed dust from his shoulders, eyes gleaming. “Was wonderin' if I'd have to come in after you,” he muttered. 

Nyssa raised an eyebrow. “Some of us had to pick up stragglers.”

Ash nodded toward the wreckage. “You’re welcome for the distraction.”

Neavin snorted. “I give it a 7.5. You lost points for symmetry.”

The drain hole yawned behind the old fountain, a narrow passage bored into stone, barely wide enough to crouch-walk. They dropped into it one by one. Neavin slid down last, sealing the entrance behind him with a muted pulse of earth magic.

They walked in darkness for a while, the air damp and earthy. The tunnel was ancient and moss-lined, narrowing and widening like a living vein beneath the city. Eventually, they reached a crumbling stone stairwell that led up through a cracked drainage spout into what had once been a noble’s keep.

Half a tower still stood, its spine twisted toward the sky like a broken finger. Vines had overtaken the outer walls, curling through cracks in the mortar, clawing through stone with slow hunger. The crest carved into the stone archway had been worn to a ghost of itself by wind and time.

A wide road curled from the ruin, sunken with age, swallowed by grass. But it was not silence that greeted them. The clash of iron on iron echoed across the clearing, grunts, a barked curse, the unmistakable chime of a blade striking armor.

They crested the last rise and stopped.

Harmon stood in the center of a torn courtyard, his silhouette framed by the rising dawn and the ruin’s shattered wall. His armor glinted in the fractured light, polished steel overlaying deeper, arcane seams of etched magic. His greatsword shone with the majesty of such a sword, haft tall as a man, its edges crackling faintly with energy that shimmered like heat rising from stone.

Three figures surrounded him, Forged, Merrick’s men. Each wore the black and iron uniform of their hidden order, weapons raised, circling like wolves.

But Harmon was no prey.

He moved like a storm given flesh. Fast. Relentless. He snatched the sword from the stone with one hand. The air cracked as he spun, blade cleaving in a clean arc. One man didn’t even scream, his head separated from his shoulders, body tumbling backward like a sack of grain.

The second lunged.

Harmon ducked beneath the swing, twisting low and letting the man’s blade skid across his shoulder. He answered with a reverse stroke, slicing across the gut, then kicked the man backward into the ruined archway. The man staggered, blood spraying from his midsection, and Harmon slid his sword tip between the ribs. He was dead before he slid down the stones.

The last Forged tried to run.

Harmon raised a hand, and the Forged’s boots locked into place. The ground trembled. With a blur of unnatural motion, Harmon was behind him. He whispered something, no louder than breath, and brought the blade down diagonally.

The last Forged collapsed, bisected, and still twitching.

Then, Harmon swayed.

The greatsword dropped from his hand with a low clang, and he dropped to a knee. His shoulders heaved.

Weslin broke from the group, rushing ahead. “Harmon!”

He slid down beside the armored warrior, arms out to catch him as he slumped forward.

“I’m fine,” Harmon grunted. “Just... get me on the horse.” As soon as the words left his lips, the sword and his armor were gone.

Weslin stared. The sword was gone and the armor with it, as if neither had ever existed. He filed it away in the part of his mind that had gotten very crowded in the last few weeks and kept moving. 

Aldrek was already there, one hand glowing white as he steadied Harmon under the arm. “You overexerted. One day, your pride’s going to kill you.”

“Not today,” Harmon said through gritted teeth.

They guided him to his steed, already saddled and waiting in the cover of vines. Weslin helped hoist him up into the saddle, and Harmon sat heavy, reins slack in one hand.

Hoofbeats thundered from the far path.

Garr arrived first, sword still drawn. His black cloak caught the wind like a banner. Aurelia followed close behind, eyes scanning, her breath misting from the cold of her internal focus.

“What happened?” Garr demanded, pulling up beside Harmon’s horse.

Harmon lifted his head, jaw tight. “They were waiting,” he said. “Not to kill me. To measure me. Seems this passage is a secret to everyone.”

Garr’s eyes swept the bodies. “You left no survivors.”

“They saw too much," Harmon said. "Aldoria and Opindale have kept the peace for too long. I will not be the thing that ends it.”

Aurelia stepped closer, laying a hand on Harmon’s side. Her fingers brushed the cut in his armor. “He needs rest.”

“He’ll get it,” Garr said. He turned, raising his voice. “Mount up! We ride now. If they find this ruin empty, let them wonder where the wind took us.”

Ash was already swinging himself onto a borrowed gelding, his coat scorched and reeking of smoke. “I’ll take wind over shackles,” he muttered.

Nyssa helped Riven into the saddle behind her. “Just hold on,” she whispered, and he gave a grin that didn’t reach his eyes.

The two men rescued from the extra cell exchanged glances. Their borrowed robes hung awkwardly on thin frames. One of them, who had called himself “Calen” without conviction, clutched the saddle horn like it might vanish. The other, “Tarn,” stared long at Harmon with eyes full of old memories and older hatred.

No one questioned it.

They rode hard into the darkness of the valley, one fire out, others waiting. Nyssa glanced back once at Harmon, seeing the weight in his shoulders that no victory could ease.
