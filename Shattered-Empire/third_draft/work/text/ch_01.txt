Weslin pounded down the stone corridors of the palace, his short legs and rounded frame protesting with every stride. The air was cool and smelled faintly of lamp oil and old dust, stirred by the echo of his hurried steps. His lungs burned, each breath louder than the slap of his boots on the polished floor. Shadows swayed with each flicker of torchlight, stretching long across carved columns and banners that lined the hall. Once, he nearly clipped the edge of a marble pedestal, his shoulder grazing the cold stone before he pushed harder.

This was no ordinary sprint. The King himself had sent him. These were the King’s halls, solemn and echoing, and his duty weighed heavier with every step. Pages did not run in the royal palace. They walked with quiet dignity, heads bowed, every movement rehearsed. But when the King sent you to fetch a knight, you did not go at your own pace.

The corridor curved sharply, its vaulted ceiling drawing his eye up for just a heartbeat to where painted heraldry looked down on him: golden crowns, silver blades, crimson dragons that seemed to stir in the torchlight. He rounded the bend too fast, boots skidding on the smooth floor, nearly pitching him into a wall. He caught himself, chest heaving, heart pounding in his ears.

Ahead, framed in the glow of a sconce, stood a broad-shouldered man adjusting the clasp of a dark, tailored cloak. He moved with the unhurried care of one preparing to depart, as though the palace and its duties no longer bound him.

“Sir Harmon… Please! Hold!” Weslin wheezed, the last word scraping out of him. He tried to wave, stumbled, and collided headlong into a suit of decorative armor.

The crash was thunderous. Steel plates clattered across the polished floor, a spear rolled into the shadows, and a bowl of glass beads, left on a nearby stand as decoration, upended, scattering across the hall like marbles. The echoes reached clear to the kitchens.

Flat on his back amid the wreckage, Weslin shut his eyes tight. His chest heaved. Every breath was fire in his throat. Glass beads pressed into his back through the tunic. Somewhere nearby, a piece of armor was still rocking, its clatter ticking down to silence like a rebuke. The first time Gus trusts me with page duty, and I've ruined it. A feast day of all days, and I've ruined everything.  

A shadow fell over him. “You know,” came a voice, unhurried, faintly amused, “I’ve a bear rug that lands the same way. Quieter, though.” A faint pause. “Less red in the face. And hairier.”

Weslin cracked one eye open. Sir Harmon Diem loomed above him, one eyebrow cocked, the hint of a grin pulling at his mouth.

With a grunt, Harmon lowered himself onto one knee beside the boy. His joints cracked, and he winced. “Gods, these knees,” he muttered. “Too many miles. Word of advice, lad, never get old. You may look wiser, but your joints will betray you for it.”

He leaned closer, brushing aside beads and resting a firm hand on Weslin’s shoulder. “No cuts. No broken bones?” His gaze moved once, assessing, already knowing the answer.

Weslin shook his head quickly, still too winded to speak.

“Good lad.” Harmon slid an arm under his back and another beneath his arm, then lifted him in one smooth motion. Even from one knee, the knight’s strength was undeniable. He set Weslin carefully on his feet and steadied him until the boy found his balance.

Only then did Harmon push himself upright. He rose with another grunt, the effort clear in his movements. Straightening his cloak, he gave a crooked smile. “Sadists made stone floors,” he muttered.

Weslin’s face burned hotter as Harmon brushed a few stray beads from his tunic. Weslin wobbled, caught himself, and looked over the disaster: scattered beads, crooked armor plates, and one lonely spear rolling slowly into the shadowed edge of the hall.

“Steady, lad,” Harmon said with a small smile. “Which of you came off worse?”

Weslin gulped for air, words broken by his ragged breathing. “The King… sent me. He needs you… before you leave.”

“Ah,” Harmon said, the grin widening. “So that noise wasn’t wasted. Well struck, page. You’ve carried out your duty, whether by feet or by falling.” He clapped Weslin on the shoulder hard enough to jostle him.

Weslin wobbled, caught himself, and looked over the disaster: scattered beads, crooked armor plates, and one lonely spear rolling slowly into the shadowed edge of the hall. Weslin swayed, then steadied. “Not the Grand Hall, sir,” he added quickly. “His Majesty said… his private study.”

That drew Harmon’s brows up. For a moment, the humor left his face, replaced by something sharper, thoughtful, edged with concern. Then it was gone, replaced by the genial mask again.

He tugged his cloak more securely across his shoulders and motioned down the corridor. “Now then, His Majesty waits. Let’s keep him from waiting longer. And mind the beads. I’d rather not end up on the floor beside you.”

Harmon had just steadied Weslin on his feet when the corridor filled with the swish of silks and the hard stamp of boots. A tall man in a fur-lined coat of the royal colors strode in, chambermaids trailing like frightened birds. His eyes swept the wreckage, toppled armor, scattered beads, boy and knight, and his face curled with disdain.

“What disgrace is this?” he snapped. His voice cracked like a whip. His gaze landed on Weslin. “Of course. You. I should have known. Trouble has a habit of finding you. Though I imagine it saves time when it needn’t look far,” Cotter said coldly in his nasally voice. “Look, ladies, is he even wearing a page’s tunic now? On a feast night?” He shook his head slowly, savoring the venom. “Gus must have taken leave of his senses. He and I shall have words later.”

Weslin opened his mouth, but Cotter cut him down with a hiss. “Silence. You’ll clean this wreck, now, and tomorrow you’ll take ten lashes for the shame of it. Perhaps pain will teach what instruction has not.”

The words struck harder than a hand, and Weslin wilted.

Then Harmon’s voice entered, calm, deep, carrying iron under velvet. “Enough.”

The steward startled, as though seeing him for the first time. He gave a stiff bow, the edge of arrogance still in his spine. “Sir… forgive me. I’m Nicholas Cotter, head steward of Fowther Palace. A busy day, you understand. Pages must be kept in line, especially on a feast night. One careless boy can shame an entire household before the first bell is rung. I assure you, this staff strives for perfection.” Nicholas's glare shot daggers at Weslin, “We try to limit the incompetence, yet some seep through. What is one to do?" 

“Incompetence,” Harmon repeated, his tone dropping like a hammer. He took a step forward, boots grinding on glass beads. “Yes. I see it plainly." Harmon pauses for a heartbeat. “Yours.”

The chambermaids froze; Cotter’s face drained of color.

“This boy,” Harmon pressed on, laying a hand firmly on Weslin’s shoulder, “just saved you from explaining to the King why I nearly missed His Majesty’s summons. An audience that your staff told me not ten minutes ago I didn't have. He ran himself near to collapse. Fell, bloodied, humiliated himself, all to do his duty.” Harmon’s eyes, sharp as drawn steel, locked on Cotter. “That is loyalty. That is courage. And you would reward it with lashes?”

Cotter swallowed. His lips parted, but Harmon snapped a hand up sharply. “Silence. You’ve said enough.” Harmon didn’t raise his voice. He didn’t need to. “If this is the measure of your judgment, then this palace is far less well run than its gilded walls pretend. Fine stone and polished floors don’t excuse poor stewardship,” he said flatly.

Cotter flinched as though struck. The chambermaids exchanged glances, their silence louder than whispers.

Harmon’s voice softened only when he turned back to Weslin. “Your name is Weslin, isn’t it?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Weslin. I require a companion this evening. Walk with me.”

The boy blinked, wide-eyed, then nodded with such force his hair flopped into his face.

“Then you’ll walk with me to His Majesty's Chambers.” Harmon gave him a reassuring squeeze on the shoulder. “No lashes. No punishment. You are with me now.”

At last, he turned back to Cotter, his tone once more edged with that polished steel. “See this mess cleared before His Majesty hears of it. He expects order in his halls, not shrill voices and toppled armor.” He paused, as though reconsidering, then added almost kindly: “Still, for all its flaws, this palace is one of the finer ones I’ve seen. You should be proud… provided you learn to keep it worthy of its walls.”

Cotter’s mouth opened, closed, and he gave a jerky bow. “Yes, Sir Harmon. At once.”

With that, Harmon and Weslin moved on together down the corridor. Weslin matched his stride to the knight’s, chest swelling, the sting of Cotter’s venom fading beneath the weight of Harmon’s words. For the first time, he felt neither chastised nor chosen.

As they reached the end of the hall, Cotter’s voice carried after them, sharp and flustered: “Let’s get this cleaned up, but you heard the man!”

Both Harmon and Weslin glanced at each other, and the same small smile tugged at their lips before they walked on.

As the steward’s voice faded behind them, Harmon and Weslin walked in silence. The boy’s stride was straighter now, chest lifted as though the knight’s hand still rested on his shoulder.

“Thank you, sir,” Weslin said, his voice quieter than he intended. “For what you did back there.”

Harmon glanced at him, a hint of humor in his steel-gray eyes. “Nothing to thank me for. You had a mission, and you carried it out. Running half the palace, crashing through armor, nearly killing yourself to do it… That’s more than some grown men would dare. I could hardly stand by and let your devotion be mistaken for mischief.”

Weslin flushed, unsure if it was from pride or embarrassment. “Still, sir. I’ll remember it.”

"Do that." He let the silence take a breath before continuing. "But remember this. Remember it as well, loyalty earns enemies first. Friends come later." 

They turned into another corridor, where two towering carved-oak doors loomed ahead. Weslin slowed, glanced to either side, then stepped toward a seemingly plain stretch of wall. From his pocket, he drew a small iron key. He slid it into an almost invisible hole and turned it. With a soft click, a hidden panel eased open, revealing a narrow passage beyond.

“This way, sir.” He produced a small iron key from his pocket that Gus had pressed into his hand for this errand, along with strict instructions on where to use it. 

Harmon’s grin spread as the wall shifted. “Ah. One of my favorite things about this palace. The secrets are tucked away where no one expects. King Wymark had a gift for such things. I mean Wymark the First, not Edric, the current King’s grandsire.”

They stepped inside. Torchlight guttered in iron brackets, their flames low and flickering. The air was cool and damp, carrying the faint scent of dust and stone. The stairwell curled upward, its steps worn smooth by centuries of secret use. Narrow alcoves yawned on either side, some empty, others filled with half-rotted crates or forgotten banners sagging in cobwebs.

The ceiling pressed low, the walls closed, and every sound seemed to echo, Harmon’s boots striking stone, Weslin’s quicker steps, even the rasp of their breathing. Strange markings etched into the stone walls hinted at old masons’ hands, wards, or signatures, perhaps, long faded by time. Weslin almost touched one, then thought better of it. Some things looked like they didn't want to be touched.  

“This place feels… different,” Weslin whispered. “Like no one should be here.”

“That’s the point,” Harmon replied. His hand brushed the wall as he climbed, fingers tracing grooves in the stone. “A king’s strength isn’t only in what is seen, but what is hidden.”

Weslin hesitated, then ventured: “You knew the Mad…” He stopped himself too late.

Harmon’s head turned sharply, eyes narrowing. “Know your place, boy,” Harmon said sharply yet showing some restraint in his tone. “King Liam Wymark was a great man. My friend. Do not dishonor his memory with tavern-born names.”

Weslin swallowed hard, his earlier confidence faltering. “Yes, sir. Sorry, sir. It won’t happen again.”

The knight’s gaze softened with the weight of years. He let his palm rest flat on the cold stone, eyes distant. “It is a shame what befell him near the end. But he had his reasons. Another word of advice, son, never speak ill of the dead. It is bad form.”

“Yes, sir,” Weslin whispered. He glanced back, for the first time seeing the true age in Harmon’s face, not hidden by humor.

“Good lad.” Harmon let the silence settle. Together they climbed, their torchlit shadows stretching and swaying along the narrow walls, until another disguised panel swung open at Weslin’s touch. The hidden way delivered them into the warm light of the King’s private study.

Though the midday sun still shone beyond the palace walls, the King’s windowless study glowed with lamplight and fire, a room that felt like night no matter the hour.

“Sir, he told me once I got you here, to invite you to sit. He’ll be alone shortly. Do you need anything else of me?”

“I am good. But don’t go far, I might need you soon.”

“Of course. I’ll be just outside.”

“Very good, son.”

Weslin bowed and slipped through the main door. As it closed, Harmon turned toward the far wall. A single painting dominated the chamber, a sweeping battle scene, lances lowered, banners snapping, soldiers triumphant.

He froze before it.

At first glance, it was reverence, but then his jaw clenched, his breath shortened. His hand trembled until he forced it flat against his thigh. Sweat prickled at his hairline.

The painting dissolved in his mind. He heard the clang of steel and the screams of dying men. The copper tang of blood rose thick in his throat, and the sour stink of smoke and charred flesh seemed to fill the chamber. Sweat beaded across his brow and ran down his temples. His hands clamped into fists, knuckles white, as his body locked against the onslaught of memory.

Faces surged forward, more vivid than oil on canvas: comrades cut down, friends’ eyes glazing, mouths open in screams that still echoed across his nights. He could smell their sweat, the iron of blood splashing on stone. It was as if the chamber itself had become the battlefield.

The painter had captured victory. Harmon saw only the dying. His knuckles had gone white. He didn't remember making a fist. His chest tightened, breath coming shallow, until a voice broke through like a blade drawn behind him….

“Father commissioned that before his death. He said if he were to lead our people, he had to be reminded of the price paid for their freedom. We placed it here, where he always liked to sit.”

Harmon blinked. The battle, the screams, the blood receded like smoke in a sudden wind. He turned.

A man stood behind him, not yet thirty, clad simply in black and blue with gold piping, the crest of his house on his chest.

Edric’s smile carried warmth, but his eyes studied the older man carefully. Harmon dragged a hand across his forehead, wiping the sheen of sweat away, though his fingers still trembled faintly. The King’s gaze lingered on the gesture, but he said nothing.

Harmon dropped to one knee. “Your Highness.”

“As you were, Sir Harmon,” King Edric said gently. “I invited you here to talk, not for a ceremony.”

Slowly, the knight rose, his body still taut. “Of course, Your Majesty,” Harmon said, giving the title just enough weight to remind Edric he would not be nudged out of it. 

“Harmon… you’re family,” Edric said warmly. “Like an uncle. When we’re in private, how many times have I asked you to call me Edric? You have stood with my family since the very beginning.”

“At least once more, Your Highness,” Harmon replied, with the faintest ghost of a smile.

“Stubborn as ever.” Edric chuckled, then sobered, his gaze flicking toward the painting again. “You and my father were close. Brothers in all but name. You’ve given me that same warmth. For that, I’m grateful.” He gestured toward a couch in the chamber’s center. “Please, sit. Wine? Anything?”

“No, sire. Not right now.” Harmon lowered himself onto the couch.

Edric turned a chair to face him. “Then let’s speak plainly. Governor Drake is expected shortly, but until then, what was it you wished to tell me?”

Harmon straightened. “Your Grace, I’ll not waste time with preamble. My days as your sword are drawing to a close. I cannot fight as I once did. The realm deserves a commander still in his prime. A commander who’s one with the men.”

The King leaned forward, voice low. “You’ve carried this realm longer than most men carry their own lives. If you say your time has come, I’ll hear it. But why now?”
Harmon’s jaw tightened, just for a moment. “Because I can feel it,” he said quietly. “I’m not the man I was, and I won’t pretend to be.”
The King studied him, the warmth in his expression cooling into something more measured. “If this is to be done, it will not be done in haste. A transition like this demands ceremony. The court will expect it. The people will as well.” He leaned back slightly. “We announce it at the feast. Let it be known properly.”
Harmon shook his head once, firmly. “With respect, sire, the realm won’t wait for ceremony. Nor will our enemies.” His voice sharpened, not in defiance, but in urgency. “If I’m to step down, it must be done with purpose, not pageantry.”
He held the King’s gaze a moment longer, then pressed forward. “We mustn’t linger long,” Harmon said firmly. “We have enemies all around. Just as age does not wait, neither does war. We need a replacement in place soon.”

The King was silent, though his mind began to move as swiftly as a general counting positions on a battlefield. Names rose unbidden in his head. Each of them had their merits, yet one was too cautious, another too volatile, and the last too untested. None carried the weight of the man before him.

The King leaned back, studying Harmon as though measuring the weight of the words. “You, stepping away,” Edric said slowly. “I never thought I would see the day. Yet time comes for all of us, even for the Sword of the Realm. Tell me, then, what lies ahead for the great Harmon Diem, once this burden is set down?”

Harmon let the silence stretch before he answered. His gaze was steady, though weariness edged his voice. At last, he drew a long breath. “There is one more service I would render, sire. I would not fade into idleness. To the northwest of Kensington lies an island, scarcely charted. Some say it is rich. I would lead men there, raise a township, and serve as its Mayor, its lord and keeper. Not to guard what already stands, but to expand.”

Edric’s expression hardened. “Expansion is rarely seen as charity. It is seen as an ambition. And ambition starts wars.”

“I have weighed it,” Harmon said quickly. “That is why it must not be the Crown’s hand, but mine. Let it be my venture, with your sponsorship. Grant me ships, supplies, and a small force. With your blessing, the guilds will shoulder the cost. Without it…” He shook his head. “Without it, the dream dies on the tide. And, sire, in all my years, I have never asked for myself. Only for you. Only for Opindale.”

The King did not answer at once. He rose slowly, crossing to the great map table. One hand rested on the parchment, but his gaze was far beyond it.

“I must consider,” he said at last, voice quiet. “Too many moves at once. For the kingdom. For you. For all of us.” His words cut further discussion to its end. The silence stretched, heavy and unresolved.

Harmon sat back, the weight of years and memory pressing on him. The painting loomed behind, its painted banners forever bright, while in his mind the screams of the fallen refused to fade.

Edric turned back around toward Harmon as the chamber doors opened. A short balding man in a plain brown robe entered first, followed by Governor Meridian Drake of Aldoria in a sky-blue tunic edged with sea-green piping. Weslin trailed close behind.

Weslin snapped to attention. “Your Highness. The Governor of Aldoria, Lord Meridian Drake, and Chancellor Ayhmeri.”

Chancellor Ayhmeri bowed. 

The King gave a curt nod. Ayhmeri gestured to Weslin. “Close the doors and see that we are not disturbed.”

“Yes, Chancellor.” With a moment’s hesitation, Weslin obeyed and slipped out.

“Your Highness,” Drake began, “thank you for this invitation. It is an honor.”

“I am honored as well,” Edric replied, his tone cordial but heavy. “Aldoria is a true ally, and you are a true friend. I only wish this were a simple courtesy before tonight’s feast. But there is a second purpose. Please, sit.”

The Chancellor remained standing while Drake lowered himself to the couch.

“I believe privacy is best,” the King said, “for what you are about to hear.”

At the words, Harmon’s attention sharpened, his earlier thoughts dissolving. He reclaimed his seat near the King.

Drake leaned forward, brows drawn tight. “Your Highness, clarify the matter.”

The King gestured toward the knight. “First, meet Sir Harmon Diem. My sword, and commander of my armies. He is here as my counsel, and now yours.”

A flicker of unease crossed Drake’s face as the King continued.

“Stormhaven has fallen victim to a Veydrassil attack. Reports are scarce, as most of our embassy staff are here for the feast, but what has reached me speaks of devastation.”

The Governor’s color drained. “My wife… my children. They stayed behind.” His voice cracked, panic breaking through. “Are they alive? Tell me!” His hands clawed at his tunic.

The King’s voice was steady, though grave. “I have no word on them yet. Our people inside the embassy walls are trapped, sending what they can. I will press them harder, but news will come slowly.”

Drake surged to his feet, trembling. “I depart at once. Grant me leave, Your Grace.”

“Meridian,” the King snapped, shedding all ceremony, “you cannot.”

Drake’s face twisted, anger and terror blending. “And why not?”

“Because you will walk into a trap. If the Veydrassil still occupy Stormhaven, this is no raid. They remain for a reason. If you go, they will cut you down before you take ten steps inside your city,” answered Harmon, speaking up for the King. 

“They are my people,” Drake shot back, voice raw. “My countrymen. They need their leader.”

“With what force do you intend to stand?” Edric’s tone cut like steel. “You came with thirty men at most. Do you think their deaths alongside yours would aid Aldoria? What would it do to those still resisting, to see their Governor slain by an oppressor’s hand?”

Drake faltered, breath shaking. “Then you would have me sit here and call it wisdom.” 

“No,” the King said firmly. “You will not ride to your death. Sit. We will send aid, but it will take time. Forces must be prepared, supply lines drawn, allies secured.”

Drake’s fists clenched, but he sank back down. The King turned to Harmon. “Sir Harmon, your counsel?”

The chamber was heavy with silence, the weight of unspoken fear pressing in. Drake’s eyes burned, his family’s faces vivid in his mind, and no answer came to soothe them. The thought of them trapped in Stormhaven hung between them all, unresolved, suffocating.

Harmon sat back slowly, his eyes narrowing as if he were staring past the walls of the chamber into the city itself. His breath came long and measured, though the furrows on his brow deepened with each passing moment.

His hand lifted almost absently to his chest. The silver chain there caught the lamplight, and his fingers closed around the plain ring that hung from it. He pressed it to his palm, knuckles whitening, as though he were praying with it or drawing some strength through its familiar weight.

The silence stretched. Edric’s gaze flicked toward the old knight, but he said nothing, waiting. Drake’s breathing remained ragged, the governor teetering between anger and despair.

Harmon kept his eyes down, thumb running across the curve of the ring as though tracing a memory in the metal. His jaw tightened. When he finally raised his head, his gaze was steady, but the room could feel the heaviness of the thoughts behind it.

“For starters—” 

“They are in a private meeting!” Weslin’s voice cracked with effort as he tried to hold back a man in armor.

“Get off me, boy, I have news for my governor,” the intruder snapped, shoving past him.

“Weslin, it is all right,” the King ordered, raising a hand. “Thank you. Back to your post.” Weslin released his grip, bowed quickly, and slipped out, closing the doors behind him.

The man in armor strode forward, saluting. “Your Highness. Governor. I bring urgent news.”

The King frowned. “Master of Arms Terance, what is this sudden intrusion?”

Drake stiffened. “I already know, unless you have something else to add.”

Terance blinked. “How, sire? The runner has just arrived.”

The Governor’s head snapped toward him. “This is not about Stormhaven?”

Confusion spread across Terance’s face. “Stormhaven? No, my lord. This is from the north. The pass has been attacked. An army of beasts from the Abandoned Plains. The Northern Garrison is believed to have fallen.”

The chamber fell silent. The King’s voice broke first, low and grim. “God above…”

Drake muttered, “Creator help us all.”

Chancellor Ayhmeri said nothing, his lips pressed thin.

Harmon stepped closer, voice steady. “Casualties? Any confirmation?”

“None, sir,” Terance admitted. “The runner and five others made it here. One was gravely wounded. They saw flames consuming the garrison as they fled.”

The weight of the words hung in the air. Then Terance asked softly, “What happened in Stormhaven?”

The King’s reply was flat. “An attack by the Veydrassil.” Terance cursed under his breath, then fell silent.

All eyes turned to Harmon. He stood with his head bowed, fingers tightening around the ring that hung at his chest as if in prayer. Then, in a single breath, he lifted his chin, his posture shifting, energy surging back into him.

“That alters the field,” Harmon said with command returning to his tone, straightening as if pulled by a line. “Too many questions, too little certainty. We lack knowledge, and that is the greatest danger of all.” He turned first to Terance. “Ready, every man. The palace goes to full alert, garrisons are armed, and walls are watched. Assume siege until proven otherwise. You have my blessing to take charge of securing here.”

Then to the King: “Sire, coordinate with Governor Drake and Chancellor Ayhmeri. Aid to Aldoria must be planned now, but without leaving us blind at home.”

His tone sharpened, leaving no space for debate. “We cannot chase shadows. Not anymore. If Stormhaven burns and the pass is lost, the enemy is already moving faster than we are. We must take control before this spirals completely beyond us.”

The chamber pulsed with his words, urgency hanging thick. Harmon paused, eyes narrowing in thought. No one in that room would be followed to a tavern. No one except a boy who looked like he was running an errand for the kitchens. “Weslin. Back in here.”

The doors opened again, and the boy hurried inside. “Over here, lad.”

Weslin came at a trot. “Yes, sir?”

“I have a task for you. Do you accept it?” Asked Harmon. 

Weslin’s grin was instant. “Yes—I mean, yes, sir,” he said.

“Good. Do you know the Broken Bolt?”

“Yes, sir,” Weslin said, nodding his head. “It’s down the road from my da’s bakery.”

“Then go there. Ask for Luca. Tell him…” Harmon leaned closer, his voice dropping, “The pawn has moved to King’s five. Order food. Sit and wait for me. Understood?”

“Yes, sir. Pawn to King’s five.” His voice carried a thrill at the secret.

“Exactly. And Weslin, tell no one where you are going or what you carry. I chose you because no one will suspect you, and because I trust you. From this moment on, you are my squire.”

The boy’s eyes went wide, his face lighting as if the whole world had opened. He nodded so hard his hair flopped into his face, then sprinted out through the doors, leaving them swinging wide as his footsteps faded down the corridor.

Harmon watched him go. The doors swung back once, twice, and settled. In the lamplight, his expression had changed, not the quiet amusement he wore in corridors, but something closer to a general who has just put his best piece on the board. 

He turned back to the King. “Sire, I would have it made formal. Weslin is now my squire, effective immediately.”

Edric studied him for a moment, then gave a slow nod. “Granted. I think the boy will rise to it.”

“Good,” Harmon said, adjusting his cloak. He turned toward the door.

“Where are you going?” Governor Drake asked, his voice tight.

Harmon’s reply was sharp, final. “To put a torch in that fog. I’ll return in five hours, and we'll see what it burns away." And with that, he stepped out, leaving silence in his wake.
