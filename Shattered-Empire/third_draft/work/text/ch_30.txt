The ridge gave them nothing. Not a trail. Not a break in the trees. Just wind cutting through fir branches and the steady groan of the siege below, muffled but persistent, like a war drum heard through layers of stone.

Lysana pulled up on the reins and narrowed her eyes against the early light. Mist drifted over the treetops. Their breaths came in visible plumes. The terrain ahead was a mess of frost-covered rocks and tangled roots, a slope too steep for most travelers and too silent to be inhabited.

“This is it,” she said, voice flat. Not uncertain. Just grim.

Luca said nothing. He dismounted and moved forward on foot, eyes scanning the rock face like a surgeon studying a scar. The trees grew too thick here, the moss too even. Nature didn’t wrap itself this tightly around nothing.

Lysana dropped down beside him and began feeling along the base of the slope, her gloved fingers trailing over frost-hardened ferns and twisted vines.

“It’s not supposed to be obvious,” she muttered.

“It’s not supposed to be anything,” Luca replied.

Then she found it.

Not a cave. Not a door. Just a half-rotted wedge of wood, nearly consumed by moss, buried in a curtain of roots. The remnants of a support beam may have been angled incorrectly, having been broken decades ago. She tugged. It groaned faintly, then cracked.

Beneath it, hidden under layers of dead leaves and snowmelt, was a narrow gap in the rock, horizontal, jagged, barely visible unless you were kneeling exactly where she was. “Here.”

Luca knelt beside her. The space was no taller than a crouch and no wider than a horse’s flanks, choked with frost-laced vines and old, packed soil. The air inside was colder than the air in the forest. Older, too, stale, like something buried and forgotten.

“No one’s used this in years,” he said.

“That’s why it might still work.”

She drew her long knife and began clearing the overgrowth, cutting slowly, carefully. The vines resisted like tendons, snapping back when she sliced too shallow. Luca braced the beam, shifting debris away from the entry point as she worked. Bit by bit, the mouth of the tunnel widened, not welcoming, but simply opened up. A brittle root snapped somewhere deeper in the dark, sharp enough to make them both pause. When the last of the roots gave way, they both stepped back.

The tunnel yawed open like a crooked throat, sloping downward at an angle steep enough to break an ankle if taken at speed. It was stone-cut, irregular, and built by hands that didn’t care about elegance. The opening was rough but passable, barely, and now exposed just enough for a man and a horse to squeeze through.

They didn’t hesitate. They lit their lantern and gathered up the horses, muffling the bits with strips of cloth and speaking low until the animals steadied.

Luca took the lead, guiding his horse by the halter into the dark, the beast snorting at the total darkness. Lysana followed, her free hand gripping the hilt of her blade as her boots scraped over ice-glazed stone.

The tunnel swallowed them quickly, and within twenty paces, the light from outside vanished completely.

The first twenty paces after the entrance were worse than expected.

The tunnel narrowed sharply, just wide enough for their horses to pass single file, heads bowed under the low, cracked ceiling. The path sloped downward, but the angle wasn’t the problem; it was the illusion of collapse.

Loose rock and heavy striations across the ceiling made it look unstable, as if it would cave in at any moment. Dust coated everything. At least twice, Lysana stopped and raised her hand, peering at stress cracks overhead. The third time, she didn’t halt — a thin rain of grit fell from the ceiling instead, pattering against her glove.

“Looks dangerous,” she muttered.

“It’s not,” Luca said behind her, voice even. “Look at the way the dust settles. Untouched.”

She squinted at the arch above. He was right. The detritus wasn’t freshly fallen; it had accumulated. Whatever happened here, it hadn’t moved in decades. If ever.

They kept going, moving carefully, horses trailing them with slow, hesitant steps. The path dipped lower. The air changed, colder, but less biting. Denser.

Then the tunnel opened like a jaw.

No warning. No soft transition.

One moment, they were shoulder to stone, and the next, they stepped into a vast, hollowed chamber, naturally formed but massive in scope. The ceiling curved high above them, too distant to catch in full by lantern light. Water dripped from ancient stalactites into quiet, frozen pools scattered across the ground like old scars. Lysana’s boot slid slightly on the damp stone, and she steadied herself with a sharp breath. Faint mineral deposits caught the lantern light, glinting with dull green and bone-white shimmer.

They led the horses forward, and the animals immediately became more at ease in the broader space. Their hooves echoed softly instead of scraping. The air, while cold, didn’t burn. It was the kind of cold you could sleep in, if you were desperate.

Which, at the moment, they were. A faint echo rippled somewhere in the vastness above them, so soft it might have been shifting stone.

“This could work,” Lysana said, brushing her glove along the stone. “Warmer than the ridge. And it echoes just enough to hear someone coming.”

“A fire wouldn’t smoke out,” Luca added. “Stone breaks upward. Chimney effect.”

She gave him a sideways glance. “Didn’t know assassins studied airflow.”

“We do. In kitchens,” Luca retorted.

That earned a short huff of amusement.

They chose the northern shelf deliberately. Stone rose on three sides, the drop guarding the fourth. They doubled the tethers, ran them through old iron rings set in the rock, and left the reins short. If anything came from the tunnels, they would hear the panic before they lost the animals. Luca struck a small flint near the center of the chamber onto a firelog, and in moments, a low flame flickered to life, thin and controlled.

From where they stood, they could see five tunnels emerging from the space like veins. One was entirely collapsed, sealed with boulders, and half-swallowed by the wall. Another looked tight, narrow, and descending at an odd angle. The other three varied in height and width, but the largest was wide enough to stand side by side without ducking.

Lysana walked toward the smallest viable one first. “Scout it?” she asked.

Luca nodded. 

They took one lantern and moved carefully into the side tunnel, leaving their fire behind like a heartbeat fading into a dream. This route was narrower than it looked, an uneven path that twisted twice and ended in a squat, square chamber cut by hand.

Crates lined the walls, some collapsed, some intact, all crusted with decades of neglect, left behind and forgotten. Dust lay thick across the stone floor around them. Luca found and picked up a hammer, holding it sideways. He swung it, smashing in the side of one to reveal old ropes. He took a moment to take in his surroundings, then nodded to himself before saying, “This tunnel was likely used during construction. To build the fort wall. Probably served as storage.”

Lysana stepped over a crate and nudged it open with her boot. “Could’ve doubled as an emergency exit as well. Looks like no one’s been here in years. Forgotten. Never documented.”

A flicker of unease crossed Luca’s face, gone as quickly as it appeared. He paused for a moment, then said, “Speaking of forgotten, we finally heard back from Harmon. A message came through not an hour ago. Looks like I was talking to just Weslin there. Harmon was out of action.”

“Is he all right?” asked Lysana.

“Seems to be now, so he told me to do what I can and report back when something changes, or we have a plan.”

“At least that is what we are doing anyway,” she said while marking the entrance wall with an ‘X’ by scraping a stone on the wall. “No way through,” she confirmed.

They returned to the central cavern and stood in silence for a moment, gazing down the wide tunnel mouth across from them, the largest of the five, its edges smoother than the others, as if deliberately crafted and reinforced at one time. However, the support beams had long since rotted.

“This one,” Lysana said.

“Looks as if this could be the main tunnel,” Luca agreed.

He marked it with a clean double line beside the entrance. Nothing ornate. Just a way to keep track of where they had gone. They adjusted their cloaks and moved forward into the dark.

This tunnel sloped downward at a steady incline, the floor worn smooth by time, ice, and disuse. Somewhere deeper ahead, a soft tap echoed, stone shifting, or something brushing against the wall. Their boots moved quietly here, the air thickening with depth, walls pulling in closer.

After a hundred paces, the tunnel narrowed again, less a corridor now, more a crack forced open by ancient pressure. Lysana slowed instinctively, testing her footing before trusting the glossy frost beneath her boots. The stone underfoot was slick, glossy in places, where condensation had frozen in irregular sheets.

Lysana took the lead, lantern raised high, her breath controlled but shallow. “Hate this part,” she muttered.

“What part?” Luca asked back.

“Walking into nothing and pretending it's a plan. Waiting on a trap that hasn’t been sprung yet. You know the fun stuff of walking in blind.” Lysana answered. 

Luca said nothing, but his eyes were scanning ahead, watching each foot placement, just as she was. 

They reached a turn where the passage curved left, steeper now, walls tighter. The footing changed. A thin dusting of frost disguised a shallow ridge in the floor, and Lysana’s boot caught it wrong. Her foot slid out. She went down hard.

The lantern struck first, cracking but not shattering, and skidded out of reach. Her shoulder hit stone. Then the slope took her. She began to slide. “Shit—!”

Luca was already moving. He dropped into a controlled slide, one boot anchoring behind a stone lip, one hand outstretched. His fingers caught the back of her cloak just as her legs kicked toward open air.

There was a drop, barely visible in the dark. Five, maybe six feet. Shallow enough to survive, but narrow enough to break something doing it.

He yanked hard. Her weight slammed into him, knocking the breath from his chest, but they didn’t fall.

For a second, they stayed like that, his arm around her torso, her breathing fast and sharp, the silence crackling between them louder than a scream. A few loose pebbles rattled down the slope beside them, breaking the stillness.

“Thanks,” she said finally.

“You’re welcome,” he replied, voice flat but strained.

They climbed up the slope together, slow and controlled this time. Lysana retrieved the lantern, its glass cracked, but the light was still flickering.

She sat back on her heels, checking the cut on her hand. It was shallow, more bruised pride than injury. Luca knelt beside her, “Let me see.” He took her hands in his as he gave them the once-over. His hand felt warm, felt warm to her. He put her hands together, looked up at her, and smiled at her. It looked out of place, but still she liked it. 

“I was just thinking,” Luca said suddenly, keeping his voice low as they moved, yet his voice still echoed. “You’ve heard my story. What’s yours? How did you get here in a cave with me?”

Her facial expression changed from smiling to surprised as she gasped, “What?”

“Fair’s fair. What’s your story, Lys?” he said with the short name sounding odd coming from him.

Whatever she was thinking, it crossed her face before she had a chance to decide not to show it. After staring at him for a second, thinking about everything this moment, she rolled her shoulder and leaned back against the wall.

“I guess you're right. Mine is not as rich as yours, it's simple, really. My parents were scouts,” she said. “Real ones. Mapmakers, route runners. I was born in the saddle, basically. By fifteen, I was running messages between war camps and scouting ahead. Outran wolves once. Not fun.”

“That explains your instincts,” he said. 

“It explains why I don’t like standing still,” she chuckled, not looking his way. She started scanning the area for their next move. 

He waited for her to continue.

“Anyways, I joined the Opindale military early. Fast-tracked because of my field time. Got good. Real good. But not smart. Took a chance on a mission, flanked an enemy group, and saved the squad, good. But I somehow ended up setting half the village on fire while doing it. Not on purpose.”

"I've met men who did less and lost more," he said. 

“Yeah. Got demoted. Not dishonorably. Just... not promoted ever again,” she said. 

“Then let me guess, along came Harmon, and he picked you after that?” he asked. 

“Something like that, he said something about wanting the wild ones. I said, ‘Good luck taming me. If you want to tame one, get a dog.’ He laughed. Then he told me about a new military squad he oversees. Told me my talent is too much to be wasted, or something along those lines. So yeah.”

Luca’s mouth twitched, not quite a smile. “Wild one, huh? Do you bite?”

“Maybe.” She gave him the side eye with a knowing smile on her face.

They stood, brushed off dust and frost, then turned their attention to the slope.

Luca knelt by the edge, pulled a small chisel and wedge from his pouch, and began scoring footholds into the rock wall where it dropped. “Just in case we have to come back this way fast,” he said.

Lysana began marking a stable path across the slope, drawing an alternate line with a chalk line where footing was better as well. “Next time we don’t roll down a bleeding mountain,” she muttered.

“We’re improving already,” Luca said. 

The tunnel deepened with every step. Not just in slope, but in stillness.

The walls closed in again, narrowing to just wide enough for them to pass single file. Old beams jutted from the rock like ribs from a long-dead thing; some snapped in half, others bowed but were intact. Their light barely reached the ceiling now. The silence wasn’t comforting. It was waiting.

Lysana walked ahead, lantern up, watching for frost-slick or cracked stone. She didn’t speak until they reached a slow curve in the path, where the air changed subtly warmer, subtly drier. The signs of recent airflow were gone.

“You’ve carried more than most,” she said, eyes forward. “With everything that happened. The sister. The mission. The... Night Veil thing. Do you have any good memories?”

Luca didn’t answer at first. His boots crunched on the stone behind her, slow and even.

Then he said, “Do you?”

She smirked faintly. “I asked first.”

Luca exhaled, not a sigh, just breath. Then he spoke.

“There was one contract. Years ago. Remote town, far north, near the woodline. That town is no longer standing. Man had filed a land claim, old blood inheritance, only it wasn’t his. He’d killed the family, buried them behind the chapel, and kept the daughter alive.”

Lysana’s shoulders stiffened, but she kept moving.

“She was nine. Maybe ten. I found her in a cellar, chained to a table. He had been using her. For... you know.” 

“I know.”

“Gabriel heard her call for justice; he set the contract, and I went. I killed him before he touched her again. Slipped a blade into his throat while he was counting coins.”

A pause for both of them to catch their breath.

“She looked at me like she didn’t believe I was real. Like the nightmare hadn’t stopped. I broke her chains, gave her a coat, and lit the fireplace. She warmed up to me and wanted to hug me.”

He slowed. “I held up a hand to stop her. Then I stepped through the shadow behind her wall and left.”

Silence stretched out between them, but it wasn’t heavy. It carried something else now.

“That was a good day,” Luca said quietly.

Lysana glanced back, her face soft. “Damn,” she said. “If that was a good day, your bad days must really suck.” She smiled at him, and for once, it wasn’t guarded. “But… that’s cool. You were there for her.”

He didn’t reply, but his expression shifted, just slightly. Something uncoiled in his jaw.

Lysana turned back toward the path. “Alright, my turn.”

He raised an eyebrow.

“Training camp. Recon test. There’s this ridge on the west end of Opindale, cold as hell, jagged, no cover. They drop you with nothing but a flint and a half-frozen canteen and say, ‘Survive for two sun rises and return to the barracks. Oh, and do it without being seen.’ The instructors set a company-sized element to search for us, with hounds. Half of us washed out or failed during this drill. Even I thought I’d.”

She ran a hand along the wall as she passed it. “But I didn’t. I made it back, bruised, frostbitten, and limping, but I made it. Because Harmon taught me to think like the wind. Be quiet, be constant, and move when they look away.”

Luca nodded slowly. “That sounds like him.”

“He didn’t say good job,” she said. “He said, ‘Took you long enough.’ But he smiled when he thought I wasn’t looking,” she said. 

Ahead, the tunnel split, a fork, each path disappearing into separate throats of stone. Neither marked. No sound. Just waiting.

They paused. Lysana pointed. “Right.”

Luca said, “Right.”

They glanced at each other. “You always fall in step with me now?” she asked, with a bit of edge.

“No, I just figured you’d take the wrong one first,” he shot back. 

“Uh-huh,” was her reply. 

They both smiled. Then, to her surprise, he laughed. A low, warm sound, brief, but real. It echoed off the stone like something unexpected but needed. “That’s the first time I’ve heard you laugh,” Lysana said.

“Don’t get used to it,” he said. 

“I won’t,” she replied. “But… it was nice.”

They headed right. Together.

The tunnel tilted again, steeper now, jagged in ways that made footing uncertain even with a good lantern. The smooth stone gave way to crumbled supports and fractured seams, as if the earth had once tried to swallow this passage and only half-finished the job.

They pressed forward carefully, navigating past broken stones and old tool racks rusted into the wall. Then the path narrowed sharply. The light from their lanterns revealed a vertical shaft ahead, ten feet across, slick and rimmed in frost, with a rotted wooden scaffold coiled around the inside like the skeleton of a staircase, or what was left of it. There were also metal rods along one wall leading to the top of the shaft. He tested one with a hard shake; it shifted in its socket. It too looked unstable, as it was missing wooden beams.

They entered the shaft, and there was a large platform, big enough for both of them to stand on with room to spare. Yet on the far end, away from where they entered and to their left was a pit. How far it dropped, they couldn’t tell as they looked into it, and the light from their lantern was swallowed by the darkness. 

Luca stepped to the edge of the structure, near the pit, raising his light. “We’ll have to climb it.”

Lysana studied the scaffold. Half the rungs were missing. The rest looked like they’d break under the weight. “There’s a ledge about halfway up,” she pointed. “Could be an old repair platform.”

Luca didn’t hesitate. He moved toward the base, testing the first rung. It held.

Second rung. Groaned.

Third rung—

Cracked.

The wood gave way without warning, splintering under his boot, and he fell towards the pit.

Lysana lunged, one knee braced on the lip of the shaft, the other leg extended for balance. Her hand shot out on instinct, catching his wrist as his weight tipped forward. His full weight wrenched her shoulder, but she held, teeth gritted, both of them dangling in the cold dark.

His breath hissed out as he dangled, one boot scraping the wall for purchase. He looked back, seeing only shadows.

“I’ve got you,” she grunted.

“I can climb—” he grunted.

“Not without both arms,” she said while heavy breathing. 

She hauled him up inch by inch until his free hand found a crossbeam and pulled. When they finally reached the ledge, they collapsed side by side, breathing hard, dust swirling in the lantern glow.

Luca turned his head, voice low. “Thanks.”

She looked him over. Glancing into his eyes, as his blue eyes pierced the darkness. Her face had gone somewhere else for just a moment, quick and unguarded, before she pulled it back. “Couldn’t risk losing our best climber,” she said lightly. She looked down, then back up at him with just her eyes and said, “Blue eyes are hard to replace.” Her breath still uneven, one brow lifting just slightly.

He blinked. “What?”

She leaned back, grinning. “You heard me.”

His mouth opened like he might respond, but no words came out. He looked away, suddenly very interested in the climb ahead.

Lysana raised an eyebrow. “What, no comeback?”

“I’m not used to… people saying things like that,” he said.

She softened just a little, then the ladder groaned above, and the moment passed. “You mean compliments?”

“No. Yes. I… I’m not used to that,” he clipped, tight breath. 

“Ever?” she asked quickly.

He shrugged. “People don’t talk to weapons. They only use them.”

That quieted her for a breath. Then she looked up, spotting a break in the wall across from the shaft.

“Hey, ladder,” she said, pointing.

A half-rotted wooden ladder led to a rounded archway above the ledge. Barely visible from below, the curve of stone above it bore an old city sigil.

Lysana tilted her head, then grinned. “You first. I’d rather someone be here if you fall again.”

Luca blinked. “What?”

“I said—” she stopped, grinning wider. “Nope. Too late. You heard me.”

He stared at her, red flickering faintly on his cheekbones from the lantern light.

“That’s not… I don’t… that’s not what I—” He shut his mouth.

Lysana laughed, real, unguarded, echoing down the shaft like a breath of air that hadn’t existed here until now. “Relax. I’ll go first. You look like you need to recalibrate your brain.”

He watched her climb for a moment, then shook his head and turned back to the wall. 

She called down as she reached the top. “Let’s leave the rope harness when we go. Make the return less… life-threatening.” 

Luca nodded, unfastening a coil of cord from his pack and quickly rigged it up to ensure they didn’t have to climb on that old, rotten scaffold.

They were still soldiers. On mission, he thought to himself. But for just a moment, they had shared something real, between the climb, the tease, and the firelight reflected in blue eyes.

The ladder groaned beneath his weight, but he made it to the ledge, where Lysana was waiting, leaning up against the walk, next to another ladder. Higher this time. 

“Your turn,” Lysana said, stepping aside.

He smiled and walked over to the ladder. This one was taller than the last. The ladder looked ancient, but climbable. Luca tested the lowest rungs with deliberate weight, then braced and pulled hard against the side rails. The wood groaned but held. Only then did he begin the ascent, slow and measured. She followed close enough to hear his breath, far enough not to crowd him.

“You’re taking this one slow,” she said, not unkindly.

“After last time?” he replied. “I’m allowed.”

“Fair,” she said. A pause. “I just didn’t expect you to be careful.”

He glanced back at her, surprised. “Is that a complaint?”

“No,” she said, climbing another rung. “Just an observation.”

He considered that, then smirked. “You observing me now?”

“Someone has to,” she said. “You look like you’d miss a step if you weren’t watched.”

“Or maybe I’d climb faster,” he said, “if I were.”

She laughed softly at that. “Don’t get ideas.”

The last few rungs ended at a circular hatch set into stone and rusted steel.

“Must be the way out,” Luca said.

Luca pressed upward with one hand, the other braced against the ladder’s final rung. Nothing. It wasn’t the weight, it was the resistance. Dirt and rubble pressed down from above. Something had collapsed onto it long ago, warping the hinges and sealing it with weight.

Again. He pushed harder, breath sharp through his nose. Just sifted down from the warped hinges, peppering his knuckles. It moved this time, but only a little, before it slammed back down.

“Let me try,” Lysana offered from below.

He didn’t move. “Just... one more.”

He shifted his weight, set his shoulder into the panel, and drove upward with a full-body shove.

The hatch gave.

It didn’t swing open. It buckled, then popped with a metallic shriek, sending loose brick and dust pouring into the shaft. Luca staggered back, catching himself on the ladder with one arm. For a heartbeat, nothing moved above but the settling haze of grit.

Luca poked his head out to check out their situation. Two figures appeared from the rubble beyond the hatch, half-hidden by drifting ash. They too looked down at his head protruding from the ground. They were soldiers, both armored, dirt-smeared, and pale. No one spoke.

“You must be the ones we’ve been sent to look for,” the soldier said, hand still on his weapon as his gaze lingered on Luca. “We been on a patrol pattern looking for you all day. Let's get you over to the chapel."

Luca reached up, grasped the arm, and was hauled the final stretch through the hatch. As he scrambled out into the ruin above, he turned and dropped to one knee, reaching back down.

“Your turn.”

Lysana’s hand met his with a clap of skin and leather. He pulled steadily and brought her up beside him. Cold air slapped up from the broken courtyard, sharp with smoke and winter rot.

They emerged into a broken courtyard. Once part of a rear district, now a cratered skeleton of fire-scorched stone. Arrows jutted from beams like iron thorns. The air reeked of ash, oil, and smoke still lingering from the last bombardment.

The two soldiers, one young, not new to war but shaken, nodded toward a half-standing chapel at the edge of the square. Lysana slowed a half-step, jaw tight, taking in the ruin with a controlled breath. Stained glass shattered. The roof partially collapsed. But its frame stood firm.

As they approached, the smell of sweat, old blood, and burnt wood thickened. Inside, the chapel had been transformed, pews turned on their sides to form makeshift walls and bedding. Blankets were bundled in corners. Small fires flickered in repurposed sconces, providing light without exposing the room to view from outside. 

Luca saw roughly forty civilians packed inside at first glance. Luca exhaled once, quietly, not relief, not disbelief, something in between. Eighteen were visibly injured. One man, pale, soaked in sweat, lay on a wooden board in the far corner, his lower half wrapped in blood-stained cloth, legs crushed and unmoving. A young girl sat beside him, holding his hand.

The room fell quiet as they entered.

A soldier stepped forward, broad, jaw tight, one shoulder plate slightly dented. Luca took him for a sergeant by the presence he projected. The way the others deferred confirmed it.

“You’re the ones from the arrow's message?”

“Yes. Name’s Roberts,” Luca said. The Sergeant extended his arm, and Luca met it. “Sergeant Rockwen,” he said. 

Luca then continued, “We cleared a path through the tunnels beneath you. We’ll bring supplies as soon as we can,” Luca said, looking around at what remained of their shelter. "You’ve held up well.”

Sergeant Rockwen gave a short, bitter laugh. “Not well. Just long.” He nodded toward the wounded. “Eighteen injuries. One critical. He won’t walk again. Might not wake up again.”

“I see,” Luca said, his voice following the tone of the moment. 

“And the rest?” Luca asked as his eyes swept the room. Children burned. Women with torn sleeves and split lips. Men bleeding through old bandages. Everyone was tired. Too tired to speak. Too tired to hope. Even those who weren’t bleeding carried bruises around their eyes, down their arms, across their ribs. Now that he was really looking, he realized every person in the room was injured to some level. 

“Forty-five civilians in total. That includes the eighteen badly wounded. The others are hurt but not seeking care. Conserving the little supplies we have left for those in need. We also have fifteen soldiers, who are still in the fight, including myself. Total of sixty survivors.”

Luca nodded his head as he took in more of the carnage. His stomach tightened at the weight of it, the quiet suffering pressing in on him harder than the cold stone walls. The Sergeant went on, “The rest of the town is either unaccounted for or…”

"Don't worry, Sergeant, you don't have to say it," Luca said. He had already done that math the moment he walked in the door. 

Sergeant Rockwen folded his arms. “How many are you?”

Luca met his eyes. “Four.”

“Four?” the man barked. “That’s all they sent?”

“No one knows. Your messages haven’t been getting through.” Luca explained calmly. A distant thump rolled through the stone overhead, faint but unmistakable, the siege breathing somewhere near them. Luca took the next few moments of quiet to fill in the Sergeant on the refugees who made it to Opindale and the other Orc camp they came across. 

Silence filled the air, letting Luca’s words sink in. Sergeant Rockwen stepped back. Not dismissive, just defeated. He ran a hand through his hair and exhaled.

“Why are you not in the fort?” asked Luca.

“Before we could get it open, the mechanism failed under the pressure. The bombardment shook the housing loose. There are rumors of sabotage. They’re trying to brace it, but nothing’s holding yet.”

“How are you communicating with them inside?” Lysana asked. 

“One of the lower archery holes, we talk with them there, it's mostly not in the line of sight or the line of fire at this moment.”

“Okay,” was all Luca said in response as he turned back to the room, working through his thoughts. “I swear to you, we will break this siege,” Luca said evenly. “We will get as many out as we can.” A lone ember drifted up from the guttering fire and winked out between them. 

Sergeant Rockwen crossed his arms. “Four of you?” he asked, knowing the answer. Then, after a measured pause, “If you have a plan worth hearing, you have my soldiers.”

No one cheered. No one spoke. But some sat a little straighter. Some breathed deeper. A few looked up, like maybe, just maybe, this wasn’t how it ended.

He stepped aside and sat on a broken pew that had been turned against the far wall, near a guttering fire.

Lysana followed, sliding down beside him without a word. For a long moment, they said nothing, just stared at the flickering light. The wind outside moaned through a cracked window frame, brushing cold air across their boots.

“This is bleak," she murmured, low enough for only him to hear.

“Yes, it is," he said in response, but more in reflex than giving her his attention. His eyes kept sweeping the area.

She continued to talk to him, but then noticed he was not paying attention. Then a bit louder, “What are you thinking?" 

“We need to help them. Why do any of this if not to?” he said quietly, but with resolve. 

"I agree,” were her only words.

"I just promised them, now we have to find a way, let's take a rest, then get back with the others and make a plan to save what is left of this village. So many others. Four of us. And Harmon sent me." A beat. "That math doesn't sit well.” 

“I won’t speak for everyone,” she said, meeting his eyes. “But I stand behind that mission. And I believe in you. Even if the others don’t… I do.” Lysana said. No waver in it. Not a word. 

They looked into each other's eyes, and no lies were present. Then she said softly, without looking at him, “I meant what I said, you know.”

He blinked.

“About your eyes, and you,” she added.

He exhaled through his nose. “Still not sure what to make of that.”

“You don’t have to say anything,” she said. “But… for what it’s worth, I see more in you than you let people see. What you told that sergeant, there is more to you. I am glad I am here with you.”

He turned to her slowly, expression unreadable.

She went on. “With what happened to make you like this, it didn’t take everything. You’ve still got something left. Maybe more than you know.”

There was a long silence. The fire popped once.

Finally, he said, “Since my sister died… There hasn’t been anyone. Not really.” He shifted slightly, shoulders tense, as if the words had weight. He looked down at his hands, flexed them like they weren’t his. “I’ve worked with people. Killed beside them. Slept in the same camps. But not close. Not… like how I am feeling now.”

He paused again. The bombardment rolled again overhead, dull and distant beneath the stone. He waited for it to pass before meeting her eyes, looked at her, just for a second, just long enough. “You’re the first person I’ve trusted this close since Eryel. I don’t intend to lose you. Which I don't understand, we’ve just really met.”

Another pause. His mouth twitched like he regretted the honesty, but he didn’t pull it back.

“Or to your eyes,” he added. Not a joke. Not quite. 

Lysana’s expression softened. She didn’t speak right away. Just reached out and took his hand. Not dramatic. Not performative. Just solid. Warm. Human. Her gaze was steady. Unflinching. “We don’t have to understand. We just survive,” she said. “Then we figure out the rest.”

He nodded. “First, we survive.”

He hesitated, but then curled his fingers around hers. They sat like that for a while, the fire crackling low between them. When the next distant strike trembled through the stone, Luca drew back first.

The chapel groaned faintly overhead, ancient timbers settling under the cold weight of the siege. The next bombardment hadn’t started yet, but it would.

It always did.
