The snow kept falling, thin and constant now, a cold veil draped over Stormhaven’s streets as midday deepened toward afternoon. It collected in the cracks between stones, softened the edges of rooftops, and muted even the sound of boots moving fast and quietly through slush.

This was Garr’s team. Not a squad. Not a formation. Just a collection of professionals used to working without needing to fill the silence with chatter. After the last span of days that passed, he wouldn’t want anyone else for this job. This was the strike team, the sharp edge of Harmon’s operation.
Garr led, sword sheathed, one hand resting near the hilt but fingers loose. He didn't need it yet, and until he did, it was dead weight. Dain moved close to him, loose-limbed in the cold, head shifting constantly to catch every alley corner and shopfront shadow. Nyssa ghosted one pace off Garr's left shoulder, eyes cataloging cover and approach angles like a second instinct. Behind them, Ash kept his pace even and quiet, a faint tension in his hands that never quite turned into fire, and Aldrek moved broad and slow beside him, mace at his hip, measuring every narrow choke point. Neavin and Aurelia held the rear as they always did, marked in both their minds as the last line if everything went wrong. 
Even now, Aurelia’s face was pale, her dark eyes distant, lips pressed thin. Whatever power she once carried felt… disconnected. Neavin kept close to her, his spirit sharp as ever, but worry was etched behind his eyes.
At last, they reached a cut in the alley, just shy of a small market square where carts sat half-abandoned, and snow was already swallowing the outlines of footprints.
And looming over it all, the Bastion of Steel.
It wasn’t a building, it was a message. Black stone, cold iron bands crossing its walls in jagged lines, murder holes high overhead that allowed no banners or beauty, no softness at all. This was a fortress first, a tower second.
Harmon caught up as they paused under a leaning stone wall, their breath steaming, their boots crunching softly in the deepening snow. He stood a long moment, taking in the sight, before turning to Garr.
“I’m not going with you,” he said quietly.
There was weight in that. No hesitation, just the hard acceptance of what needed to be done.
“I have to secure the withdrawal route,” Harmon continued. “Last time I stayed on mission like this…” He let that sentence hang for half a breath before Dain filled the space for him.
“Last time,” Dain said flatly, “you had to kill four men, and you nearly died yourself. We carried you unconscious for eighteen hours.” Dain’s words hit hard, but no one argued.
Harmon nodded slowly, that brief, tired smile cutting across his face again. “Exactly. I know the signs, I have learned.”
“Just be careful, that's all I am saying,” Dain said. 
Harmon simply said. “Thank you.”
He then turned to Neavin and Aurelia. “You two hold here. You’re the rear security. If this goes sideways, you’re our last eyes and last line.”
Neavin gave a short nod, already focusing inward. His falcon spirit, a sleek gray bird, circled tight over his head before perching on his gloved wrist, feathers flickering faintly as it waited.
Aurelia met Harmon’s gaze, her face pale but steady. She understood exactly what was being asked: they would not follow inside. They would stay, watch, and die if need be to hold the way clear.
Harmon clasped Garr’s shoulder, not needing to say more.
Then he stepped away, boots soft in the snow, disappearing into the narrowest gap between buildings with the quiet grace of a man who no longer needed words to say farewell.
For a moment, Garr just watched the gap in the wall. He'd been handed the sharp end of the knife, same as he always was. He was fine with that. "Eyes up," he said. "Weapons ready, but not drawn until needed." 

Nyssa gave a sharp nod. Dain smirked faintly but said nothing.

They approached the tower deliberately, using the broken line of market stalls for cover. The smell of damp wood and rust rose around them, but there was no sound except their boots crunching snow and the distant clatter of a loose shutter high above.

No matter what they already knew, Stormhaven itself still felt wrong. It wasn’t empty. People moved here and there, but beneath their steps, a stillness lingered, a strange, eerie hush that kept everything on edge.

The Bastion itself felt impenetrable. Its walls bore no obvious weakness. That was because the Bastion of Steel wasn’t simply a tower. It was a fortress compound, built for endurance and intimidation. An outer curtain wall surrounded it, rising nearly three stories high, reinforced with layered black stone and heavy iron bands. The wall had been designed to repel siege, every inch of its surface marked by craftsmanship meant for war, not beauty. Arrow slits pierced its face at perfect intervals, and walkways behind its crenellations gave defenders commanding fields of fire over every approach.

At the heart of the compound, the tower itself rose from the inner courtyard like a hammerhead spike driven into the sky, square-cut for much of its height, but narrowing slightly near the top where its open carousel sat like a watchful eye. Thirty-five floors climbed above the ground, and beneath it, the team knew from Harmon’s stolen plans that four full sub-basements were descending deep below street level, a labyrinthine depth carved into the bedrock of Stormhaven itself. Garr turned what he knew over in his mind. The plans had given them routes. Routes weren't the same as a way in. 

They took their time circling the tower. Each of them had their eyes scanning for cracks, some forgotten entrances. The diagrams retrieved gave them routes inside, nothing more. No hidden passages, no forgotten doors. 

Nyssa slipped forward to check a side passage, a drain channel too small for more than a child. She shook her head once. Garr tapped a finger on the nearest wall, iron-cold beneath his glove. There would be no easy way in. At the far western edge of the outer wall, Dain was looking around. He was with Nyssa. He then saw it. “Puzzle door ahead,” Dain said quietly, gesturing toward a heavy stone entrance recessed into the wall, its face covered in strange geometric patterns.

Nyssa was already moving and quickly crouched before a recessed iron panel almost invisible from even a few paces away. The dwarven lock was built flush into the stone, lacking a handle or visible keyhole, its surface etched with layered geometric patterns designed to conceal rather than invite. Its position was perfect: hidden beneath a shadowed buttress, partially obscured by weathering and snow, a detail most would overlook entirely.

This was not a door meant for attackers or visitors. It was an old supply entrance, a maintenance tunnel built by dwarven engineers long ago, a forgotten path Harmon had found only through recovered architectural plans. If solved, it would bypass the open courtyard entirely, leading directly into the lowest cellars, beneath guard posts and patrol routes.

Nyssa brushed her fingers over the cold iron face, eyes narrowing slightly as she traced one pattern after another. This wasn’t a brute-force puzzle; it was a craft puzzle, meant to test patience and precision, not strength. The grooves were tight, half-hidden under frost and grit, nested in a way that required memory and instinct both.

She brushed a gloved thumb over one groove. “Old dwarven work. This isn’t something we force; it’s something we solve. See here,” she said, pointing to a spot on the lock. “It is a Tri-key sequence. Partially stuck from rust, too.”

Dain knelt beside her, not crowding, watching her fingers move.

He leaned just slightly closer, voice pitched low. “Can you do this?”

Nyssa didn’t look at him right away. Her fingers continued tracing the cold metal, her mind working fast and focused.

Her reply came without emotion, steady as stone. “I believe I can. Just need to think and work.”

Dain nodded once. “Okay. I’ll give you time. I have your back while you work it.”

At that, Nyssa finally turned toward him. Her smile came broad, warm, and real.

Their eyes met for a beat longer than necessary.

“Thank you,” she said simply, and meant it.

Nothing else needed saying.

Ash paced nearby, fire at his fingertips but hidden still beneath a clenched fist.

Aldrek stood guard near the tower’s corner, watching the street. People were watching them now, casually, but too many. Heads turned when they passed, too coordinated, too quiet. Minutes slipped past.

Nyssa worked the latches, sweat already slick on her temple despite the cold. A soft click. One down. She exhaled slowly, shaking out her fingers.

Garr watched the exchange; he watched them all. And then, without a word, he gestured for Neavin to come over.

Neavin moved quickly to Garr's position. “What do you need?”

“Let's get a bird's eye view. Everything from the Tower to us. Approaches, the street we’re coming in on. I want to know what's moving before it reaches us. ”

Neavin nodded, then closed his eyes, head tilting back as the falcon spirit surged upward in a burst of shadow and wind, wings flashing as it climbed fast into the frozen gray sky.

Neavin’s body stiffened slightly, one hand raised as if balancing an unseen weight. His eyes fluttered, but did not quite close. He was “gone” now, his sight turned upward, watching through the falcon’s sharp eyes as it swept across the tower’s flanks.

He didn’t speak for long seconds. His breathing slowed, barely perceptible in the freezing air, and then his voice came low and even.

“Patrols are on rotations. About five minutes out. Tight. Disciplined.”

He turned his head slightly, adjusting for the falcon’s perspective.

“No gaps. Guard stacks at every main entrance, quad-stacked at the south and east gates.”

Another pause, longer this time. “North face. Three Red Cloaks. One Adept. He’s scanning the streets, slow and methodical. He hasn’t made us yet… but he’s close.”

Garr gave a single nod. “What about near us?” he asked. 

Neavin’s falcon circled once more, high and tight.

“Coming back around, now,” Neavin paused, then panic covered his face. “We have two… No, make that three coming right at us now from the west.” 

His breath hitched faintly as it spiraled back down, landing silently on his wrist and vanishing into shadow as it returned. He blinked, rubbing his eyes as if returning from somewhere far away.

Nyssa kept working on the puzzle lock, her breath came steadier now, fingers sliding from groove to groove with almost unconscious precision. She was at work. The tension of their situation didn’t disappear, but her mind knew exactly what to do.

The rest of the team prepared for the unwelcome visitors. They stood ready, and they waited. Then waited some more, and just when they thought the danger might have passed them. Garr then spotted movement at the far end of the alley. Two figures. Red cloaks peeling off from their pattern, walking casually but angling toward their position.

They weren’t alarmed yet. They didn’t know what they were seeing. But soon, they would. 

Nyssa worked faster, her fingers finding tiny latches hidden in the grooves, her breath clouding in front of her face. Dain shifted subtly, sliding one foot behind the other, casual but prepared. Ash stepped just slightly to the side, adjusting his coat, one hand brushing over his belt but keeping the flame hidden. Garr angled himself toward the wall, half-shadowed. The first Red Cloak’s gaze swept toward their corner. Their gazes locked on Garr, then flicked quickly to the team as a whole.

They didn’t hesitate. The first shouted a warning, raising a curved sword high, and sprinted forward. The second followed, boots hammering stone, face tight with fury.

“Contact,” Garr snapped.

Before his eyes could narrow, Nyssa was already moving, smooth, precise, silent.

A knife drawn from beneath her cloak slipped under his ribs as she closed the distance fast, her free hand bracing the back of his neck to ease him down as he died without a sound.

Dain stepped smoothly into the second’s path, one hand rising in what almost looked like a greeting, but the other swept low, rapier flashing out in a tight arc, catching the man just beneath the sternum.

A wet choke, a gasp, and then nothing.

The third Red Cloak hesitated. Almost too late.

Ash was already beside him, a step from shadow, a palm landing flat against his chest, not fire, not yet, but a twist of balance, a brutal shove that sent him staggering sideways right into Aldrek’s waiting arm.

Aldrek’s gloved hand clamped around the back of his neck, wrenching, quick, and efficient.

The third man dropped.

Three bodies down. No alarm raised. No shouts. No blood spilled on stone beyond what they could cover.

Nyssa went back to working on the lock, her fingers moving faster now, heart hammering in her chest. Another click. Almost there.

The snow whispered against stone, falling steadily as it coated the narrow streets. Even now, midday light was dim and watery, caught behind thick clouds that pressed low over Stormhaven. The air smelled of wet iron and old smoke.

Garr watched the people moving through the district, a scene that should have seemed alive but felt anything but. Everything Garr looked at told the same wrong story. Bread for sale, yet it had gone completely moldy, still arranged with care. Merchants fussing over goods nobody browsed in days. Cleaners sweeping stone that was already clean. The show must go on, Garr thought grimly. It was all performance. The only thing that ever broke the pattern was the Red Cloaks and the guards, whose patrols cut the false calm like blades.

His eyes narrowed as he scanned the Bastion of Steel’s shadowed base. No cracks. No gaps. Every surface was hard and cold. He turned toward Aurelia, where she stood near Neavin, her face pale, her brow furrowed.

“Aurelia,” Garr said, keeping his voice low but firm. “Can you reach out? Quietly. Not just a count, I want to know what’s inside. How many Red Cloaks, guards… what we’re walking into.”

Aurelia hesitated only a moment before nodding. “I can try,” she murmured.

She lowered her head and closed her eyes, two fingers brushing her temple. Her breathing slowed. The snow settled gently on her hood and shoulders.

Nothing happened. Then it did. She seized, hands flying to her head, knees hitting stone hard enough to crack the silence, and the sound that came from her was sudden. She gasped, sharp and ragged, and then she screamed.

She choked, wrenching sound tore from her throat as her hands clawed at her head, shaking violently.

The team spun toward her instantly. Neavin’s eyes went wide as he stepped in, instinctively reaching for her shoulder.

Aurelia’s mouth worked, but no words formed, only desperate, broken fragments.

She babbled nonsense at first: “The veils... the old stones... shadows beneath the stars... blood of the First... cracks in the firmament...” words that had no meaning, but chilled the air around them.

Her fingers dug into her scalp, and her face twisted in agony. She let out a scream that would haunt the dead.

Dain moved to Garr’s side. “Garr, what do we do?”

Before Garr could answer, Aurelia’s body locked. Her back straightened unnaturally, her head tilting up as if jerked by an invisible cord.

And then it stopped. All the screaming vanished as if cut by a blade.

She rose smoothly, too smoothly, until she stood upright, arms limp at her sides, her face blank.

When she opened her eyes, they were different, pupils blown wide, irises pale and clouded. And when she spoke, her voice was guttural, ancient, with an accent that didn’t quite belong. The words came slowly. Like they had all the time in the world.
"I let you flies pretend long enough. I let you scurry and scheme. You believed yourselves clever. You believed yourselves free. You were neither. Every step that brought you here was a step I permitted.”
Her eyes were open, but she wasn't behind them. 
It continued. You were seen before you arrived. You were marked before you chose your path. And now… now it is time to stop. Step forward, and you do not merely trespass. You volunteer. You offer yourselves for His design. I serve the Great Lord to come, and in His sight, you are not warriors or saviors… You are insects. Chaff to be gathered, broken, and remade. Leave now… or join the chorus that sings only His name, forever.”
Her head tilted slightly, birdlike, eerie. A silence heavier than stone fell across the street. It was broken not by sound, but by stillness. Every single person in the district froze where they stood.

A merchant was setting his wares. A woman folding linen on a windowsill. The boy kicked snow along the curb. Every head turned toward Garr’s team in eerie unison, slow, synchronized, unnatural. Dozens of eyes locked onto them.

The puppet show had paused. But it was still watching. Aurelia’s body shuddered once, and the pain returned. She gasped and staggered backward, nearly collapsing into Neavin’s arms before she bolted, running a few paces down the alley, breath ragged, hands still clutching her temples.

Neavin’s falcon, perched nearby, let out a shrill cry, its wings flaring.

Then Neavin himself swayed, closing his eyes as his connection to the falcon faltered for a heartbeat, unbalancing him. He recovered quickly, but his eyes found Aurelia first, before anything else, before the tower, before the team. 

Ash’s voice broke the heavy silence first, sharp and tense. “We should leave. Now. We’ve seen enough. The whole damned city is watching.”

He glanced toward Garr. “This isn’t just a tower anymore. This is a trap.”

The only sound was Nyssa's fingers on the lock.

Even Nyssa’s fingers froze at the puzzle-lock as doubt bled into her expression.

Brother Aldrek stepped forward beside Dain, one hand resting lightly on Dain’s shoulder. His voice was low, almost gentle, but steady.

“I know how it looks,” Aldrek said. “I know the fear. But duty doesn’t wait for comfort.”

"Duty!?!" Ash said flatly. "I'm here for coin and mischief. Not duty. But I'm not stupid either, and that thing just told us it's been watching since before we arrived." 

Aldrek's gaze swept the group. “We came here knowing this wouldn’t be easy, that it might cost us everything. And we come this far, only to walk away now?”
Dain nodded once, voice tight but clear. “If this were my family… I would want you to finish what you started. This isn’t about gold anymore. Or promises. There are innocent lives on the other side of that wall. Children’s lives. We volunteered for this, we finish it.”
Ash didn’t fold immediately. His jaw clenched, but he shook his head, voice sharp, coming from a place more of fear than defiance. “How do we even know what the hell that was?” he snapped. “That voice, maybe it’s just bait. A trap to pull us in.”
His breath came faster now, and his eyes darted to the civilians who still stood watching them, unmoving. “What if there’s something worse waiting inside? Something none of us are ready for?”
Somewhere behind them, one of the watching civilians took a slow step closer. Not running. Not threatening. Just closing the distance by one careful pace. 
Brother Aldrek stepped forward, and he placed a hand on Ash’s shoulder.  His voice came low and steady, but with a weight that came from a lifetime of service.
“I have stood before darker thresholds than this,” he said, meeting Ash’s gaze without hesitation. “Fear is no reason to turn back, not when there are lives that depend on us walking forward. Whatever waits inside, we face it. That’s what we should do. It is what is right.”
Ash hesitated, eyes flicking from Aldrek to Dain, then to Nyssa, who hadn’t stopped working at the lock despite the rising tension. Ash exhaled slowly, nodded once. “Alright,” he said, his voice low but resolved. “Alright.”
Nyssa finally spoke, her voice soft but resolute. “What about Aurelia?”

Neavin straightened fully now, nodding quickly. “I’ll stay with her. I’ll put the falcon on her. She won’t be lost.”

Garr’s nod came fast, without hesitation. “Good. Then you two hold here. Rear security.”

His gaze swept over Ash, Nyssa, Dain, and Aldrek.

“You four, forward. We finish what we came for.”

Garr’s voice was low but carried an authority that cut through the tension. He looked at each of them in turn, his breath misting in the cold air.

“We’re not just getting in, we’re going to have to get out. With no means to communicate. We will have no way to know what is happening outside this tower while we’re inside it… so anyone who goes through that door is responsible for finding the way back out.”

His gaze settled briefly on Nyssa as she worked the dwarven lock, then on Ash, Dain, and Aldrek.

“I need all of you thinking about the exit, every step of the way. If this is our path in, this might not be the path out. We are very likely to have to improvise the exit. ”

He let the words settle. “Once we breach, we move as one. Quiet, careful. If we get separated… we don’t stop. We keep going. We keep pushing until we reach that family.” He met each of their eyes again. “No mistakes.”

Nyssa returned to the lock, chewing her lip. Her thoughts flicked like sparks: It’s all on you. The mission. The family. No pressure, right? 

Nyssa’s gloved fingers trembled, not from fear, but from the biting cold that was seeping deeper as she worked the dwarven puzzle lock. She could feel the pressure of the team behind her: waiting, watching, every second stretching longer than the one before.

Her breath clouded the metal face of the lock, a sheen of condensation leaving dark smudges where she leaned close. The grooves and nested slots were complex, more intricate than even she had anticipated, layers buried under layers, meant not just to guard but to frustrate.

She’d almost solved it twice already. Almost.

But the last catch eluded her.

Ash leaned close behind her, his breath tight. “What’s the hold-up?”

Nyssa didn’t answer immediately. She squinted, brushing a line of frost from the lowest groove. And then she saw it, not what she’d missed but how she’d misunderstood it.

A false groove, a decoy etched into the pattern, drawing the solver’s eye away from the true sequence.

She exhaled slowly, muttering to herself, “Crafty old bastards…”

Her fingers moved fast now, resetting the sequence carefully. This time it felt right, a subtle click as she twisted the final node into alignment.

The lock shifted under her palm with a muted thud.

A seam appeared.

A deep grinding noise rumbled from behind the wall, old gears turning, dust falling from stone.

The door eased inward.

Cold, stale air rushed out, thicker and damper than the street air, carrying the smell of deep earth, iron, and ancient stone.

Ash gave a sharp nod. “Finally.”

But Nyssa didn’t rise right away. Her eyes narrowed.

“Wait,” she said. “Something’s wrong.”

The team stepped closer and looked past her shoulder.

Inside was a narrow stone passage, but after only ten feet, it ended abruptly in a wall of collapsed stone and twisted metal supports. Damp earth spilled through the cracks. Blackened timbers jutted at odd angles, some scorched, the ceiling beyond long since given way. 

“Damn it,” Dain muttered, pulling back. “It’s dead.”

Nyssa pressed a hand to the edge of the opening, leaning in for a better look. “This should have gone under the courtyard straight to the cellars… but it’s gone. Fully collapsed.”

Ash swore under his breath, his face tight with frustration. For a moment, no one spoke. Aldrek crouched beside the entrance, peering into the rubble. He ran gloved fingers along one side, brushing aside dust. “This collapse isn’t new,” he said quietly. “Years old.” The realization sank in. The plans had been accurate at the time they were drawn. But this path had been cut off long before their mission.

Nyssa leaned back, breath sharp in her throat. “We’re back to square one,” she said. But then she stopped, fingers brushing an uneven line in the stone just inside the doorway, a small indentation worn smooth with age." 

Something tucked into the alcove, a heavy iron key, looped through a corroded ring, hidden where no one would casually find it.

She pulled it free, holding it up.

“What’s this?” Ash asked, leaning forward.

“Old,” Nyssa said. “Dwarven make.”

Ash spoke first, voice tense. “Could be a backup. A key for something inside.”

Nyssa nodded. “It’s worth holding onto.”

Nyssa held up the ancient dwarven key, its weight reassuring in her hand, but something about the architecture felt incomplete. She stepped back, scanning the wall carefully.

She walked back in with a feeling that she had missed something. She began examining the walls with perception, running her eyes over every inch. Something near the base of the wall caught her eye. She crouched low again, running her gloved fingers along the wall just inside the ruined threshold. There, a faint seam in the stone, almost invisible, but precise.

“Aldrek,” she called quietly. “Look at this.”

Aldrek knelt beside her, breath misting. “There’s something behind this. Old stonework, almost ceremonial. But sealed.”

Nyssa tested the key in her hand. Its teeth didn’t match any visible keyhole until she angled it sideways and noticed a shallow socket hidden at the base of the seam.

She slid the key in, turned.

A soft metallic click sounded deep within the wall.

The seam split open, a narrow compartment sliding aside to reveal a recessed chamber no larger than a cabinet.

Inside lay another key, but this one shorter, thicker, clearly meant for a different lock.

And beside it, etched into the stone back panel, a dwarven sign:
"Where stone falls and steel rises, trust four hands to hold weight."

Nyssa frowned, translating aloud for the others.

“What does it mean?” Dain asked, stepping forward.

Ash pointed toward the floor. “Look at this.”

A ring of faint, circular grooves radiated out from a section of stone at the center of the chamber floor, hidden by dust until now.

This wasn’t a dead-end.

Nyssa stepped back slowly, her gaze sweeping the chamber again. The rubble that blocked the tunnel wasn’t random, and this small room, this apparent dead end, felt far too deliberate.

Her eyes narrowed as she ran her palm along the nearest wall, fingers brushing over fine seams and subtle chisel marks almost lost to time.

“Here,” she whispered. “This space… It’s hiding something.”

As she pressed gently at a faint depression, the wall groaned inward, a section sliding aside with a low grind.

Beyond was a narrow chamber, no larger than a storage closet, and inside it: four squat stone columns arranged in a perfect square, each rising from the floor and disappearing into the ceiling. Carved dwarven runes spiraled down each column.

Above them, etched into the stone lintel, a single line in dwarven script. "When one stands alone, skill serves; when many stand together, trust prevails." Ash read it and said nothing, which was unusual enough that Garr noticed. 

Aldrek stepped forward, eyes narrowing as he read. “It’s a counterweight system,” he said quietly. “Each column controls part of the locking mechanism for the door.”

Dain ran a hand along one column’s base. “They rotate,” he noted.

Nyssa tested another, feeling how it gave slightly under careful pressure. “But they’re interlinked,” she said. “When you adjust one, it changes the others… Look, watch this.”

She gave her column a careful turn, a soft metallic groan followed, but then one of the other columns shifted slightly back in response.

Ash grimaced. “So this is a balance puzzle.”

Aldrek nodded. “It’s meant to challenge a lone dwarf. One person could do this, but it would take patience and finesse.”

He ran a finger along the edge of a groove at the base of his column. “But with four? We can feel the adjustments together. Make it faster.”

Dain straightened, flicking a bit of dust from his coat. “Alright, let’s get moving, we’re burning daylight.”

He stepped forward first, brushing his fingers over the nearest column’s edge, feeling the grooves worn smooth by centuries. He tested a careful turn, just a fraction.

The column shifted beneath his hand, a low metallic groan echoing from deep within the walls. But as he moved, Ash’s column shifted in response, drifting just out of alignment.

Ash grunted. “That moved mine.”

Aldrek tested his own, adjusting carefully, feeling how it resisted at first and then yielded, but as he did, Dain’s column tipped the other way.

Nyssa drew back slightly. “They’re all connected.”

“Of course they are,” Dain muttered, crouching low to peer at the base of his column. “Trust the dwarves to turn a door into a test of patience.”

Ash shook his head, rolling his shoulders. “Alright. Let’s try again. Slowly.”

They worked together, coordinating their movements.

Nyssa guided first, adjusting her column gently.

Ash followed her lead, but too quickly this time. His column slipped, and a sharp metallic snap rang out. The mechanism reset with a loud clunk.

Ash swore under his breath, hands tightening into fists. “Damn it… Sorry.”

Aldrek placed a steadying hand on his shoulder. “It’s not about speed. Feel it. Let the stone tell you.”

Ash exhaled slowly. “Yeah. Okay.”

They began to move in smaller increments, each one calling out their adjustments.

“Quarter turn here,” Nyssa murmured.

“Hold,” Aldrek said, pausing to feel the counterweight shift.

“Little more on mine,” Dain added, turning carefully, his gloved hands precise.

Ash braced himself, easing his column deliberately.

But again, the mechanism resisted. Somewhere inside the wall, the weights pulled against each other. A metallic groan filled the chamber, but not the right kind.

Then, a jarring clack as it locked once more. They froze. A moment of shared frustration passed between them, cold sweat running down Nyssa’s temple despite the freezing air.

Ash raked a hand through his hair, muttering, “This thing’s rigged.”

Dain crouched again, this time pressing an ear against the stone near his column. “It’s not cursed, it’s built to break your patience.”

Aldrek allowed himself the faintest smile at that. “It’s dwarven work. It wants us to think together.”

They stood for a moment, hands off the columns, not speaking. The cold pressed in. Somewhere outside, boots passed on stone, unhurried. Then, without a word, they returned to their positions. 

This time, no one rushed. They felt for every shift, every vibration in the stone. Nyssa made the smallest adjustment yet, a whisper of movement.

Ash steadied himself, waiting for Nyssa’s next cue before adjusting in turn. Aldrek watched both of them carefully before applying pressure to his column, slow and even. Dain handled his with exquisite balance, moving fractionally to offset the others.

They began to breathe together now, falling into an unspoken rhythm. The columns moved, this time with a deeper, smoother groan, the sound more harmonious.

Nyssa glanced around. “We’re holding it… Keep going.”

Ash smiled tightly. “This feels better.”

Aldrek nodded slowly, face set with focus. Dain called out gently, “A hair more here… just a hair…”

And then... A deep thud reverberated through the stone floor. The columns locked into place. A hidden section of the floor beneath them shuddered. From somewhere deeper in the stone, Nyssa thought she heard a note, like a deep chime, resonating. As if the old dwarven hall had been waiting.

The wall rumbled beneath their feet. Dust cascaded down from seams overhead as a hidden section of stone slid aside, revealing a descending stairwell. Narrow. Dark. Cold.

Nothing came from below. No light. No sound. Just cold air pressing upward like a hand against the chest. Ash exhaled sharply. "Damn. That felt earned."
Dain smiled faintly, wiping sweat from his brow despite the cold. "Let's hope it's not the first of many."
