The clearing sat like a held breath in the woods. Nothing had touched it in a long time. No footprints broke the snow’s thin crust. The old fire pit was nothing but a shallow depression rimmed with frost, its stones half-swallowed by moss and years of fallen needles. Wind hissed through the bare crowns of nearby maples, carrying the scent of old pine and the faint metallic tang of frozen earth.

Riven reined in first, his horse’s hooves crunching in the stillness. He didn’t dismount right away. The place had the wrong kind of quiet, not peaceful, but the watchful kind, like the forest itself was deciding whether to keep its presence a secret.

Reau rode in a slow half-circle behind him, scanning the edges where shadow pooled deep under the trees. “Looks dead,” he said, though his voice was pitched low, as if speaking too loud might stir something best left sleeping.

“Dead doesn’t mean empty,” Riven answered.

They moved through the clearing with the deliberate patience of men who’d lived this routine too many times, checking where the treeline dipped, where a half-rotted log might hide the curve of a crossbow limb. Riven knelt by the pit, scraping away a layer of frost with the edge of his glove. The black ash beneath was powder-dry, brittle between his fingers.

“Months,” he said. “Maybe longer.”

Reau eyed the treeline again before swinging down from his saddle. “No one here, then. Or no one who wanted to be found.”

“Same difference.”

Only then did Reau pull a twist of pine-scented kindling from his saddlebag. The flint strike cracked sharply in the cold air, and a few sparks caught in the resin-rich wood. The first curl of smoke rose thin and pale, then thickened into a gray ribbon that wound up through the branches, a signal meant for distant eyes.

“Fire’s our flag,” Riven muttered. “If anyone’s watching, we just told them where to look.”

“Then we’ll deal with it,” Reau said, though the words carried the weight of a man already making contingencies.

They kept their saddles on the horses, loosening the girths but leaving everything else ready to move. Neither spoke much. The clearing, for all its emptiness, still felt like a place with a memory, and neither of them trusted memories they couldn’t read.


It was Harmon’s group that broke the quiet an hour later, the crunch of wheels in frozen ruts, the steady beat of hooves. Harmon drove the lead cart, Neavin beside him. In the back, Aldrek lay unconscious under a thick blanket, his face pale, lips parted in the slow rhythm of deep, spent sleep. Aurelia sat near his feet, hood drawn, one hand pressed against her temple as if the cold itself had teeth. Weslin rode a short distance behind, scanning the treeline like the shadows might follow them.

Harmon reined in near the fire and took in the camp with a quick, measuring look. “Clear?”

“For now,” Riven said. “We walked the perimeter. No tracks in the snow but ours.”

Neavin helped Aurelia down, steadying her as her boots hit the snow. She leaned on him a moment longer than she meant to, jaw tight. The further from Stormhaven they’d come, the lighter the pressure in her skull had grown, but it still pulsed, sharp, sudden flashes of noise and heat bleeding through her thoughts: the roar of the crowd, the burn of fear in the air, the hollow tolling of the bell.

Harmon climbed into the back of the cart, kneeling beside Aldrek. He placed his ringed hand against the unconscious man’s chest; the firelight caught the band’s engraved edges, and a low vibration seemed to pass into the air. A faint glow sank into Aldrek’s armor, subtle as warmth bleeding into frozen metal.

“That’s all I can give him,” Harmon said when he climbed down. “Any more, I’ll be joining him in the back.”

They set to work without being told. Neavin dug through supplies, passing a small pot to Reau to fill with snow for melting. The first hiss of water over the fire joined the snapping wood. Harmon moved among the group, checking small injuries, a split knuckle here, a shallow slice there, cleaning and binding with salve that stung sharply but was clean.

Riven prowled the perimeter again, the tip of his boot breaking frozen moss as he passed, while Reau scraped ice from the stones around the fire pit. The smell of pine resin thickened as more wood caught, and the clearing began to feel less like a waiting place and more like a temporary home.

The second cart announced itself with voices, Nyssa’s low and wry, Garr’s deeper rumble answering. She rode in first, her horse’s breath ghosting in the cold. Ash followed a few paces behind, the lines of fatigue carved deep into his face. Garr drove the cart, and the governor’s family huddled under blankets in the back with Tarlon. Tarlon had one hand on the cart rail and his eyes on the woods. He hadn't stopped watching since they left the city. All their eyes widened at the sight of the smoke curling above the trees.

Dain rose from where he’d been crouched near the fire. He stepped toward her without thinking, catching himself halfway and shifting his weight as he’d only meant to check her tack.

“You made good time,” he said.

Nyssa arched a brow. “Were you worried?”

“Who would I be if I didn’t?” he said with a half-smile. “But the place looks better with you in it.”

Her smirk softened into something warmer, eyes catching his like a hook. “Careful. Keep talking like that and people will think you actually missed me.”

Dain didn’t step aside right away. He let the words hang, a flicker of challenge in his gaze. “What if I did?”

That one landed. She blinked, just once, but it was enough for him to see the shift. “Then you’d be admitting more than you mean to,” she said, voice quieter now.

“Maybe I mean to,” he countered, lips curving into a slow grin.

For the first time in days, she didn’t deflect. Instead, she closed the distance, her shoulder brushing his as she passed. Then, just as he started to turn, her voice came low, close enough for the warmth of her breath to touch his ear. “Then maybe I’ll stop making you work so hard for it.”

It froze him for half a beat, long enough for her to be three steps past before his smile returned, sharper this time, the kind a man wears when he’s just been given something worth chasing.

From where he stood, Riven’s glance toward them was short, but with a smile.

Someone got the pot on the fire. Snow in first, then the meat and onions, hitting the water with a hiss that the clearing hadn't heard in months. The smell was rich with fat and spice, cutting through the cold. Bread was torn into rough halves and passed around. The rescued family ate quietly, murmuring thanks.

Aurelia sat a little apart, blanket pulled tight around her shoulders. Each pulse of her headache seemed to match the flicker of the fire, dragging with it another sliver of Stormhaven, a street she hadn’t walked in years, the echo of shouted orders, the faintest brush of another’s thoughts like fingers against glass. Neavin crouched beside her, speaking low until the tension in her jaw eased.

Somewhere beneath the noise and the faces and the heat, there was an architecture to it. A shape. Something had been built over Stormhaven, and it was still standing, and she could still feel the edges of it from here.

The fire popped, throwing an ember into the snow, where it hissed and vanished. Around them, the clearing had settled into a quiet that wasn’t quite peace, more the brittle pause between storms. Garr knelt beside the family’s youngest, showing the boy how to hold a strip of bread over the fire without burning it. Ash sat a little apart, polishing soot from the metal buckles on his coat, his gaze slipping now and then to the tree line, the way a man watches when he's already decided something is out there.

Weslin hunched over near the horses, rubbing them down with slow, methodical strokes, his breath fogging in the cold. He moved down the line with a patience that had nothing of exhaustion in it, even though he hadn't slept any more than the rest of them. The horses were calmer when he was near them than they were at any other time. No one had mentioned it.

Dain and Nyssa were close enough to share the same patch of firelight, though not quite close enough to touch. She was sharpening a dagger, the rhythmic scrape of steel on stone oddly soothing in the stillness; he was pretending to watch the flames, though his eyes kept drifting her way. Riven prowled the perimeter again, a shadow among shadows, his boots whispering over the crusted snow.

The smell of stew thickened as the last of the onions and dried meat surrendered to the heat, steam carrying it into every breath. Bellies rumbled, a sound almost comical against the hush, and when Garr finally ladled the meal into rough wooden bowls, no one needed telling twice.

They ate in small clusters, conversation low and scattered. The family kept to themselves, their murmured thanks barely above whispers. Those not on watch claimed whatever warmth they could.

It was the kind of rest that comes between things rather than after them. And when the bowls were scraped clean and the pot set aside, the camp began its slow shift into the long watch of the day.

After they’d eaten, Riven sat cross-legged near the fire, pulling a worn leather pouch from his belt. He loosened the drawstring and poured its contents into his palm, a scatter of items that caught the light in dull glints: a brass-capped quill, a small glass vial stoppered with wax, two signet rings, and, last, a dull, heavy medallion on a cracked leather cord, its surface marked with curling, unfamiliar sigils.

Nyssa passed behind him, eyeing the collection. “You always loot with such… variety?”

Riven smirked faintly. “Took what looked worth the space… and a few things I just liked.” He turned the medallion between his fingers, the firelight sliding over the engravings. “This one? No idea. But I didn’t feel like leaving it behind for Merrick.”

She stopped, crouched beside him. “Mind if I…?”

He tossed it over without ceremony, and she caught it in one hand. It was cold against her palm. A faint, steady vibration hummed through her fingers.

Nyssa’s brow furrowed. “It’s… vibrating. Did you feel that?”

Riven nodded. “Yeah. Didn’t think anything of it. Figured it was a ward or some old charm.”

She turned the medallion over, eyes narrowing at the etched sigils. “No… this feels familiar. Back in the tower, we ran into a frequency lock, the vault doors. It felt just like this.” Her gaze cut to Reau. “Like your collar.”

“Did you try it on it?” she asked. Reau’s head came up.

“No,” Riven said. “It doesn’t look like a key.”

“It could be,” Nyssa countered. “If the frequencies match, that’s all it needs. Every lock’s different.”

They exchanged a brief look, the kind that carried risk and possibility in equal measure, and then moved to try it.

“You think that thing’s going to work on this?” Reau asked, fingers brushing the iron band at his throat. Frost had gathered along the seam where metal met skin.

“Only one way to know,” Nyssa replied.

He hesitated, then shifted to one knee, lowering his head to give her access. She pressed the medallion's edge to the collar's seam. The vibration deepened instantly, running through all three of them the same way, at the same instant, like a lock recognizing a key. The sigils on the medallion flared, not blinding, but a molten, ember-glow that sank into the metal.

A muted click broke the stillness. The collar loosened, slid down over his shoulders, and fell into the snow with a solid, ugly clink.

Reau’s first breath was almost a sigh, his shoulders easing as though a weight had been peeled away from his bones. His eyes half-closed, lips parting slightly, the magic sliding back into him like the handclasp of an old friend, warm, familiar, welcome.

But welcome turned to invasion.

The warmth swelled to heat; the steady seep became a river, then a torrent. It hit him like a dam breaking, shoving past every barrier his body had grown to protect itself. His breath caught. Muscles twitched. His fingers curled hard against his knees.

“Too fast,” he ground out, voice tightening to a rasp. “Gods—”

The next surge drove through him like hammer-blows, filling every nerve, crowding every thought. His spine arched. A blue light bloomed behind his eyes. The sound dropped to a muffled roar like rushing water under ice. The air around him almost seemed to shiver.

Color drained from his face. Sweat slicked his skin despite the cold. Then his stomach revolted. He doubled over, retching hard into the snow, the steam curling upward in thin, ghostly threads.

From the cart, the family watched. The mother pressed her youngest against her side. None of them spoke. They had learned in Stormhaven not to ask questions about things they didn't understand.

Nyssa was already bracing his shoulder. “Easy.”

“It’s…” His voice broke; he swallowed against another wave. “It’s like drowning… in my own blood.”

“Breathe,” she said sharply, steadying him with one hand and the medallion still clutched in the other.

Riven moved in without a word, catching Reau under the other arm. Between them, they got him to the cart, his boots dragging ruts in the snow. By the time they eased him into the bed of it, his breathing had slowed, but his gaze was unfocused, glassy, every muscle slack as if the magic still churned inside him, refusing to settle.

Without opening his eyes, Reau’s mouth twitched faintly. “Might not want to… for the next few hours,” he murmured, “but I’ll live.”

From the fire’s edge, Harmon had been watching. He didn’t move to interfere, but his jaw was set, his expression caught between disapproval and calculation. Merrick’s men had been the ones to clamp that iron on Reau’s neck, but it was what Reau had done to himself before that, the lines he’d crossed and the risks he’d taken, that put the weight in Harmon’s stare. Such choices carried a shadow. Some called it taboo. Others simply called it reckless. He didn't like it. He'd had this argument with himself before, and it never ended differently.

His gaze lingered on the boy longer than it should have. A faint tightening at the corner of his eyes. The smallest shift in his mouth, almost a smile. For a heartbeat, it was the same look he used to give during those early training days, when Reau was still raw and eager, before ambition and desperation had carved sharper edges into him.

The affection was still there, stubborn and immovable beneath the years and the mistakes.

Reau had been seventeen the first time he'd burned himself reaching past his limit. Harmon had been the one who'd found him and said nothing, just wrapped his hand and waited for the questions to start. They always started. He'd answered what he could and lied about the rest, which was the same thing every teacher did with a student worth keeping.

Harmon's eyes narrowed again, the softness shuttered away. He shook his head once, a quick, private motion, and settled apart from the rest, journal balanced on one knee, quill back in hand. Every few lines, his gaze lifted toward the treeline, long enough to weigh the shadows between the trunks, then back down to the page.

The sharp crack of a branch split the quiet. Not a twig’s casual snap, but the brittle, splintering sound of something heavier underfoot. Harmon’s head came up, pen stilled mid-word.

“That’s close,” Riven said from his post, eyes narrowing toward the sound.

Harmon closed the journal without a sound, tucking the quill into its spine. “We’ve rested enough,” he said, voice low but carrying. “Pack it up.”

Movement rippled through the camp, blankets shaken and rolled, buckets upended, the pot lifted from the fire with a hiss of steam. Boots thudded against the frozen ground. The clearing pressed in around them.

Another sound came then, a faint shift of snow, like something moving parallel to the camp but not yet pressing in. Garr crossed the distance to Harmon in a few long strides, Neavin following. The mental haze of his spirit-sight wasn’t in his eyes now, but his expression held the same watchful focus.

Garr kept his voice just above a whisper. “Sir, half our people are in the back of those carts, and the family's scared enough to bolt. A stand-up fight right now is a way to lose what we kept.”

Harmon’s gaze swept the camp, Aldrek still limp in the cart, Aurelia with her head lowered against a blanket, Reau pale under another. He took the measure in silence, then gave a sharp nod.

He raised a hand and caught Dain’s eye, curling two fingers to bring him over. Dain stepped in quickly, already reading the set of Harmon’s jaw.

“Pick two,” Harmon told him, voice pitched low. “You ride out now, find the other team. They started their part from the port city, heading there first.” Harmon handed him a map of the area with the city circled. “You have two days. If nothing, get on a boat and head home.”

Dain’s brow furrowed. “And you?”

“The rest of us go south,” Harmon said. “We’ll keep to the back roads, away from the river and towns unless we need supplies. Three, maybe four days if the weather holds. Right now, we don’t know who’s who, or whose side they’re on.” He fixed Dain with a steady look. “I don’t care how long it takes you to find them. Don’t come looking for us. I won’t have another team lost chasing ghosts. You get to a boat, get home.” He held Dain’s gaze until the meaning was clear. “We’ll all meet back at the stable.”

Dain's answer was immediate. “Clear.” Riven, who had closed the distance without anyone noticing, echoed it. Nyssa had edged in as well, listening.

Dain turned, all business now. “Nyssa, you’re with me.”

Neither of them acknowledged what had passed between them by the fire. That was its own kind of acknowledgment. Riven’s voice cut in before she could reply. “Let me go with you.”

Dain measured him for half a breath, then nodded. “Good. We ride in five. Grab what you need.”

The camp moved with purpose. Saddles tightened. Bedrolls cinched. Reau’s horse was led forward, its tack shifted to Garr’s cart. Weslin unhitched his mount and fastened it to Harmon’s wagon, climbing up into the driver’s seat so Garr could ride bow-ready.

Harmon took one last glance toward the treeline, listening. The forest’s sounds had changed. The wind was still there, whispering in the high branches, but beneath it came a layered murmur, the faint crunch of snow that wasn’t theirs, the slow drag of weight through frost-stiff grass. Whoever was out there knew enough not to charge, but not enough to stay silent.

A cold draft curled through the clearing, lifting the last smoke from the fire pit. The smell of pine and damp ash hung heavy. Neavin’s head tilted slightly toward the dark line of the woods. “They’re moving with the wind,” he murmured.

Harmon looked at the carts. The wounded. All of them. Ash was pale, the hollows around his eyes deep with it. Aurelia had her head in her hands, crying. She had been since the city. She didn't know what was real anymore and what was bleeding in from Stormhaven. Aldrek hadn’t stirred since they left the city. The rescued family huddled together in Garr’s cart, their eyes darting toward every shadow.

Somewhere in the distance, a bird cried, a sharp, abrupt sound that cut off too cleanly, like it had been interrupted.

Harmon didn’t hesitate. “Ride! Now.”

The group split without another word, snow crunching under hooves and cart wheels as they pulled away from the clearing. In moments, the fire was a dark patch of trampled ash, and the trees closed in behind them.

The woods seemed to lean closer as they rolled forward, the tree trunks swallowing what was left of the clearing. For a moment, Harmon thought he caught a flicker of movement. Two dark shapes, not enough to identify, but enough to know they’d been right to leave.

Dain didn’t look back. The road northwest was little more than a frost-hardened track, winding between the dark flanks of the pines. He kept his mount, an iron-gray gelding, just ahead of the others, the cold air burning his lungs as he breathed deep. Behind him, Nyssa rode with her hood up, amber eyes scanning the brush with that restless thief’s gaze, and Riven followed close, one gloved hand resting on the pommel of his saddle dagger.

They’d left Harmon’s carts less than an hour ago. Dain hadn't looked back, but the distance was there. South was safe for the wounded and the family. Safety didn’t find lost comrades. This northwest route was a gamble, and he felt it with every crunch of hooves on frozen dirt.

A jay screeched somewhere to their left, the sound sharp enough to turn all three heads. Nyssa’s gaze narrowed.

“Company,” Nyssa murmured. They slowed to a cautious trot, the forest closing in until the track dipped into a shallow hollow. That was when they saw them. The forest here felt older, heavier, the kind of place where sound went to die.

For a time, it seemed they’d slipped the danger trailing them.

Suddenly, just ahead of them, four shapes broke from the treeline, scaled, long-limbed, eyes like burnished copper. Lizardmen. Broad shoulders hunched under mismatched bits of scavenged armor. Each of them was armed with something mean. Their weapons ranged from a rust-pocked cleaver to a short, brutal sword. Long tails lashed behind them, shifting snow in slow arcs.

“They’re blocking the trail,” Riven said, voice tightening.

Dain’s hand went to his hilt. “Then we clear it.”

They dismounted in unison, boots sinking deep into the cold powder. The Lizardmen spread to match them, tongues flicking the air in slow, tasting movements.

The spear came first, lunging for Dain’s chest. Steel clashed as he swept it aside, stepping into the counter. The axe-wielder was already charging Riven.

Riven sidestepped, blade flashing low, too slow. The axe smashed against his guard, jolting up his arm, and a tail whipped around with bone-snapping force. It took his legs out from under him, throwing him flat into the snow.

The cleaver was already rising above him.

Riven’s eyes locked on the descending blade, and then Dain was there, sliding in like the strike belonged to him. His sword came up under the cleaver’s guard, wrenching the lizardman’s wrist wide. In the same breath, Dain’s boot drove into its midsection, shoving it back just enough for Riven to roll clear.

They came up together. No words. Just the kind of look men give each other when they’ve both seen the same edge.

Then they ended it. Dain’s sword took the creature through the side of the neck in a vicious cross-cut while Riven drove his dagger up under its chin, the point bursting through the top of its skull. The lizardman sagged between them before they shoved it to the ground.

“On your left!” Nyssa’s voice cut across the clearing.

They spun. She was already a whirlwind, one knife embedded in the axe-wielder’s ribs, another flashing toward the short-sword fighter’s exposed side. It struck true, staggering him, and she closed the gap in three strides. Her blade flashed again, low and decisive, and the creature dropped with a wet hiss.

The spear-carrier came for her next, thrusting high, but she ducked under it, sliding across the snow. Her dagger punched into the soft flesh under its jaw, and when she wrenched it free, blood steamed in the cold air.

The last lizardman, wounded but furious, swung wildly at Dain. He caught the blow on his guard, dragging it low, and Riven stepped in to finish it, blade biting deep under its ribs.

Silence fell in jagged gasps. The snow was trampled and stained dark red, steam curling from fresh blood. Nyssa moved among the bodies, plucking her knives free one by one, wiping them clean before sliding them home.

Riven let out a long breath, meeting Dain’s eye again. “Owe you one.”

Dain only grunted. “You’ll pay it back.”

Then Riven’s gaze snapped to the treeline. Movement, dozens of shapes, weaving between the trunks. “There's more.”

“We shouldn't just hang around then,” said Dain. They mounted fast, spurring their horses into a gallop. Wind burned their faces as they drove the animals hard, snow spraying up behind. From the treeline, a tide of scaled bodies and glinting steel filled the trail behind them. They veered northwest, pushing hard, the pursuit loud behind them. The three of them leaned into it, the cold burning in their lungs, the port city somewhere ahead in the trees. Behind them, the shapes kept coming. They didn't slow down to count.
