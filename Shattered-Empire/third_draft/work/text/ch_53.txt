The streets of Opindale were quieter in the pale warmth of morning, the kind of warmth that came off the sea breeze rather than the sun. Gulls cried faint and sharp overhead, their voices carrying down the narrow lanes. Harmon's boots struck steadily against the cobbles, Weslin keeping close but a half-step behind, his head bent in thought.
Ahead, Breckenstall Stable crouched at the lane's end, its timbers gray with age, its loft windows blank with dust. In lamplight, it had once seemed a den for ghosts. Now, in daylight, it only looked smaller. The years showed plain in sagging beams and weathered stone, yet a weight clung to it still.
"It doesn't look the same," Weslin said at last, breaking the long silence between them. "Smaller than I remember. Dirtier too."
Harmon glanced at him, then back at the building. "The place hasn't changed. You have. That's why it looks different."
Weslin considered it, mouth set in thought. He shifted, fingers brushing the hilt of his short blade. "What does it really take?"
"To do what?" Harmon asked.
"To be a good squire. Good enough that you'd let me be a knight someday."
Harmon slowed a fraction. "An arcane knight," he corrected. "You're an adept, Weslin. Whatever you become, you carry that into it. Don't let anyone hand you a smaller word for what you are."
Weslin's brow lifted slightly, then settled. "An arcane knight, then. What does it take?"
"Learning to see. To listen. Knowing when to speak and when silence does more than words can. Knowing yourself plainly, what you can carry and what you can't, before you ever try to carry someone else." Harmon tapped his temple once. "Sword drills will build your arms. This is what makes a knight, arcane or otherwise."
Weslin nodded slowly, chewing on it. "So the blade comes last."
"The blade comes last. Everything that matters comes before it."
For a time, only their footsteps filled the lane. Then Weslin asked, quieter, "The ring. How does it work?" His eyes flicked toward Harmon's hand, then away. "When I was small, I heard a story that a Ringbearer could walk through an iron door like it wasn't there."
Harmon was quiet for a moment. "There are a lot of stories. Most of them wrong. A few of them are less wrong than I'd like." He flexed his hand once, the dull metal catching the light. "What it does in truth is give me power. Strength when I call for it, speed when I need it, reach past what my body alone could manage. But it wasn't a thing I bought or earned in any market. It was a gift from God, and gifts of that kind carry a weight to them."
"What kind of weight?"
"This morning kind," Harmon said. "Every time I draw deep on it, there's a morning after where the body wants to stay on the floor. The more I take, the longer that morning lasts." He looked ahead. "And no, it doesn't walk me through iron doors. That part you can let go of."
Weslin studied him sidelong, catching the things Harmon had chosen not to say. He didn't push. He opened his mouth, closed it again, and nodded once.
They reached the stable doors. Sunlight pooled across the yard, warming the wood without lifting the smell of long neglect. The boards groaned as Harmon shoved them wide, and the stink met them both, stale straw, old hay gone sour, a rot that had settled into the rafters years ago and never left.
"Smells the same," Weslin muttered, wrinkling his nose.
"Some things don't age well," Harmon said. "Leave filth long enough, and it becomes part of the structure."
They walked the long aisle, boots whispering over brittle straw. Light fell in narrow shafts from the loft windows, catching dust that drifted slowly in the still air. Weslin remembered the last time he had walked this hall, shadow pressing every corner, his pulse loud in his ears. Now, with the sun at their backs, he noticed things he'd missed before. The narrowness of the passage. The thinness of the rafters. The way the table in the back room had seemed massive once, and now only looked worn.
He paused where the hall pinched in, just before the feed room. Last time he had kept close to the wall here, made himself small. His feet didn't do that now.
"It felt bigger before," he said quietly.
"You were smaller," Harmon answered. "And you carried less."
At the hall's end the warped feed-room door sagged on its hinges as it always had. Harmon pressed it open and crossed to the back wall where the boards met unevenly. The ring brushed the wood. A faint click answered from somewhere inside the frame, and the hidden seam opened.
"Still standing," Weslin murmured.
"Still keeping what it was built to keep."
Harmon glanced back once before stepping through. "When we're done here, you go home for a while. Your family. You tell them what you've done and what it's made of you, all of it. They've earned the truth, and they love you enough to carry it." Something showed in his voice that rarely reached his face. "We've learned too well how short that window can be."
Weslin swallowed hard. "I will."
"Good. When you're ready, there's more to learn. Blade, shield, oath, what it costs to hold another person's life in your hands. All of it." He turned and stepped through.
Old air moved past them, stale leather, old ink, the musk of years pressed into the wood and the stone beneath it. Voices stirred in the shadows beyond. Harmon set his shoulders and went in. Weslin followed closely.
The room was smaller than memory had kept it. After Stormhaven, after the pass, most things were. It wasn't the place that had changed. It was them.
Several had already gathered. Aldrek stood near the table, broad and steady on the surface, though the way he held himself told the truth to anyone paying attention. Every movement measured, nothing spent that didn't have to be, a man whose reserves had been drained to the floor and had crawled back to just enough. Garran leaned against the far wall, arms crossed, shoulders set like a load-bearing beam. Ash sat hunched on a bench, scarred hands laced, eyes on the floorboards as if something still burned there just under the surface.
At the back, Taerel had settled into his chair with the particular ease of a man who has decided a room will accommodate him rather than the reverse. Aymir leaned forward on his elbows beside him, both brothers watchful but each in his own manner. Reau sat off to one side, coiled and quiet, present in body and somewhere else entirely in mind, his eyes fixed on a stretch of wall that had nothing on it.
The scrape of Harmon's boots drew attention without demanding it. Voices fell. Postures shifted. Every one of them was marked, some plainly in skin and sinew, some in the way they held their faces when they thought no one was watching. They were here. All of them. That alone was something.
Weslin's eyes moved around the room with a different kind of attention than he'd carried the last time he stood in it. He caught the scratches gouged into the chair legs, the ink stains on the corner desk where someone's pen had gone wrong years back, the grooves worn into the floor by chairs dragged forward and back over time. His voice came out hushed. "Feels different this time."
Harmon allowed himself the smallest smile. "That's because you're not the boy in the corner anymore. Once you sit at the table, nothing looks the same again."
Across from the door, Nyssa sat alone, her chair turned just slightly from the rest. Her eyes were fixed on the two empty seats in front of her and hadn't lifted when Harmon entered. She stared at those chairs with the deliberate attention of someone who has decided that looking away would be a mercy she didn't deserve. Two chairs. Two names pressing on her chest with a weight that felt physical. Guilt had settled into her the way cold settles into stone, all the way through, into everything.
Harmon's gaze moved there and away. He didn't press it.
He crossed to the head of the table, set both palms on the scarred wood, and let the silence thicken until it had weight of its own.
"You're all here," he said at last, voice even and heavy together. "By blood and steel and the kind of sacrifice coin doesn't repay. I count it no small thing to stand in a room with every one of you still breathing."
He drew a flat leather case from inside his cloak, set it on the table, and opened the clasps. Inside lay neat rolls of parchment, each sealed with the personal wax of King Edric himself, the mark pressed deep enough to leave no argument about where it came from. Beside them, purses with enough weight to announce themselves.
"Your pay." He nodded to Weslin, who stepped forward and moved the length of the table, setting each scroll and purse down with both hands. "And more than pay. These are pardons, signed by King Edric. Whatever you carried into this, it's gone. Every charge. Every warrant. Every name on a list in some magistrate's drawer. Clean. You walk free."
The words went into the room and didn't come back.
Garran's hand shook as he broke his seal. He didn't open it. He pressed it, still rolled, to his chest and held it there with both fists. Ash made a short, dry sound too tired to be a laugh, muttered something no one caught, and tucked his away inside his coat. Taerel accepted his with a flicker of a smirk that didn't quite hide the weight of it, trading a look with Aymir that held what neither chose to say aloud. Aymir's nod was brief and real.
Aldrek took his scroll in both hands and bowed his head over it. The motion was small and cost him visible effort. He said nothing.
Reau's lay in front of him, untouched. His eyes came up to Harmon.
"Yours is a different matter," Harmon said. "We talk after."
Reau held the look and gave one short nod.
Nyssa's scroll sat in front of the empty space where her attention had gone. She hadn't reached for it. She hadn't reached for anything.
Harmon let the room settle, let the sound of seals cracking and purse strings being tested fill the quiet for a moment, then drew a breath and brought them back.
"Before I ask anything of you, you're owed something first. The whole picture. Not the piece of it you stood in." He looked around the table. "We fought this in two places. Most of you only know one of them. I won't be the only voice in this room laying it out, because most of you lived parts of it I only heard about secondhand. So we tell it together, and when we're done, everyone in this room knows what everyone else paid."
No one moved for a beat. Then Harmon began.
"Stormhaven. We went in blind. That was the mission. Find out what happened to a city that went dark without a sound, map it, and get the governor's family out alive if they could be found. No messenger had come out. No flares. Just refugees stumbling south with whatever they'd kept in their heads."
He let that sit, then turned the floor.
"Riven got us in," Ash said, picking it up. His voice had the dockside forward push to it, words tumbling a little before they settled. "Old fencing runs under the city. Thieves' road. The kind of path you can't buy a map for, because the men who knew it would sooner take a knife than sell it. Without him, we're standing outside a wall watching, and that's the whole trip." He scratched a thumb along his scarred jaw. "The city was scrubbed clean when we got down there. The Veydrassil had gutted the guilds, emptied the Underlane, swept the streets till there was nothing left moving. People didn't lose a fight. They got caged, and most of 'em never even knew by what."
"There was a ward over the city," Aldrek said, and his voice carried the grave weight the priest gave to anything that mattered. "We took it for an occupation working at first. It was not. The Red Cloaks raised it from inside the walls, anchored to something old buried beneath the streets. It was not built to keep us out. It was built to keep something in. A seal upon a link of magic, holding a thing under Stormhaven out of the world and hiding what it was at the same time." He paused, the words deliberate. "When the seal failed, we glimpsed a part of what it had been hiding."
The room had gone very still.
Harmon took it back. "What met us above was bad enough on its own. I'll give you the name, because the name is the name it is." He said it plainly. "Sythrael Korr."
The effect moved through the room at once. Taerel's ease left him. Aymir's elbows came off the table. Garran's arms uncrossed. Even Reau turned from the wall.
"Korr is from the Nine," Harmon said.
The shift that followed was hard to put a word to. Not panic. The particular stillness of people who have just heard something old and large and bad enough that it had stayed in stories rather than memory. Ash sat back slow. Aldrek lowered his eyes to the table. Garran's weight settled, his shoulders pressing flat against the wall behind him as though the wall had become necessary.
"The Nine were knights before there was a shared empire," Harmon went on. "Before the first Accord, before the map looked anything like it does now. Men of tremendous ability in their age. Korr among them. They were bled and cursed into what they became, some of them by their own choosing. Korr chose. That tells you what kind of man he was before the change took the rest of him."
Ash leaned forward. "Are they coming back? All of 'em?"
"No." Harmon's answer landed flat and immediate, the firmness of a thing meant to be believed. "What I saw was one of them, moving through a projection, active in a way none of the Nine have been in a very long time. That is not a return. If it were, we would not be sitting here drinking the same air. I would have you scattered to the four corners of the realm, and I would be somewhere else entirely."
That settled some of them. Not all.
"There was something else under that city," Harmon said, and his voice dropped, which was more than most things made it do. "Something I can't name yet with the certainty it deserves. Korr was a herald. This was the thing that sends heralds. I saw enough of it through the ward to recognize what I was looking at, and I have hoped a long time that I never would. Until I can be sure, it does not leave this room. Not a word of it. We do not even speculate where others might hear."
No one pushed him.
"The family came out alive," Dain said into the quiet that followed, and his voice carried the polished court control that sharpened rather than softened when he was holding something hard. "Korr's projection cost him to maintain across that distance. Every blast he threw, he paid for twice. It threw Aldrek through a stone wall, and Aldrek got back up anyway, which is the only reason some of us are sitting here." He didn't look at Aldrek when he said it. The respect was in not looking. "We got them out. We got ourselves out. Then Harmon split us at the clearing and sent us northwest to find the other team."
Harmon let that hand off, then looked to the brothers. "The pass."
Aymir sat forward. There was nothing ornamental in his voice, military-clean, structured, but not without warmth even now. He spoke it the way a man speaks something he's run through too many times in the dark to keep any illusions about.
"Luca's team moved on an Orc war camp in the mountains. The plan was to thin them before they could march. Poison the stores, hit them from the ridge, break their numbers before they were ever a column." He stopped briefly. "It turned. They came back at us in a wave. Joblins mostly, Goblins running them. Hundreds. We held the line as long as men can hold against something that doesn't care how many of itself it loses."
Taerel, beside him, was looking at the table. He didn't speak, but his thumb pressed slowly against the grain of the wood.
"Lysana was covering Luca," Aymir continued. "She'd just put an arrow through a Joblin about to take his head off. The two of them caught eyes for half a breath, that was all, half a breath." His voice stayed flat and clean and honest, which was the only way he could get the words out at all. "A Joblin came down off a ledge above her with the dagger already up. She never saw it. It put the blade through her before any of us could move. Luca killed it in the same second. It was already too late."
Ash made a sound that wasn't a word and pressed a fist against the table's edge. Garran's head had gone down, one hand flat on the wood.
"After that," Aymir said, "Luca went dark. Not cover. Dark in a way none of us has a name for. The whole grove went black around him, and it wasn't the absence of light. It was a presence, the kind that presses on the lungs. When it lifted, nothing on that field was moving but us. He walked off after. No word. No stop. We've had nothing from him since."
"He didn't pack first," Taerel said. It was the first thing he'd offered, and it came quietly, clipped, the formality holding back something underneath it. "We found his horse still tethered. Saddled. He simply walked and left it standing." He looked up briefly. "Luca does not leave a thing of use behind. That is not the man we rode in with."
The silence held that. The ones who had been in the tower were hearing the pass for the first time. The ones who had been at the pass were hearing the tower. All of it in the same room now, the same shared weight, nobody carrying their piece alone anymore.
Harmon let it work, then let it go.
"While we were gone, the war reached home, too." Harmon's tone shifted, the briefing cadence giving way to something he was carrying straight off the road. "I've just come from the king. There was an attempt on Edric's life during the Feast. Governor Drake was the hand meant to do it."
That moved through the room. Dain's head came up first, the fallen noble in him reading the shape of it before the rest.
"Forced," Harmon said, before the question could form. "The Brotherhood's been disbanded for years, since they turned on the realm, but the men who wore the cloak didn't die with it. One of them took Drake's wife and children and made his cooperation the price of their lives. Gave him a blade built to hollow a man rather than kill him. Leave the crown on a king who was no longer the king."
"And he agreed to it," Dain said. Not quite a question. He knew what a man would do with a knife to his family.
"He held out as long as a man can with his children on the scale. When the moment came, he moved." Harmon let it land. "Edric was already wearing mail under his coat. The blade turned on steel. Drake broke and confessed all of it, the steward behind it, the whole arrangement. His family was reached in time. They're alive."
"How'd the king know to wear the mail?" Garran asked.
"Because the warning reached him," Harmon said, "through a channel most of the court doesn't know exists." He turned slightly toward the corner where a man had been sitting quietly since before any of them arrived. "Which brings me to the one some of you have been wondering about."
The man stood, easy, with the manner of someone who has never once needed to fill a room with noise to own it. "Adin," he said. "Collector of rare things, keeper of other people's secrets, and the reason a fair number of you still have heads on your shoulders." The grin was real, the sharpness behind it sharper. "The note that put the mail under the king's coat tonight. The timing at your gate in Stormhaven. The word that set you on the right floor at the right hour. The whisper dropped in the right ear three weeks before any of you went near those walls. That was me."
He raised his right hand. The ring on it was the twin of Harmon's.
The company drew breath as one. Even the brothers went still.
"Adin is a Ringbearer," Harmon said, and gave that a moment to land. "He won't be in the field with you except under a need none of us want to meet. His place is the work behind the work. The eyes and ears. The intelligence that tells us where to move before we move. The assets that get us into a place and back out of it when being caught means something permanent." His voice settled into something flat and final. "He is the master of shadows. He acquires what a mission needs, and he sees what we can't. He answers to me. You answer to him. When Adin asks something of you, understand that I am asking. And believe me, if he is asking, the situation is one that requires an answer."
Adin eased back where he stood, unhurried. "My reach runs wide, and runs at every level. Tavern cellars in port towns. Correspondence rooms in kingdoms that would not open their gates to us. I have people in places our enemies are certain are safe, and they tell me what those people would much rather keep quiet. Targets. Routes. Names. What's planned, and when, and by whose hand." The grin dropped into something cleaner. "What I don't have is anyone who can act on it. That's you. I hand you the picture and the means to move inside it. You do the work. You never walk into a room I haven't already mapped."
Harmon picked it up. "When the word comes, we move. Swift, precise, into the places an army can't go without announcing itself. Unseen until we choose not to be. What we're building isn't a company of soldiers. It's the thing that moves where soldiers can't and is gone before anyone knows it was there."
He drew a breath.
"And there's a war ahead of us regardless. King Edric's army is moving. We will secure Aldoria." He said it without a hedge and let the certainty stand in the room. "It'll go better than it would have, because of what we carried back. The maps. The tunnels under the mountains the enemy used to slip past the pass. The strength and the patterns of what's out there. Edric's commanders have all of it now, ahead of the march, instead of learning it the way we did, with bodies in the snow. If it saves one soldier who'd otherwise walk into the dark blind, then what we bled for already bought something. I believe it buys a good deal more than one."
He let that settle.
"That's why I asked you here. Not only to pay you and pardon you and send you back to your lives, though you've earned both. But because I mean to keep going, and what I'm building serves the crown and the good of this alliance without taking its orders from men who've never stood where you stood. When the army marches in the open, we're already where no column can reach without being seen. When an enemy hides behind walls or whispers, we go through the cracks." He set his hand flat on the wood. "And it doesn't end with the enemies who call themselves enemies. There are men who sleep safe inside this alliance, who wear its name and prey on the very people they're meant to rule and serve. Men who believe their title and their treasury and their high walls put them past anyone's reach. We will teach them otherwise. In their coffers, in their names, and when it comes to it, in their blood."
Garran's eyes had come fully up. The storm-dark steadiness in them said he'd heard that part clearly.
"I know what you're carrying," Harmon went on, quieter now. "I know the ghosts that followed you off that ridge and out of that city. Lysana. Riven. Luca, wherever he's walked to. Aurelia, still fighting her way back at my manor with Neavin at her side, which is the only reason the two of them aren't in this room. Every one of you came into this with something and is leaving with more than you came in with. That's the real cost, and I won't stand here and call it clean or fair."
The room held it without moving.
"What I will tell you is this. The ones who aren't in this room paid the real price for what we're about to start. What we are building here was bought with what they gave. I mean to make it worth that. I need to know if you do too."
Before the silence could close, Reau pushed his chair back. The sound of it cut sharply through the quiet. He stood without looking at anyone, hands flexing at his sides, eyes on the door.
"You've been family to me," he said, voice low and direct, few words and no flourish. "What you gave me when I had nothing doesn't get forgotten. You know that." He stopped, something tightening behind his face. "But I have a hunt that isn't finished. My father was betrayed and murdered by the same people who turned on this realm, and the ones who did it are still breathing. I won't sit at any table, this one included, while that's true. None of you would sleep right knowing I was out there with it undone, and I won't ask you to." He looked at Harmon. "I'm sorry."
He turned for the door.
"Reau." Adin's voice was quiet, but it stopped him. The shadow man pushed off the wall and crossed the room without hurry, drawing a folded scroll from inside his coat as he went. Harmon moved to meet them, and the three of them stood close by the door, heads low, the scroll open between Adin's hands.
The rest of the room caught nothing but fragments. Adin's voice, pitched low. "Path's already cut." Harmon's, lower still. "Closer than you think." And then, from Adin, almost gentle: "Your hunt ends here, if you let it. We've been building toward the same thing from different ends. You just didn't know whose hand was on the other end of it."
Whatever was written on that scroll, Reau read it twice. His jaw worked. His eyes moved over it a third time as though the words might rearrange themselves into something he could refuse.
Then his voice was loud enough to carry. "All right. I'll hear it out. I'll stay for that much."
He came back to his chair and sat, the scroll folded small in his fist. His shoulders were still tight, and the set of his face said the decision was costing him even as he made it. He'd made it anyway. That was enough. Whatever the rest of that scroll said, he kept it to himself, and no one asked.
Before Harmon could bring the floor back, Nyssa stood.
The chair scraped, sharp and sudden, and heads turned. She stood with her hands at her sides and her eyes moving around the table without settling, without finding the one face she couldn't trust herself to find yet. When she spoke, the words came fast, the way words come when the fear is that slowing down means they stop.
"I want to say yes," she said. "I do. But I can't. Not yet. There's something I owe first, and until that's done, I'm not in a position to give what being here would ask of me." She stopped, steadied herself. "When it's done, I'll be back. That's not hope. That's what's going to happen."
Harmon's nod was slow. "Then the door is open when you're ready."
"Thank you."
She reached out and picked up her pardon. Left the coin where it sat.
And then, because she had spent the whole of this gathering refusing to look, refusing to let herself confirm what she was afraid to confirm, and because some part of her had known since she sat down that she could not walk out of this room without doing it once, she looked up.
Dain was there.
He had been there the whole time, seated beside Aldrek, still enough and shadowed enough behind that broad frame that when she'd come in with her eyes already going to the empty chairs, she hadn't seen him. Had seen two empty seats and built her guilt around them both. He was in one of them. Alive. Sitting with the particular stillness of a man who has come through something and knows it and isn't ready to call it over yet. His face was the face she knew, unscarred, because Aldrek had spent what was left of himself making certain of that, but his eyes were older. Not in years. In what they had kept.
He was looking at her.
No accusation. No demand. Nothing that asked her to be other than what she was. Just the same thing he'd been telling her since the dock, the same thing he'd been trying to tell her for longer than either of them had admitted, quiet and patient and entirely certain.
I'm still here.
Her breath caught. She held his eyes for as long as she could, which was not long, and then she looked away and walked to the door. The latch clicked. She was gone.
Nobody rushed to fill the silence. Dain's hand had moved to the table's edge without him noticing it there. Nobody said anything about it.
The lantern shifted in a draft. The stable groaned once above them.
Harmon let it breathe, then set his hand flat on the scarred wood, the ring catching the lantern light, and looked at each of them in turn.
"Who stands with me?"
Aldrek rose first, slow and deliberate, his mace-hand flexing once as if testing what remained in it and finding enough. "I do. For faith. For the crown. And for those who gave more than we can pay back."
Dain followed. His leg took its moment, nothing broken, nothing Aldrek hadn't mended, but a body doesn't forget what was done to it on a dock in the dark no matter how clean a healer leaves the bone. He came upright carefully. When he spoke, it was quiet, the polish steady over the ache underneath. "I'm in. Because walking away would mean it broke me." A pause, short and certain. "It didn't."
Aymir and Taerel exchanged one of their wordless conversations, the kind that had run between them their whole lives, fluent in everything that didn't need saying. Aymir's half-smile settled it. Taerel drew a slow breath out through his nose and let one brow rise.
"I find," he said, with the unhurried precision of a man who has weighed exactly how much a moment is worth before he speaks into it, "that the company is poor, the pay irregular, and the lodging a stable." He let it hang. "And yet I cannot think of anywhere I would rather suffer all three. We shall remain."
Aymir put out his hand. Taerel looked at it a beat in the way he looked at most things before he accepted them, then clasped it firmly, and the two shook once. That was the whole of it, and from the brothers it was a great deal.
Ash leaned back, smirk sharp under eyes that hadn't slept right in four days. "You're all insane, you know that. But hell, I like insane. Count me in, long as nobody objects to the occasional fire."
The laugh that came was not clean or comfortable. It was the kind that escapes when grief and exhaustion have run long enough that something absurd finally catches them off guard, the seal cracking just a little. It moved around the table in pieces, a sound from Aymir, a breath from Aldrek, the slow release from Taerel. Even Dain's face came loose for a moment.
Then Garran.
He stood slowly, the kind of slowly that says this matters, and the silence gathered around him the way it gathers around a man who has carried something long enough that the room can feel its weight.
"If I do this," he said, his voice rough with something he wasn't going to name, "there's one condition. No more half-name. Not Garr." He stopped. Said the rest plainly. "Lysana gave me that name. It was hers to give, and it stays with her." His eyes were dry. The weight in them was not. "If I stand here, I stand as Garran Thornebrook."
The name landed the way names land when they've been held in silence too long and are finally set down in a room able to hold them. Heavy. Permanent. Real.
Harmon inclined his head, and when he gave the name back, it carried the weight of a recognition. "So be it. Garran Thornebrook."
One by one the rest gave their answer. No vow spoken in unison. No recited oath. Just a circle of people who had bled together choosing to do it again, each in their own way, each in their own time, the ones not in the room present in every word and in the silences between them.
Harmon watched every face.
The circle was smaller than when this began. He didn't look away from it. He let himself see it plainly. The empty chairs. The coin was still sitting on the table where Nyssa had left it. The absences pressed down heavier than furniture. What Lysana gave at that camp. What Riven gave on a dock in the dark. What Luca carried off that field alone, walking away from a fight he'd just won without once looking back. What Aurelia was still climbing out of, four days on, at his manor. What Aldrek had spent of himself to keep one more of them breathing.
He looked at the coin.
Left it where it lay.
"Go to your families," he said at last. "The ones who love you and the ones you love. Time is a cruel thief. Every one of us has watched it cut a life short without warning or courtesy. Don't waste the days that are yours."
He let a breath move through him.
"My manor is open to those who'd rather stay close. When Weslin comes back from his family, he'll need a hand with the blade. I expect more than one of you to be there for it."
That pulled something from the room. A dry, wry stir. Taerel elbowed his brother, the two of them sharing the private grin of men who had just signed onto something larger than themselves and weren't above enjoying it. Ash muttered something about fire being a more useful trade than swordwork. Garran's shoulders squared like a man who'd already decided.
Harmon's gaze moved last to the empty spaces. Not the chairs alone. The shape of what was gone, the absences with more weight than anything in the room that still had a body attached to it. His voice softened, but the thing beneath it didn't.
"We buried friends. We bled past what any accounting could hold. But what we've built here, out of ruin, out of broken pieces, out of people who had every reason in the world to walk the other way, it will hold. And if we carry it honestly, it will be worth what it cost."
He stepped back from the table and stood straight in the lantern glow.
His hand moved once, taking in the table, the faces, the circle.
"This is not an ending. It is a call. When it comes again, I'll be waiting." He looked at each of them, once, final. "I'll expect you to answer."
The weight in the room was not silence. It was something built of grief and purpose together, the kind of resolve that doesn't come from the absence of loss but from the decision to carry it forward anyway. Garran bowed his head, fists at his sides. Aldrek murmured a prayer too low for the rest to hear. The brothers shared a nod slower and more serious than their usual. Ash, for once, kept his quiet.
Dain pressed his palm to the table's edge before he pulled himself upright, the phantom weight dragging at a leg that had been healed whole and hadn't forgiven it yet, his eyes going not to the floor but to the others, one by one.
And Weslin, still at Harmon's shoulder, stood straighter than he had walking through the stable doors that morning. The boy gone from his face. The beginnings of something harder and more deliberate settling in its place.
Harmon nodded once to them all.
"Go."
The scrape of chairs. The rustle of cloaks and the low murmur of words already moving on to the next thing. Footsteps out through the stable and into the morning. One by one they filed past, each carrying what they'd come in with and something more that hadn't been there before.
On the table, in the space where Nyssa had been, her coin lay untouched.
Harmon looked at it once.
Then he walked out and left it there.
