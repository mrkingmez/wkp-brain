The echoes of the Feast still lingered in the streets behind them, half-eaten garlands tangled in gutters, spilled wine dried in crimson streaks beneath streetlamps, and the faint, sour smoke of last night’s fire dancers curling up from alley hearths. Somewhere deep in the city’s belly, a flute played its final song, off-key and alone. 
The fog had swallowed the echoes, replaced revelry with silence, and turned the wharf into something sacred and somber, like a cathedral built of water, stone, and breath. A place for departures, not parades.
Yet on the Western Docks of Opindale, Harmon stood watching the world move, slow, deliberate, and heavy with purpose, as this band prepared to head upriver.
Harmon stood at the edge of the dock, taking stock of the team in silence, noting habits, spacing, and the small tells that never lied on a first task.
The riverfront stretched long and low along the curve of the Andralis River, stone docks slick with night moisture and the faint sheen of fish oil and algae. Wooden pylons jutted into the dark water like the ribs of a half-drowned god. Moss crept up from beneath the boards, and a pair of barn owls sat silently atop a broken lantern post, eyes pale and unblinking. Somewhere downriver, a bullfrog croaked once and went quiet.
The air was damp, smelling of rope mold, cold iron, and river brine. The kind of smell that clung to your boots long after you left it behind.
A few lamps still burned along the loading quay, but their light was low and golden, barely strong enough to fight the creeping silver mist that rolled in off the water. The fog moved in slow coils across the cobbled wharf, swallowing the occasional crate, piling, or stray rope with lazy hunger.
Further off, in the deeper dark, loomed the silhouettes of trading ships and barge-haulers tied along the upper docks, massive, broad-decked creatures meant for cargo runs out to the bay and the sea beyond. But just beyond it, in the southernmost berth, the vessel was preparing to depart. It was smaller. Sleeker. Built for speed and maneuverability, not bulk.
The Wyrm’s Wake, river-runner class. A two-deck, curved hull built of black alder and reinforced with riversteel plating along the keel and sides. The entire bow was shaped in a long serpentine curve, ending in a carved river-drake head, fangs bared and polished with old blood-red lacquer. 
Runes, etched deep into the alder at its birth, flickered faintly with the pulse of the river. A wide, braided line stretched out from the mount, vanishing upriver. It was already connected to the first segment of the tetherline rail, a buried system of interlocked stone pylons threaded with enchantments and embedded gear-drives that pulled boats against the current using pre-loaded anchor charges and momentum slings. It was part magic, part engineering, part madness, but it worked. What mattered to Harmon was not the system, but how the crew handled it.
The sounds of final preparations echoed up through the fog. A plank creaked. A hammer rang softly against a rail. Hooves clattered against cobbles as a nervous horse was led toward the ramp, breath misting in the cold. A canvas sail flapped loose and was quickly caught by two crewmen, hands rough and practiced, moving like they’d done this a hundred times. They had.
Around the dock, the workers moved in silence, muffled voices trading instructions, straps tightened, crates shifted, saddlebags passed from hand to hand. The clank of tack and harness. The low groan of a wooden beam accepting another burden.
At the head of the ramp, Harmon stood, with hands clasped behind his back, his long dark cloak rippling gently in the river’s breeze. The silver trim at his shoulders caught the lanternlight in cold flashes. The ring on his left hand was plain iron, the kind a man might keep for sentiment or habit. Nothing about it invited a second look. That was the point.
He did not speak. He watched the people around him. He took notes on who lent their arms to help with what was not theirs, who hung back until pressed, who calmed an animal with a touch rather than a shout. These were the things he counted.
A commander in stillness, Harmon’s presence was a kind of gravity, quiet but undeniable. He watched as horses were coaxed up the ramp, tack still damp with dew, and as bundles of gear were fastened down with rough canvas rope. Crates thudded onto the deck in rhythm, and the crew moved with the practiced speed of urgency, not panic.
Most of these faces were new to him. Not all strangers in name, but strangers in action. This wasn't a regular squad. This wasn’t a polished unit trained in proving grounds. It was something else entirely. The start to his vision of what he had planned for years and for the first time, he was seeing it. Harmon still wasn’t sure whether it would work. If these people could work together, it was worth the risk.
“Last horse is up,” Harmon called over his shoulder in a command voice. “That gate closes in three minutes.”
He took a long pull from a brass thermos at his side, steam rising from it. Spiced tea, by the smell of it. Something strong and rooty.
From the stone steps behind him, Adin approached, hands in his coat pockets, strolling. He looked out of place, not because of his clothes, which were clean and elegant, but because of the stillness in his eyes.
Adin stopped beside Harmon, watching the activity below.
“Still can’t believe you are doing it,” he said quietly. “Are you sure about this?”
Harmon grunted. “It is late for second thoughts. These are the people you placed in my hands.”
“Yes, but they weren’t—” Adin trailed off, gesturing vaguely toward the boat without naming names. “They’re different.”
“They are something. Uncertainties aside,” Harmon muttered, “It begins here.”
Adin sensed something different about his friend this time. It had been years since they’d last stood side by side, but he knew this man better than anyone. The one before him felt harder. Sharper. He wondered if it was the ring’s influence.
The two stood in silence for a moment, the river lapping at the pilings below.
A gust of wind rolled through, bringing with it the faint sound of bells from across the river—the Temple of the First Order, just beginning their dawn chime.
On the far bank, the city of Opindale began to stir. Smoke drifted up from the baker's chimneys. A small fishing skiff pushed off into the fog, one lone figure rowing. Lanterns along the bridges flickered as city wardens did their final sweep before shift change. The world was waking up—but not this part of it. Here, in the riverborn edge, it always felt like time held its breath before letting go.
Back on the deck, a crewmember called out: “Towline charge set. Anchor relays ready.”
Harmon raised a hand. “Hold until full board.”
He turned to Adin again, voice lower now.
“You didn’t come just to watch,” Harmon said. “But I’m glad you did. There’s something else I need to ask.”
“More?” Adin asked, arching a brow. “You’re starting to run out of favors.”
“Let me know when we are even.” They both gave a low chuckle. Adin looked at him again, and in that moment, the man he knew was standing before him. He was still in there, and that eased a little of Adin’s worry. 
“So what is it?”
Harmon reached into his pack and retrieved a map case. He unfolded it, showed Adin, then pointed to a spot, “Can you get me a ship there? Have it waiting on us. In, let's say, fourteen days.”
“This will not be cheap; word of the danger will be widespread in a day or so. It will be hard to find a crew who will risk it.”
“Give them this, I’m sure someone will take it for that,” said Harmon as he passed over a coin purse. Adin took a quick look inside. Platinum caught the lanternlight.
“I am sure it will. Is that all?”
“You know me so well. One more thing. I was unable to return to the palace tonight. Could you get this to the king?” As he passed over a satchel this time.
Adin couldn’t help but take a peek. It was just a mirror and another smaller sack.
“The smaller one is for you,” Harmon chimed in. 
Adin looked up at Harmon with a puzzled look on his face, then removed the small sack while placing the satchel on the ground and opened it. “A stone? Wait, is it a sending stone?”
“Yes, it is; it is the one we used back in our adventures.”
“I didn’t know you still had them. Am I your backup messenger plan?” Adin asked. “I can do that.” His face lit up with a grin, and his eyes drifted toward old memories.
Harmon examined the man. He knew that look on his face. “That, among other things. Now, don’t go getting that nostalgic look on your face. No matter how much I would like for us to ride again, I need you here. I put a note in the sack for you, but I will tell you now. Something bigger is moving,“ Harmon said quietly. “I need someone here I trust. Someone who can act if things turn.” He paused. “They know help is already in the city. They do not know it is you,” another breath. “ Mr. Holt has all their pay if something should befall me, they’re covered.”
“Something befall you? This is meant to be a simple peek and report,” Adin said, but his voice lacked conviction. Harmon only gave the faintest smile in reply, one that didn’t reach his eyes. They clasped wrists, firm and quick. “Fourteen days,” Adin murmured. “Be careful.” Then he turned, his figure swallowed by the fog.
Down at the dockside, someone whistled, short and sharp. The final ramp was lifting. Horses were tied, gear stowed, and the last of the figures filed on board. The dock quieted for a moment. Then Wyrm’s Wake let out a low groan as it began to move. They were on their way.
The deck swayed underfoot, a slow rocking rhythm as the riverboat cut its way northward through the dark, silken waters of the Andralis. The city of Opindale faded behind them like a memory, its lantern-lit docks dwindling to pinpricks against the predawn mist. Only the occasional glimmer of tower braziers marked the city’s sleeping sprawl, while the sky above grew a deeper shade of bruised blue, whispering of a coming dawn not yet ready to bloom.
The boat was wide and flat-bottomed, built to haul cargo, livestock, and soldiers upriver, but now refitted for a quieter purpose. Ten war-trained horses stood secured in pairs toward the rear. Canvas tarps stretched over the midsection, covering supplies and rough sleeping space, while crates of rations and spare equipment had been lashed down with sailors’ knots. The scent of riverweed clung to the damp boards, mingling with horseflesh and the faint tang of lantern oil. 
Near the stern, a small elevated pilot house overlooked the vessel, its window shutters propped open to catch the air. The crew was minimal, just two hired hands and a grizzled steersman who guided the vessel with a long tiller, eyes half-closed but instinctively aware of every twist and pull in the water.
Harmon stood near the horses, one hand braced on the rail, watching the murky current glide beneath them. He had been silent for most of the launch, his gaze drifting from the vanishing city to the team that had just embarked on something far more delicate than a patrol.
The slap of boots echoed across the deck as Weslin returned, stopping beside him with a sharp nod. “They’re gathered.” He had dark circles under his eyes, and his jaw was set in the careful way of someone who had not slept and was not going to admit it. Behind them, Opindale was already gone, swallowed by the river bend and the pre-dawn dark. Weslin had not looked back. Harmon had watched to see if he would. He had not. That told him something. 
Harmon glanced at the others, now assembling near the aft quarter by the horses. Some leaned against the railings, others squatted beside their packs, rolling shoulders and shaking off the early chill. Luca, Garr, Dain, and Nyssa were among them, their faces half-cut by lanternlight and river shadow. The only sounds were the soft clop of hooves on the deck and the murmuring shuffle of riverwater against the hull. 
Lysana stood at the rail on the north side of the deck, one hand resting on the wood, looking upriver into the dark. Not anxious. Just oriented. The way a tracker faces the direction they are about to move before they commit to the step. When Luca came to stand near her, she did not turn, but her posture changed slightly, the kind of change that is not quite relaxing but is something adjacent to it. Neither of them spoke. 
Harmon stepped forward, the slow sway of the deck beneath him unacknowledged. His voice was low but carried, measured, and certain.
“I won’t waste your time with ceremony. Most of you I haven’t worked with. That’s not an obstacle, it’s an asset. This mission doesn’t require familiarity. It requires precision, adaptability, and discretion.”
He paused a beat, gaze drifting across the shadowed faces watching him.
“We have received word that there was an attack at the northern pass. We don’t know by whom. Refugees came through, fewer than two dozen, but their stories are inconsistent. Scattered. Some say there was a fire. Others say the sky turned black. All we know is that whatever hit the pass, it wasn't a simple raid.”
A silence followed, deep and uneasy. Even the horses shifted restlessly behind them.
“Almost at the same time, Stormhaven was hit. A similar story, a small group of refugees with hollow-eyed, ash in their hair. All they brought was fear. They are saying the gates were still open when they fled. Since then, no word. No riders. No more refugees.”
The tone shifted, darker, colder. “They may already be graves.” Harmon crossed his arms, “We do not know. We will shed some light on the matter before we add to those graves.” 
He turned slightly, eyes sweeping from stern to aft, voice low and even.“Our assessment points to the Veydrassil,” Harmon said. A subtle shift ran through the group at the name.
“If that’s true, then the former Brotherhood won’t be far behind. Where the Red Cloaks go, the old hounds follow, and they don’t ask questions first. Capabilities? Mostly Adepts, if the pattern holds. Well-armed. Disciplined. Fanatical. The kind who believe death is a message. If the stories about the Pass are even half right, we might not be chasing ghosts. We might be walking into a trap. Either way, we need to know what we are up against.” 
He leaned forward slightly, voice firm. “We are here to scout, assess, and pull truth out of smoke. Soldiers would not work for this. That is why I asked Adin for people with a particular set of skills. He gave me the lot of you. I don’t know why each of you came. Honor, debt, a clean slate, coin. It doesn’t matter. What matters is that you’re here, with eyes open and steel ready. Perfection isn’t required. Presence is.”
He paused. “But here’s the thing.” He stepped toward the rail, fingers resting loosely on the polished wood, voice tightening with purpose.
“I want a small team. Fast, quiet, surgical. You’re not here to march in rows or light fires. You’re here to disappear into places no one else can reach, to pull answers out of silence. Sometimes,” Harmon said evenly, “you need hardened hands to do necessary things.”
He let the weight of his last words settle.
“But you should also know, if this goes wrong, there’s no wall to fall back to. No horn call to bring reinforcements. This isn’t a battlefield. It’s a fracture line. If it breaks, we break with it.”
Ash’s voice, dry from thrust, called out, “You trying to talk us out of going?”
If that speech changed your mind," Harmon said, "you were never going to be much use anyway." He looked at the voice's direction. "I don't see anyone leaving." 
A ripple of laughter moved through the group, which broke up the tension that was building. Harmon holds up his hands to bring back. 
“Yet some may want to leave. That is fine, just do it at the first transit station. No hard feelings, and a pocket full of gold. All I ask is that you keep your mouth shut until we return.  The real test is if you stay. Because no one will ever know what you did, and they never will.  You might, no, you will save lives and give the kingdom a much-needed asset. Information.”
A low murmur erupted in the group as conversation and prayer started. Harmon saw some doubt on a few faces. But no one moved. 
He stepped back into the center of them, voice calm and iron-flat.
“We have a chance to be a part of something that has not been tried before. You may never tell the story over a pint, but you will know. Also, three hundred gold. Each. When you return. Does that answer your compensation question, Dain?” He didn’t wait for an answer before continuing. “Adin already has the payment in full.”
He paused and looked each one in the eye, searching for reservation. Not a one.
“Okay then. Here is the start of the plan. We’re going to split into two teams. One team of four is headed to the pass to assess damage, gather intelligence, and track survivors. The other team, five strong, is bound for Stormhaven to do the same, but there might be more waiting there. The Pass team will be headed up by Luca. The Stormhaven team will be led by Garr. My command cell will operate alongside the Stormhaven team. We will provide support, track movement, and get reports.”
He glanced toward the dark river, then back to them.
“You’re not riding into glory. You’re riding into smoke. I expect clarity. I expect control. If there’s something to report, report it back to me. If you have a question if it should be reported, report it. We will get the word back to the King. This team is the only eyes and ears we've got. We could stop senseless bloodshed. I know I ask a lot, but this is what I am asking, and what is required.”
A pause. A few glances were exchanged between the team, but no one spoke. Only the river, whispering past.
"That is all." He let the silence settle for a moment, the way he always did after something that needed to land. "Get some rest. The next few days will not be kind." He glanced at the dark water moving alongside them. "Tomorrow morning. Well. This morning." The correction was acknowledged without apology. "Sleep. The river carries us for now." 
He turned, leaving them to murmur among themselves. The wind picked up slightly, brushing through the deck with a scent of river moss and the wild tang of dew-drenched trees rising along the banks. 
Luca had not moved during any of it. He stood where he had been when the briefing began, at the edge of the group, close enough to hear everything and far enough to watch everyone. When Harmon finished, he did not murmur with the others. He was quiet in the particular way of a man who has already decided what he thinks and sees no use in announcing it. He pulled his hood slightly lower and looked north, upriver, toward the dark where the pass would be waiting. 
Owls called distantly, and frogs sang in lazy rhythm somewhere beneath the reeds. The boat moved on, and the wilderness swallowed it. As the others began to slip into a quiet routine, unrolling bedrolls, tending to gear, or simply slumping down where they were, Nyssa didn’t. She lingered at the edge of the group, arms crossed, face half-hidden beneath the shadow of her hood.
She materialized beside Harmon the way she always did, with the ease of someone who had never been told no in a way that stuck. 
"You know most men find night watches more enjoyable with company," she said.
"I am not most men."
"No." She considered him with something that was partly assessment and partly amusement. "That has been established."
She let the silence run a moment, which was not her instinct, which meant she was deciding something. "There is something I need to tell you. I would have told you sooner, but there was never a quiet moment."
"There is one now."
"There is." She looked out at the water. "There is a man named Riven. He knows Stormhaven the way I know people. From the inside, through the back, via routes that do not officially exist." The performance in her voice was present, but doing less work than usual. "He would open that city for us in ways I cannot do alone."
"Where is he?"
"Forgehallow. Locked up." A pause. "Before you say it, yes, I know. But Riven in a cell is worth more than most people walking free."
"That is the professional case," Harmon said. "What is the other one?"
She looked at him then. Measuring. "You are very good at that."
"I have been doing it a long time."
Another silence. When she broke it, the flirtation was simply gone, like a coat she had taken off. "He is family. Not blood. The kind that means more than blood because you chose it." She said it to the water, not to him. "I was heading to Forgehallow before this job came along. I thought I could do both. Get him out. Do the work. Come out the other side with everyone I care about still breathing."
"And you need me to make that possible."
"I need you to make a small detour that also happens to get us a very good thief." The lightness tried to come back. It only made it halfway. "I am not above asking. Or begging, if it came to that. Though I would prefer not to beg. It ruins the mystique."
Harmon looked at her for a long moment. "Get some rest."
"Is that…"
"It means get some rest, Nyssa."
She studied his face, found whatever she was looking for, and pushed off the rail. "Thank you," she said. Not lightly. Not with anything on top of it. Just the words.
He turned back to the river. "Goodnight."
She went. He stayed. He thought, there is still more to that story. He filed it and watched the water.
He turned and looked back at the water crashing up against the side of the boat, aware that this mission carried more than it showed. Either this was the beginning of something bold or the final chapter. He slid down to sit there, leaning against the railing, listening to the water.
