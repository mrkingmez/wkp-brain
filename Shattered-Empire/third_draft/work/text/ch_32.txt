The capital rose before them like a god made of stone. Stormhaven was no city. It was a fortress carved into the mountainside, its layered walls draped in fog of the Blackspine’s lower ridges. The outer bulwarks were wide enough to ride five horses across, ringed in silver wards that shimmered faintly beneath the overcast sky. Spires from the War Colleges jutted above the fog like the teeth of a slumbering titan. Beyond them, the burnished copper dome of the Eternal Forge exhaled its eternal smoke into the pale sky. The city didn't breathe so much as seethe, a pressure chamber barely containing its secrets.

No one spoke as they sat astride their horses in the tree line. Garr’s gauntlet creaked as he tightened his grip on the reins, the only sound anyone dared make. The smell of damp pine lingered under the ozone bite of distant wards. Even Weslin, who fidgeted at the best of times, sat rigid in the saddle, his wide eyes fixed on the behemoth of stone and magic before them.

“Hard to sneak into a mountain that doesn’t mean to let you in,” Garr muttered, his eyes still on the walls. 

“We’re not going through the mountain,” Riven said, guiding his horse toward a crumbled path off the main trade road. “We’re going under it.”

They followed in single file through twisted trees and a half-collapsed stonework path that hadn't felt wagon wheels in a generation. The fog thickened as the elevation dipped, giving way to wild growth and choking vines. Eventually, they reached a weatherworn statue, half-swallowed by the forest. Moss clung to its legs, and ivy had claimed its outstretched hand.

It depicted a long-forgotten general, sword buried in the earth at his feet. No plaque. No name. Just stone eyes that gazed forever toward the city he’d died defending.

“This is it,” Riven said quietly. “This shaft was abandoned before the Guild cracked down. Too many cave-ins. Too many dead smugglers. Even the undercrawl stopped trusting it.”

Riven stepped behind the plinth, found the hilt of the buried sword by feel, and turned it once. He'd done it a hundred times and never once felt like the city deserved to keep its secrets. A click, then a grind of stone. Behind the statue, a slab of ground hissed open, revealing a metal grate veiled in rotting leaves.

“You really were a rat once, weren’t you?” Ash said, a crooked grin flashing before it faded.

“I prefer the term ‘urban opportunist,’” Riven said, smirking. “Help me with this.”

Ash and Garr moved in to help lift the grate. The hinges creaked, resisting. Beneath it: a dark stone shaft and a ladder disappearing into blackness.

“Old smuggler path,” Riven said. “Used by the fences and hawkers who didn’t want to pay stormgate tolls. Led straight to the criminal quarters undercrawl.”

Harmon dismounted, eyes sweeping the surrounding trees. “We’ll leave the horses here. Tether them under the low canopy. Garr, brush out our approach. If anyone finds them, they’ll think traders camped too close to the wall.”

They moved quickly. Weslin hesitated, one hand brushing the mane of his horse.

“It’s watching us,” he whispered.

Aurelia, already beside him, placed a calming hand on his shoulder. “It’s just the weight of the place. Cities like this remember things.”

Weslin nodded but didn’t look convinced.

One by one, they descended the ladder. The metal was slick with condensation, and every rung a measured echo. Harmon went last, sealing the grate with a quiet clang above him.

Below, the air was thick and stale. The tunnel sloped forward, not down, just enough to keep the moisture flowing along a central channel carved into the floor.

They passed through a crumbling arch into a wider passage. The walls were rough-hewn stone braced with wooden support beams, some still sturdy, others gnawed by time and rot. The silence was oppressive. Even their footfalls seemed dampened.

Then they reached it.

The Underlane.

What had once been a low, vaulted market tunnel now sprawled before them in eerie silence. Abandoned stalls lined the corridor like the bones of a long-dead beast. Aurelia slowed, fingertips brushing the stone as if feeling for echoes that simply weren’t there. Rusted cages. Torn awnings. Mugs tipped over mid-drink. A streak of dried blood arced across the flagstone like a final scream frozen in motion.

Ash crouched by an overturned table. “Whatever happened here, it wasn’t slow.”

Riven stood at the center of the lane, slowly turning in place. His usual swagger was gone. He'd stood here a hundred times with coin in his pocket and nowhere to be. He'd watched bards play drunk, and fences argue over gem weights, and men with bounties drink beside the men hunting them. It had been dirty and dishonest and alive. Whatever this place was now, it wasn't that. 

Dain’s hand drifted to his blade, not drawing it, just grounding himself. “This place used to be full at all hours. Fences hawking cut gems. Bards, playing drunk. Hell, there’s a bar two tunnels down that only serves criminals with bounties.”

“No rats,” Garr noted, scanning the corners. “Not even vermin.”

Aurelia’s voice was soft, but cold. “It’s been emptied deliberately.”

They pressed forward, Riven now quieter, as the unease of the surrounding setting took hold, guiding them through a maze of offshoot tunnels. One led to a flooded hall. Another storage room was looted bare. At last, they reached a wider chamber with a stacked crate in the center.

Riven nodded to it. “This is the lift-up. They sealed the main shafts after clearing the undercrawl. Smaller ones like this get overlooked.”

Ash and Garr helped move the crate. Beneath, a hatch opened to reveal a short ladder and a dim shaft above.

“This’ll bring us out on the edge of the working quarter,” Riven explained. “Not our final stop. Just a shadow to hide in while I work.”

They headed up one by one. Dain went first. Garr leaned over to Harmon and said quietly, “We should check this out more. There is more here than we can see at first glance.” 

“I agree, but later,” replied Harmon. Then the two of them ascended the ladder.

The team climbed up into the back room of a dusty warehouse, its windows painted shut, the air thin with the scent of old grain and oil.

As they filed out into the light of day, the city greeted them like a tomb wearing a mask of a parade.

From here, Stormhaven was unnervingly silent. The guild halls stood tall but still. Market awnings hung undisturbed. In the distance, a squad of Veydrassil marched past, uniform, mechanical, glowing brands on their skin.

Ash tensed. “Those aren’t mercs.”

“No,” Harmon said quietly. “That’s Veydrassil.”

Ash watched them go, “So that’s what they look like without their red cloaks.”

Riven lowered his hood. “Let’s move. The longer we stand still, the more this city breathes us in.” Like shadows in a city full of ghosts, they slipped into the streets. A lone bootstep echoed somewhere down the block, too distant to place, too sharp to ignore.

Stormhaven’s merchant quarter should have buzzed with vendors shouting over cracked wheels and mismatched coin. Instead, silence clung to the cobblestones. The sky was overcast, muting the light, and the chill of the mountains hung close to the skin. The only sound was the rhythmic clack of military boots, measured, exact.

They stood just inside the shadow of a boarded-over entryway, the team stacked tight behind warped beams and frost-brittle crates.

“This is wrong,” Garr murmured. “Even on a bad day, you’d hear beggars or street fights.”

“They’ve scrubbed the city,” Aurelia said, watching the distant patrols with narrowed eyes. “Not just the dirt. The noise. The color. It’s like someone washed all the people clean.”

Weslin nodded slowly beside her, lips pressed together. “The walls feel... full. Like too many thoughts echoing at once.”

“You picking anything up?” Harmon asked.

“No specific thoughts. Just... pressure.” Weslin rubbed at his temples. “It’s like trying to hear through fog.”

“Same,” agreed Aurelia.

Harmon said nothing, but he noted it, just as he had done with everything else.

Across the street, a group of five Red Cloaks turned the corner.

They moved with uncanny symmetry, their scarlet cloaks catching the gray light like blood spilled across snow. Each one wore reinforced leather, fitted to move, not withstand siege. At their sides hung maces, hand-crossbows, and obsidian-hilted daggers. Some bore quarterstaves etched with carved personal sigils, not true runes, just marks of allegiance or mimicry of ancient power they didn’t fully understand.

Their faces were calm, distant, and empty. Beneath their cloaks, each wore a small shard-embedded pendant or ring, the faint glow of ether drawn from the leylines barely visible to the trained eye. The magic was always there, just beneath the skin, waiting to be unleashed.

Ash watched them pass without moving. 

“They’re Adepts,” he muttered. “Not footmen. That one on the end? Drawing from a red shard. I can feel the fire in his aura.”

“They all carry shards,” Aurelia confirmed. “Tiny threads, but constant. They’re channeling just enough to stay ready.”

Ash tilted his head. “So they’re all tuned up. Like walking powder kegs.”

“Ain't just fanatics,” Neavin said, not looking away from the street. “Somebody's been training 'em. Feeding 'em power on a schedule. You can feel it." 

“Which makes them dangerous,” Aurelia added softly.

“They’re Veydrassil,” Harmon said matter-of-factly. “Followers of Lucent. Fanatics.”

Dain clicked his tongue. “Bold of them to wear the cloaks now. Used to be only in back alleys or cult hideouts.”

“They’re not hiding anymore,” Riven said. “They’re occupying.”
Harmon took in all the sights and lack of sounds. He muttered under his breath, eyes sweeping the unnaturally quiet streets. “Whatever was burning through this place… It’s already done its work.”
The team said nothing, but the weight of his words hung over them as they moved. Even the wind seemed to hush around the stone alleys, like the city itself held its breath.
Riven stopped at a narrow intersection and gestured to a modest two-story home pressed between a shuttered bakery and a boarded-up apothecary. The exterior was plain, with weathered paint and vines creeping up the sides, but the small iron lantern above the door still had a wick in it, unlit, but telling.
“Here,” Riven said. “Safe enough for now.”
Dain raised an eyebrow. “Charming. You sure it’s empty?”
“Used to belong to a bookmaker who got caught skimming from the Guild,” Riven said. He paused, watching the street through a crack in the curtain. “They've already checked it. Word is he fled. The Guild stripped it clean. No ledgers, no coin trail. Just dust and bad luck. So I took it.”
Nyssa gave him a look. “You really do have a place in every city, don’t you?”
He winked at her. “Comes with the job. Or whatever this job is now.”
The lock clicked open, and the team slipped inside, one by one.
The interior was dusty but intact, plain furniture, a cold hearth, a small upstairs room with a pair of narrow beds, and an attic crawlspace. Nyssa nudged a dusty chair with her boot. “At least it doesn’t smell like corpses or mold. I’ll take it.” The curtains were moth-bitten but drawn. There was no food, no signs of recent habitation, but more importantly, no sign of watchers.
“Better than I expected,” Aldrek admitted, checking the window slits from the upper room. “Limited lines of sight, good vantage if we’re pressed.”
“It’ll hold,” Harmon said. “But it’s just a stop. We’ll rest here for a moment, regroup, and move out before sundown. We still need something more permanent.”
Riven was already at the door, hood drawn. 

“Where are you going?” Harmon asked.

“Securing the place I told you about. This house, I know the owner and we can use it for short stops. Trust me, it will have a bed or two.”

“You’re not going alone,” Harmon said. He turned to the group. “Ash. Go with him.”

Ash cracked his neck. “Figured I’d get the quiet shift. Guess not.”

“Keep your hoods up,” Garr added. “And don’t look anyone in the eye.”

“Wouldn't dream of it,” Riven muttered. The pair slipped out the door a moment later. 

Once they were gone, the rest of the team began fanning out through the upper floor of the house. They kept the shutters tight, watching the city through narrow slats. Each aperture offered a slightly different view of the surrounding blocks: a cloth vendor with no wares, a tanner’s shop locked tight, and a side alley that remained perfectly clean, no footprints, no refuse, no scent.

Garr stood beside one window, arms crossed.

“Never liked cities,” he said. “You can’t see what’s coming.”

“You can feel it, though,” Aldrek replied quietly, standing nearby. “Like a prayer left unanswered.”

Dain lounged in a wooden chair near the map table, one boot up, blade across his lap. “This whole place reeks of performance. It’s a stage, and everyone’s been given a script.”

Nyssa leaned beside the other window, watching a woman slowly sweeping a spotless stoop. “Except nobody looks like they want the part.”

“You see that?” Aurelia said, pointing across the next street over. “Man in the blue tunic. He’s been standing at that corner for four minutes. Hasn’t moved.”

Weslin flinched. “He’s not thinking. I mean, I don’t feel anything from him.”

“He’s not alone,” Neavin added, leaning over her shoulder. “Two more like him by the well. Same posture. Same blank stares.”
A sudden whistle cut through the stillness. Not loud. Not panicked. Structured.
Across the square, one of the Red Cloaks lifted two fingers. The patrol at the far end of the street pivoted in perfect unison and began moving inward, tightening their sweep by half a block.
Garr shifted his weight slightly toward the door. “They’re adjusting.”
“No,” Aurelia murmured. “They’re compressing.”
“They’ve been narrowing the grid since we surfaced,” Aurelia said quietly. “Look at the intervals. It’s district-wide. They are looking for someone.”
Bootsteps multiplied. Not rushed. Not frantic. But deliberate. The patrol pattern had shifted. Where there had been two rotations per street, there were now three.
Harmon didn’t move from the window. “We’ve been mapped,” he said quietly. “Not found. Mapped.”
The woman with the broom stopped sweeping mid-stroke and stepped back inside without looking up.
The street grew smaller.
Reau finally spoke from where he stood in the corner, arms folded.

“Something hollowed this place out,” he said, gaze fixed on the street below. “And left the shell standing.”

His voice was cold. Measured. But something in it told Harmon he’d been here before, in a different city, under a different chain.

They waited, the silence pressing tighter with each passing breath. They rotated shifts, watching out the windows. A shadow slid across a nearby shutter, but it was only a torn banner fluttering loose from a hook. Nyssa noted a pair of Red Cloaks entering a house and not coming out. Dain counted two patrols per street, moving in rhythmic intervals.

Then, without warning, Neavin stiffened near the far window.

“Rooftop,” he said. “Southeast. Movement.”

Harmon moved to his side. On a tall tenement across the way, the shadow of a figure in dark robes leaned out, watching. Then, just as quickly, it was gone.

“You think it was one of theirs?” Garr asked.

“Don’t know,” Harmon said. “It's hard to tell anymore.”

Weslin swallowed. “They know we’re here?”

“Not yet,” Harmon said, turning back to the others. “But we’re not the only ones walking this city with secrets.”

They fell into silence again.

Below, the woman finished sweeping, nothing, and stepped back inside. Across the square, a Red Cloak stood beneath a hanging lantern that hadn’t been lit in days. His staff rested beside him, one gauntleted hand twitching every few seconds as if something was poking it.
Across the square, the man in the blue tunic stiffened. He had not moved in minutes. Without warning, his body jerked.
Once.
Twice.
He dropped to his knees like someone had cut a string. No scream. No cry for help. Just collapse.
The Red Cloak beneath the lantern did not rush. He simply turned his head slightly, as if listening to something only he could hear. A faint shimmer pulsed at the pendant beneath his collar.
The man on the cobblestones inhaled sharply, eyes wide, and then… stilled. He stood. Returned to his corner. Resumed the same blank stare.
Weslin went pale. “That wasn’t fainting,” he whispered. “That was… correction.” No one disagreed. No one moved for a long moment. Someone's hand had found a blade hilt and hadn't let go. 
Inside the townhouse, the team began to write down observations and patrol timings. They noted other odd movements and markings around the area, no detail too small. It was how you learned to breathe behind enemy lines, one note at a time.

Somewhere in the city, Ash and Riven were still moving. Harmon didn't let himself think about where. 

And above them all, Stormhaven loomed like a memory that refused to die.

The door creaked open just enough for Riven to slip inside, followed by Ash, hood drawn low, eyes sharp. Both men paused in the entryway, checking the windows before they spoke.

Riven shut the door quietly. “It’s weirder than you even think.”

Ash moved to the nearest wall slit and took a slow breath. “Whole city’s moving like it’s got someone else’s heartbeat.”

Harmon stood. “Talk.”

They all gathered near the crude table in the center of the townhouse loft, hands resting on blades, arms folded, waiting. The only light came from a single shuttered lantern, flickering across the old wood and maps laid out beneath it. 

Riven leaned forward. “It’s not a cell. It’s a hand, closing.”

Neavin looked at the floor, shaking his head once. 

Dain frowned. “Veydrassil? Occupying? Does it seem like them? I thought they only did hit-and-runs.”

“Not hit-and-run.” Riven set his hands flat on the table. “Full occupation. A hundred Adepts, all in their red. A thousand soldiers across the districts. They've taken the plazas, every choke point. The city has been taken." 

“They're dug in everywhere,” Ash added. “They’re using the city's own supplies. Every stall, every cart, every warehouse is under their command, used to feed this host.”

Harmon said, “They are ensuring no one notices for as long as they can. Supply trains draw eyes.”

Riven nodded. “And they’re embedding. We’ve already seen a dozen checkpoints between districts. They’re establishing lanes of movement.”

Harmon’s jaw clenched, but he said nothing yet. His fingers drummed once on the table, the only sign he was absorbing the weight of it all.

Riven leaned back slightly. “Red Cloaks. Full uniforms, soldiers. They are not hiding. They’re setting up curfews and redistributing supplies. Ration lines are active near the plazas. We saw one force a merchant to sign over her stall to the city."

“And she agreed?” Garr asked.

“Didn’t flinch,” Ash said. “Like she’d been waiting to.”

Aurelia’s brow creased. “That’s not compliance. That’s conditioning.”

Reau stood in the back, arms folded, silent as the mountain.

Neavin rubbed his jaw. “So we’re not looking at a rogue cult. We’re looking at organized state-backed subversion.”

“Well,” Dain said under his breath, “that escalated quickly.”

Nyssa glanced out the slit again. “How long until they find us?”

“They won’t,” Riven said. “Not where we’re going.”

He gestured to the door. “Get your gear. I found us a real place.”

Harmon eyed him. “Better than this?”

“Much,” Riven said. 

Harmon gave a slow nod. “Lead on.”

They slipped out just after dark, cutting south through a pair of back alleys and across a maintenance ditch shielded by overgrown vines. Riven led them without hesitation, weaving them through a neighborhood that had once been part of the artisan ward, now hollow and silent. 

He led them deeper into the artisan quarter, where cobbled alleys gave way to polished stone and narrow thoroughfares lined with shuttered shops. In a wealthier time, this would’ve been the heartbeat of commerce, guilds, forges, and scriveners. Now, even the lanterns seemed to burn at half strength.

They arrived at a quiet, run-down training stable, tucked behind a blacksmith’s annex. The smell of old hay and charcoal clung to the walls.

“Here?” Dain said, raising an eyebrow.

Riven only smiled. “Upstairs.”

The loft above the stables was spacious and surprisingly intact. Thick timber rafters supported a low ceiling. There were two narrow windows and four discreetly drilled peepholes in the walls, offering a view in every direction. The air was dry, warmer than expected.
A horn sounded in the distance. Low. Single note. Then another. Riven froze mid-step. “That’s not a city guard,” he said.
Bootsteps echoed below in the street outside the stable. Not patrol cadence. Heavier. Coordinated. Neavin moved to one of the peepholes and leaned in carefully. “Red Cloaks,” he said. “Eight. No, ten.”
They moved door to door across the artisan lane, knocking once. Then entering without a reply. One shopkeeper opened before they knocked. He bowed before they reached him. They still went inside. Ash’s expression hardened. “That’s a sweep.”
“Routine?” Dain asked.
“Can’t tell, normally I would say no,” Riven said quietly. “That looked like a search and confirm.”
The horn sounded again. Closer this time. Harmon did not raise his voice. “Positions. Quiet.” The stable loft that had felt like shelter a moment ago now felt like a ceiling too low. 
“This’ll do,” Garr muttered, nodding while walking into the loft area.

Dain stepped forward, inspecting the space like a merchant checking a shipment. “All this for us? I am touched,” Dain said dryly, brushing dust from his sleeve.
“You’re about to be noble again,” Riven said, though his eyes flicked once toward the street below.
Dain exhaled through his nose. “Always the lord, never the lowlife.”
“You kept that outfit because you liked how I looked at you in it,” Nyssa murmured, stepping just close enough to matter.
“Maybe,” Dain replied, though he did not look away from the peephole nearest him.
Ash shifted near the rafters. “If this turns into poetry hour, I’m taking roof watch.”
No one laughed this time. The horn sounded again. Closer. Riven’s smile faded entirely. “Keep it short,” he said quietly. “They’re sweeping.”
Nyssa’s teasing edge softened. “Then we’ll flirt when the city isn’t listening.”
Dain gave her one last glance, the levity still there, but thinner now. Below them, the patrol shifted against the stone. The moment folded back into discipline. 
Coming from outside, the first bell tolled just as the light drained from the sky. Deep. Resonant. Not the bright peal of ceremony. Not the rolling cadence of festival. This was iron striking iron.
A pause.
Then a second toll. Across the district, shutters snapped closed. A lantern was extinguished mid-street. A child’s voice cut off mid-laugh somewhere beyond the alley.
Dain looked toward the sound. “That’s not a prayer call.”
“No,” Aldrek said softly. “That’s curfew.”
A third toll rolled through the stone like a verdict.
Harmon folded the map slowly. “They own the night.”

Reau stood near the window, quiet as ever, though the corners of his mouth twitched slightly.

“Enough,” Harmon said, cutting through the levity. “Let’s get to work.”

They moved with discipline, rolling out blankets, assigning peephole coverage, and clearing a central table for operations. Nyssa brought out oil and cloth to polish the exposed hinges on the trapdoor, muttering that she'd rather not hear every mouse fart beneath them.

Harmon reached into his satchel and drew out a thick roll of parchment. “At least this time I brought the map.”

He unfurled a detailed floor plan and neighborhood schematic of the city. Old streets, storm drains, and sewer lines crisscrossed with notes in Harmon’s own tight, angular script.

“Spread out your notes. Everything we’ve observed so far. List them all here, fires, patrols, civilian reactions, and district lines. This becomes our log.”

Riven pointed to the westward alley on the map. “That’s where we saw one of the worst zones. Burned carts. Alley full of blood. They dragged someone off, and no one screamed.”

“They’re breaking people,” Aurelia said quietly. “It’s psychological warfare.”

Harmon looked around the group. “This is not a local riot. This is an occupation. Treat it like one.”

He tapped the map.

“We’re here to get intel. Not to free the city. Not yet. Our job is to determine three things: what caused this, who’s running it, and where it started. We need to see if our force is walking into a trap or if they can free this city.”

He looked each of them in the eye.

“Until then, observe. Infiltrate. Survive. And get out quietly.”

There was a moment of silence. Then nods all around. Weslin went over to the far end of the loft and nodded. He stood near them for a good ten minutes before he made his way back to Harmon's side. “Sir?” Harmon was taking in everything going on around him, lost in his thoughts. “Harmon,” Weslin tried again, voice rising. Still nothing.

Reau watched the scene for a moment. Then his eyes settled on Weslin. 

Weslin glanced over. Reau was already watching. He mouthed the words. 'Your big boy voice.' Then he gave a single nod. “Sir!” Weslin said much louder this time, breaking Harmon's concentration and pulling his attention back.

“What is it, boy?” asked Harmon.

“I received a message from Luca. He has news and a plan. He wants your input.”

For the next fifteen minutes, the two stood over the map table. Weslin spoke quickly and efficiently. “The tunnel is viable for movement and safety. Not transport. Not yet. He reinforced what he could.”
Harmon nodded once.
“The chapel won’t last long,” Weslin continued. “The bombardment is shifting west. The stone above the outer wall is starting to fracture. If that quadrant collapses, the civilians lose their last cover.”
“And the gate?” Harmon asked.
“Still jammed.”
A beat.
“The orcs aren’t pushing,” Weslin added. “They’re adjusting formation. Luca thinks they’re preparing for something coordinated.”
The words settled into the room.
“One more thing.” Weslin swallowed once. “He gave his word. Told them they would break the siege.”
No one spoke after that. “How long?” Harmon asked.
“Two days, at most,” Weslin replied. “Less if the bombardment intensifies.”
Harmon stared down at Stormhaven’s map, fingers resting against inked streets that suddenly felt too far from stone and blood.
“Tell him,” he said evenly, “reinforce the tunnel entrance. Prepare the wounded in waves.”
“Yes, sir.” Weslin stepped away to send the reply.
The loft felt tighter now. Stormhaven was closing its fist. And somewhere beyond the mountains, another clock was running. Riven walked over to him, “Harmon. The horses. We're running out of light. ”
“Right,” said Harmon. He scanned the room. “Garr. Weslin. Neavin. Aldrek. Aurelia,” Harmon said, as he turned. “You five, retrieve the horses. Curfew’s already in effect. Patrols are running everywhere. If you’re spotted, you abort. No heroics. We lose the horses before we lose you.”
Aldrek stepped forward, voice steady. “Or we take them clean. Fast and decisive. A knight does not abandon his mount to the enemy.” Neavin shrugged from the doorframe. “If we can't get back, we don't. Sleep rough with the horses, bring 'em in the morning when the patrols thin. Ain't no different from the mine roads.”
He took a moment to write something and then called Dain over.

“Dain, sign this as the lord. Do you know what name you’re going with this time? It should be different.”

“I was thinking along the lines of Lord Theren Vayle, a minor lord who deals in horses,” replied Dain.

Harmon then said, “Good, now create yourself a cover story. Do you need documents for that?”

Riven stepped in, “He will need a bill of sale for the horse we will put downstairs and a writ to purchase horses in the city.”

Dain pointed at Riven. “What he said." 

Harmon said, “I will get started, of course, with Nyssa's help.” He handed Garr the signed pass. “This should get you and the horse in and give you clear passage back, if we have to use the morning to do it.”

Garr grabbed the document, and within seconds, they all left.

Back in the loft, Dain dropped a weather-stained satchel on the table with a dull thud. “We’re going to need a normal. Schedules. Watch rotations. Guard rotations. Contacts.”

“I’ll start mapping patrols,” Ash said.

“I’ve got eyes east,” Dain added, peering through a peephole. “Nothing but lanterns and footsteps.”

“Let’s hope it stays that way,” Aurelia murmured.

Reau, arms crossed, said, “We’ll need codewords. If they’re listening.”

“They always are,” Nyssa said softly.

Harmon's fingers traced part of the map. “We'll make this our stronghold. And if they're really bold enough to come here…” He looked at each of them once. “Then they'll learn we're not just passing through." 

He said nothing else. He didn't need to.
