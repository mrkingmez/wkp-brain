The air was cold but not biting, just enough to carry the memory of last night’s frost in the lungs. A thin cover of cloud stretched across the sky, muting the sun to a dull, pale disk that barely colored the afternoon. After nearly fourteen hours of hard riding, the camp had finally taken shape in the crook of a narrow, wooded valley, nestled beneath a scrim of trees that did little to break the northern wind.

Inside Harmon’s tent, the air was thick with quiet dread.

Weslin sat beside the cot, his small frame curled slightly forward, fingertips resting on the back of Harmon’s hand. The older man lay still, chest rising shallowly beneath his blanket, a glisten of sweat dampening his brow despite the cool. Aurelia sat nearby, quietly sorting fresh linens and tonic supplies, her eyes shifting between Harmon and the boy with a faint crease of worry marring her brow.

“He’s breathing steady,” Weslin muttered, almost to himself. “But it's too steady, like he’s stuck. Like nothing’s moving forward.”

“Still no change?” Garr’s voice broke the hush, gravel-thick and low.

Weslin shook his head, lips pale. “No fever, no convulsions. But nothing else either. He hasn’t flinched, or stirred, or blinked. It’s like he’s... not here.”

Garr hovered near the flap of the tent, his arms crossed in a pose he hoped looked like calm leadership. In truth, he felt like a man treading water in armor, every decision heavier than the last. He was trying not to let it show, but it was eating at him.

“We don’t know what kind of toll that magic took,” Garr said, aiming for reassurance and missing by a hair. “But you kept him breathing. You watched the numbers. That matters. Don’t let your head tell you otherwise.”

Weslin’s voice cracked. “I’m not even sure I’m doing that right. I’m not a real medic, I’m not even really a squire! Harmon just picked me because I knocked over a suit of armor or something, and now he’s like this, and I don’t know what I’m supposed to do.”
“He picked you,” Garr went on, quieter now, firmer. “Not anyone else. You. From what I’ve seen, you’re doing exactly what you’re meant to do. You’re watching your knight. You’re holding the line where it counts.”
He paused, just long enough to let it land.
“No training ever prepares you for what the field takes from you. But you’re here, without any of that, and your instincts haven’t failed you yet.”


Aurelia looked up, her tone soft but firm. “You’re doing better than you think, Weslin. But you can’t hold your breath every second waiting for him to wake up. That won’t help him, and it won’t help you.”

Weslin looked down at his hands. “I just… I keep thinking, what if we did something wrong? What if he’s like this because I missed something?”

“You didn’t,” Garr said, a little too quickly. He shifted, exhaling slowly. “We’re all shaken. No one’s untouched by that break. We got out, but not without cost.”

Weslin’s eyes flicked toward a folded parchment beside him, one of several. “Luca’s team hasn’t heard back from us in nearly a day. They’ve sent three messages. They're asking for guidance, orders... anything. What should I tell them?”

Garr hesitated. That hesitation was a crack in the armor, and he felt it immediately, felt how closely it was being watched. He swallowed it down and straightened.
Weslin had been watching him and spoke again. "The messages from Luca's team," he said carefully. 
Garr didn't answer right away. He moved to the small folding table near the tent wall and turned the stack of folded parchments face down without reading them.
"What do you want me to tell them?" Weslin pressed.
"Nothing," Garr said. "Not yet."
"But what if—"
“I said ignore them,” Garr replied without looking back. “Right now, if they don’t think they’re on their own already, they’re lying to themselves. We’ve got five fresh bodies out there with no shelter, Harmon’s down, and if we were followed…” He trailed off, the unspoken consequence heavy in the air. “We need to lock this down first.”
Weslin opened his mouth, closed it, then opened it again as a stiff gust slapped the tent wall and made the poles tremble. “But what if they are in danger? What if they are waiting on something that could save their lives?”

Garr turned slowly to face him. “Then they’ll do what we’ve all done. Improvise. Adapt. Survive.”

Weslin looked like he might argue again, his eyes glassy with the threat of something rising, but Aurelia put a calming hand on his arm.

Garr exhaled through his nose. A muffled shout echoed from somewhere outside, sharp enough to make his brow tighten. He pushed out through the tent flap into the daylight, the weight of command sitting heavier than ever on his shoulders.

Weslin went back and sat beside Harmon, knees drawn in, shoulders hunched. His fingers twitched nervously on the cot’s edge, eyes locked on Harmon’s still face like the boy feared if he blinked, the man might vanish.

Aurelia sat cross-legged across the tent from him, her long robes gathered neatly beneath her. Her brow furrowed with concern, not just for Harmon, but for the boy silently unraveling in front of her.

“Weslin,” she said gently, her voice as calm as falling rain. “We need to start your next lesson.”

His head snapped up. “Now? I—Aurelia, I don’t think I can right now.”

She kept her voice soft, not pressing. “That’s why we start now. When your mind’s scattered, when fear creeps in… That’s when you need your training most.”

He shook his head. “You don’t understand. I was just picked as a squire, just a boy playing at armor and maps. I’m not ready for this. I don’t even know what all my duties are supposed to be.” His voice cracked a little, and he clenched his fists. “I’m not sure I know who I am anymore. What does my power even mean? Or what I’m supposed to do with it.”

She leaned forward, hands clasped in her lap. “That, my dear boy, is exactly why we do this.”

He blinked at her.

Aurelia set two fingers briefly at his wrist, steady and precise, before letting her hand fall. “You trust in the people close to you. Not just the team, but those who invest in you. You trust in the gods who shape the breath in your chest. And when it gets quiet enough… You trust in your training.”

“But that’s just it,” he whispered. “I don’t have training.”

Aurelia gave him a faint smile, warm and sad all at once. “You’ve had it since the beginning. Who do you think set up this tent? Who’s been tracking vitals, organizing notes, maintaining order in a storm?” She nodded toward the folded parchments beside him. “Those are message logs you’ve been keeping. Look at the way they’re ordered, the precision. That’s not a child’s work.”

He frowned slightly, looking at the stack.

“You did that,” she said. “And in the prison, you kept your head. You moved with the team. You got out. You protected others.” Her voice softened even more. “Now we need to focus on something that steadies your nerves. You need that. We need that.”

His lip trembled just a little. “I don’t know how.”

“That’s what I’m here for,” she said. “You’re not alone, Weslin. Let’s begin.”

He gave a small nod, uncertain but willing.

“Close your eyes,” she said. “Picture a candle. Tall and steady. Just you and the flame.”

His eyes shut. He inhaled slowly, but his shoulders were still tight.

“Now,” Aurelia continued, “imagine your fear. Your doubt. One by one, toss them into the fire. Watch them burn. Let them go.”

Silence. His brow furrowed. His fingers curled. “It’s not working,” he muttered.

“Good,” she replied. “Failure is part of it.” A faint rustle outside brushed the canvas as if something passed close, but Aurelia kept her focus on him.

He sighed, shoulders drooping. "Keep going." She said it quietly, like it was already fact. 

He took another breath. This one was deeper. Slower. Again. And again. Until something eased. “I see it,” he whispered. “The fire. It’s just me and the flame.”

Aurelia’s voice grew softer, steadier. “Good. Now reach. Listen for the hum beneath the world. That’s where the power lies. Draw it in, not forcefully. Like pulling a thread.”

A flicker of light danced in his vision. Faint. Brief. Then it sputtered. He sighed, shoulders drooping.

She gave no reproach. “Again.”

This time, the light lingered. Faint as a breath, but steady.

Aurelia smiled. “How do you feel?”

He opened his eyes slowly. “Safe.”

“Good,” she said, touching a hand to his shoulder. “Now let’s begin your first spell.”

He sat up straighter.

“It’s not complicated, but it’s important,” she said. “It’s called True Eyes. My first spell. It allows you to see through illusion, through glamours and lies woven in magic. In this world, there will be many.”

He nodded. A small light now kindled behind his eyes. Not certainty, but something close. Hope? Weslin’s eyes went to Harmon without thinking. Aurelia followed the look, brief and measured, and then returned her attention to Weslin. “You are not casting,” she said quietly. “You are observing. Nothing here will touch him.”


The air was colder outside the tent, but not enough to chase the weight from Garr’s shoulders. He stood for a moment just beneath the flap, arms folded, letting the dull gray light catch across his worn features. Inside, he could still hear Aurelia’s calm, instructive tone weaving through the quiet tension of Harmon’s sickroom. She spoke gently but firmly, coaxing Weslin through some kind of focusing exercise, something to settle the boy’s frayed nerves.

Garr didn’t move. He just stood there, letting her words anchor him. A breath in. A breath out. Then another. He couldn’t show what he was feeling. Not now. They were all watching him. He stepped out into the open.

The camp was quiet, too quiet. No laughter, no idle banter. Just the crunch of feet on frost-bitten grass, the rustle of canvas, and the occasional soft clink of armor being adjusted. The weight of the last two days was visible in every movement, every look exchanged.

Garr turned his gaze toward the row of lean-tos near the edge of the clearing. Aldrek knelt beside a stump, wrapping a bandage around the wrist of a pale, wiry man with dark hair pulled into a tight knot, Torrek Mull, the earth-digger. Beside him sat Haldrin Vance, the aging scholar with ink still smudged on his sleeves, sipping from a tin cup with trembling hands.

He’d thought he was ready for this. He’d trained under Harmon for years, learned tactics, logistics, and leadership. But training wasn’t the same as living it. Not like this. Not with Harmon out cold and the whole weight of the company pressing down on him.

He was swimming upstream. And everyone was watching, waiting for him to slip.

He crossed to the center firepit and found Dain standing near the lean-tos, arms crossed, jaw tight.

As he made his way towards Dain, he scanned the camp again. A few yards off, Calen, the younger of the two rescued swordsmen, stood near the fire, posture stiff, eyes scanning the horizon like he was still behind enemy lines. He wasn’t speaking much, but he wasn't avoiding the others either. Just… watching.

Tarn, on the other hand, had made himself a ghost within the camp. He sat alone on the far side, cloak drawn tight, head down as if daring someone to approach. Nobody did.

“You look like someone poured a barrel of stress into a man-shaped container,” Dain said, his voice low, pitched so it wouldn’t carry.
Garr let out a hollow chuckle. It barely reached his eyes. “Feels about right.”
“Still no change?”
“No,” Garr said after a beat. “Weslin’s barely slept. Aurelia’s in there now. Trying to keep his head straight.” He hesitated, then added, quieter, “Trying to keep him from tearing himself apart.”
Dain exhaled through his nose. “I know it’s weighing on the kid. He’ll be holding it in, telling himself it’s fine. But it leaks. Always does.”
“He’s not the only one.”
A cold breeze slid between the tents, carrying the smell of ash and pine. Somewhere deeper in camp, a fire popped. They stood without speaking for a moment, the silence doing the work words couldn’t.
“I thought I’d have more time,” Garr said finally. “With Harmon. To learn the things that don’t fit in drills or plans.” His fingers flexed once at his side. “The soft parts. The parts you don’t realize you need until someone’s breaking in front of you. How he carries this weight without letting it crush him.”
Dain studied him, then reached out and clapped a hand to Garr’s shoulder. It was firm. Grounding.
“You’re doing fine,” he said. “They don’t need perfection. They need a steady hand. And you’re giving it, whether you believe that or not.”
Garr swallowed, eyes still on the tent flap.
Dain’s expression softened, though his posture stayed straight, controlled. “You’re not alone in this, Garr. But don’t mistake that for relief.” His voice lowered. “It doesn’t get lighter just because others stand beside you. It gets survivable. We carry it together. We carry it now. We’re here. And we’re alive.”
Garr nodded slowly, letting the words settle somewhere behind his ribs.
After a moment, Dain glanced toward the tent again. “I heard you earlier,” he said.
Garr stiffened slightly. “You—”
“I wasn’t listening on purpose,” Dain cut in gently. “I was walking past. But I heard enough.” He paused. “You told him to trust what he was feeling. To listen to himself. Those instincts don’t come from nowhere.”
Garr’s voice came quieter. “He thinks he’s failing. That because he’s scared, he’s doing something wrong.”
“And you told him he wasn’t,” Dain said. “You told him that fear doesn’t mean weakness. It means awareness.” A faint, rueful smile touched his mouth. “Harmon would’ve said the same thing. Just with fewer words and more judgment.”
That earned a small, real breath of a laugh from Garr.
Dain leaned back, gaze drifting across the camp. “You know… for a long time, I thought I was finished.” His tone stayed composed, but something underneath it loosened. “After what I lost, I stopped imagining a future that involved me. I told myself I was just… passing through. Coin, contracts, something to occupy the days until one finally didn’t end.”
He hesitated, then continued. “There were times I thought about disappearing. Not dramatically. Just… taking a job I wouldn’t walk away from.”
Garr turned to look at him fully now.
“But this,” Dain said, gesturing faintly at the tents, the fires, the quiet movement of people holding together, “this has a way of pulling you back. Reminding you that you still matter to someone. That you can still matter.” His jaw set, controlled again. “That old bastard in there gave me that back.”
The tightness in Garr’s chest eased, just a little. “Yeah,” he said. “He did that for all of us.”
After a moment, he added, “I was a mercenary for a while. Before Harmon found me. I told myself it was simpler that way. No attachments. No responsibility beyond the job.” He shook his head faintly. “Turns out that was a lie. I just hadn’t met people worth staying for yet.”
Dain nodded. “Seems we both took the long road to the same place.”
“I guess we’re in it now,” Garr said. “Whether we meant to be or not.”
Dain’s mouth curved slightly. “Seems so.” He paused. “And for what it’s worth… I’m here. If you need to talk again.”
The two men shared a quiet, understanding look. No ceremony. No promises. Just acknowledgment.
After a beat, Garr cleared his throat. “You seen Ash?”
"On patrol," Dain said. His eyes moved briefly toward the trees. "He won't be far." 

“Thank you again," Garr said as he stepped off to find Ash.

A cold gust rolled across the clearing, stirring loose frost at Garr's boots and carrying a faint creak from the treeline as he made his way toward the woods. Near the edge of the woods, Ash leaned against a tree, arms crossed and cloak flapping lightly in the breeze. He looked tired, more so than usual, but his amber eyes still held that flicker of irreverent fire, sharpened now by a glance toward the deeper woods.

"You’re a hard one to pin down," Garr said, stepping up.

"I’m like smoke," Ash replied. "Try to grab me, and I’ll just make your clothes stink."

Garr snorted. "What? Wait, never mind, see anything?”

“Camp’s quiet,” he answered, glancing past Garr towards the other side. “No weird sounds, no creeping figures, and no exploding magical rocks. I’d call that a win.”

Ash looked off into the distance and stood up, posture tightening as a faint crunch echoed from the ridge. His expression shifted. “Company,” he muttered, low enough for Garr to hear. “But the good kind, I think.”

Garr followed his gaze.

Two riders crested the ridge, Nyssa and Neavin, cloaks flaring in the wind, their pace brisk and purposeful, their mounts kicking up loose frost as they descended.

"Finally," Garr muttered.

Ash started jogging forward with Garr a step behind.

Neavin was pale but alert, eyes scanning the perimeter even as his falcon, a shimmer of pale bronze in the fading sky, swooped low overhead before vanishing into the trees. Nyssa’s hair was wind-whipped, cheeks flushed from cold and effort, but her posture was relaxed as she dismounted, though her fingers lingered a moment on the hilt at her hip.

“Tie your horses up by the others,” Garr said, motioning toward the picket line. “How are you both holding up?”

“Alive,” Nyssa said. “Mostly numb, but I’ll take it.”

Neavin gave a tired nod. “We’ve had worse.”

“That’s the mood around here.” Garr's voice dropped into a tired rasp. “See anything we need to worry about?”

Neavin exhaled, rubbing the bridge of his nose. “Sent the falcon up from one of the higher ridges, and he caught something. Forged, a small group. Four or five at most. They were hours behind us. Looked like they were veering west, toward the mountains. Not toward us.”

Dain stepped in, his tone dry. “Merrick’s bloodhounds. Probably checking on his ‘investments.’” He jerked his chin subtly toward the cluster near Brother Aldrek's tent, Calen, Haldrin, Torrek, and Riven. He let his gaze move briefly to Nyssa. It stayed there a beat longer than it needed to, something quiet and unconcealed moving behind his eyes before he pulled it back. "Wouldn't be surprised if he wants them back. All five." 

Garr’s brow furrowed. “Anything else? Foot patrols? Uniforms?”

Nyssa shook her head. “Nothing visible. No standards, no banners. Just shadows in the trees and dust in their wake.”

“That’s something,” Garr said, rubbing a palm over the back of his neck as his gaze swept the perimeter once more. “We’ll make preparations for nightfall. No fires past dusk. We’ll do rotations and keep our eyes wide. And get some food down, half this camp looks ready to drop.”

As Garr turned to issue orders, Dain lingered. Nyssa was already walking his way. He caught her sleeve lightly as she passed. “Walk with me?”

She nodded, falling into step beside him as they moved toward the far edge of camp where the trees thickened just enough to muffle the world behind them. The momentary quiet felt like a relief.

“You’re still standing?” he asked, a faint smirk on his lips.

“Just barely,” she replied, cracking a smile.

They walked in silence for a moment before Dain broke it, his tone softer. “Hey… what’s the story with Riven?”

She glanced sideways, catching the careful neutrality in his question. “We grew up together. Same orphanage. Not blood, but close as it gets. He’s been in my life longer than anyone.”

Dain gave a slow nod, his smirk turning rueful. “Good to know. Just… trying to figure out the field I’m playing on.”

She laughed—genuinely, if a little wearily. “There’s no field. Just a lot of bad timing and worse luck.”

He stopped, crouched, and picked up a flat stone, rubbing the grit between his fingers. “For what it’s worth, if this all works out or not, maybe we could…”

Nyssa tilted her head. “That's an offer?”

“A hope,” he said, standing again. “Not everything has to stay broken.”

Back in camp, Garr handed out assignments with the clipped precision of a man holding everything together by sheer will. Brother Aldrek moved through the new arrivals, checking bandages and handing out boiled root tea. Calen stood apart, but observant, arms crossed, expression unreadable. Tarn lingered farther away, face a stone wall, tension coiled tight in his shoulders.

Torrek was quiet as ever, contentedly tending a pot of stew while muttering in his strange lilting cadence, and Riven had found a boulder to lean against, eyes closed, his palm resting on the earth as though it told him secrets only he could hear. Haldrin, still pale from withdrawals, sat cross-legged near the fire, scribbling notes into a soot-streaked journal.

Ash returned from the treeline as dusk bled into deeper blue, his expression unreadable. “Perimeter’s quiet. No signs of trouble.”

“You sure?” Garr asked.

Ash nodded. “Sure as I ever am.”

Then a commotion stirred at the far end of camp.

The squeal of leather, the sudden loud clatter of hooves. Tarn, true to the distance he’d kept all day, was already on one of the horses, racing away from the camp. No explanation, no farewell.

“He’s heading southbound,” Ash muttered. “He’s not even going toward Forgehollow. He’s just… going.”

Garr didn’t move to stop him. “Let him go,” Garr said, voice flat with the decision. “If he meant to be part of us, he would have stayed.”

They just stood there and watched as the dust trailed behind him, one last breath of movement before the dark swallowed him whole.

The smell of stew wafted through the camp, simple, thin, and barely seasoned, but warm enough to stir the dull ache of hunger in every gut. The fire crackled low in its pit, the circle of orange light pushing gently against the press of cold and shadow.

They ate in silence at first, the only sounds the scrape of spoons on tin and the occasional muttered curse. A bowl clattered softly as someone shifted, the small sound cracking the quiet like a seam under strain.

Riven lifted a spoonful to his mouth, frowned, and let it drop back with a plop. “This is a heinous crime against vegetables,” he muttered, too tired to even be theatrical about it.

“Better than gruel,” Torrek rumbled from where he sat hunched beside the pot, licking his bowl clean. 
Ash raised a battered flask and gave it a shake. “To not dying,” he declared. “May we always fail upward.”

A few chuckles flickered around the fire. Even Brother Aldrek allowed a wry smirk as he leaned back against a rock, eyes scanning the flickering shadows just beyond the firelight.

The firelight danced across weary faces, faces that had seen prison walls and smoke-choked tunnels only a day before. Their eyes were red, knuckles scabbed, some still sporting the makeshift bandages Brother Aldrek had wrapped. But they were alive.

Garr sat nearby, rubbing his temples as he tried to read the room. The exhaustion was thick, too thick to cut with any speech. But no one had broken yet. That counted for something.

Calen, who had spent most of the day silent and watchful near the edge of the camp, finally stepped closer to the fire. His arms were folded tightly across his chest, but his eyes had softened.

“Thank you,” he said quietly, voice a little hoarse. “For getting us out. I didn’t think… I didn’t think I’d see free sky again.”

Nobody replied, but a few heads nodded. 

Brother Aldrek had finished his rounds, checking over the wounded once more. He knelt beside Torrek, who was grumbling about the bandage being too tight.

“It’s tight because your ankle’s barely holding together,” Aldrek said. “You want to walk tomorrow or not?”

Torrek grumbled something inaudible. 

Aurelia emerged quietly from Harmon’s tent, pulling her shawl tighter against the evening cold. She paused beside Weslin, who stood at the tent flap, eyes distant.

“He’s stable,” she said softly to Garr, who had risen to meet her. “He’s been out most of the day. Drifting in and out. No worse, yet no better.” Her mouth tightened. “If he wakes, don’t mistake it for strength.”

Garr nodded, then looked to Weslin. “How’s our little squire holding up?”

Weslin gave a weak smile. “Still breathing. Same as Harmon.”

Aurelia placed a gentle hand on the boy’s shoulder. “He needs rest. You won’t help him by unraveling.” Weslin nodded, eyes brimming, but held steady.

Haldrin Vance stepped forward, clearing his throat. “If I may,” the scholar said, brushing the dirt from his robes as if addressing a royal court instead of a ring of war-weary fugitives. “A tale. While we eat, give us a moment of reprieve, perhaps. A tale with some truth, some fire. For those who still believe stories matter.”

Dain nodded once. “Let’s hear it.”

Haldrin smiled and stepped to the fire’s edge. He didn’t speak at first, letting the crackling logs fill the hush. Then, in a voice both melodic and fragile, he began to recite:

“Before the breaches, before the burning,
There were the Veylnar, Seven bound in flame…”

The Shattered Pearl. The story was older than most written things, part history, part myth, part warning. Haldrin wove the tale like it was stitched into his bones, his voice rising and falling in rhythm with the fire’s breath. He told of the Adept Knights who defied the heavens, the forging of the Pearl, and its catastrophic shattering.

He spoke of breaches and spirits, sparing detail but not weight. He told of a boy untouched by magic’s hue, the Reality Bender, child of no order and no god, who sang with the world and bent it to his will.

When the story ended, no one clapped. The fire hissed, a log cracked. Someone sniffed. “That’s a lot to put on a bedtime story,” Ash said, voice quieter now.

Haldrin gave a bow, more a nod of habit than showmanship. “The past always carries more weight than we think. Every scholar I've known learned that too late.”

As people shifted and began gathering bowls, the camp’s small noises rose again, tin on tin, boots on frost. Then the fire popped hard, and the sound cut clean through them.

A voice came from beyond the ring of light. “The story’s almost right… but not quite.”

All heads turned. Harmon stood there, as though he’d been listening longer than any of them realized. He was gaunt and pale. Sweating through his shirt. His right hand gripped a crooked branch for support, but his spine was straight, and his eyes were sharp.

The camp froze. Weslin was on his feet in an instant, bowl clattering to the ground. “Harmon?”

The old man gave a tired smile. “Someone leave a light on for me, or was that stew smoke drawing me in?”

Nobody moved for a breath. Then the whole camp rose slowly, stunned, as if seeing a ghost. And the fire crackled between them.
