The last golden streaks of sunlight filtered through the warped, grime-slicked glass of Harmon’s room in the Ironflask Tavern, casting fractured ribbons of amber across the scuffed floorboards. Outside, Forgehollow stirred with the restless energy of early evening, the rhythmic clanging of a distant forge, laughter rough and raw from miners freed of their toil, and the drifting song of a hawker peddling roasted almonds. Smoke, soot, and song braided into the dusk air, and from some unseen alleyway came the half-hearted strumming of a lute, the tune slurred by ale and apathy.

Inside the cramped upper chamber, the air felt close, burdened with tension that clung to skin and breath alike. Maps, sketches, and ink-smeared diagrams littered the table, pinned and layered with empty mugs and the low, twitching light of a lantern that guttered with every draft. Seven figures crowded the space, shoulders brushing, voices hushed. The room was too small to hold the decision it demanded.

Harmon stood at the head of the table, his palms braced against the scarred oak. Beneath the edge of his hood, lines carved by years of command and compromise cut deep across his face. His beard, streaked with ash-gray, caught the failing light like old silver. There was weariness in him, but something harder beneath it. Resolve.

"That’s the plan," he said, voice low and carved from stone. "You all know your assignments. If there are questions, speak now."

A ripple moved through the room: shifting boots, a creak of leather, a glance exchanged here, a frown there.

Then Aurelia stepped forward.

She stood straight, arms crossed over the gleaming surface of her light tactical armor, jaw set. Her voice, usually velvet and controlled, was edged now with steel. "I’m having second thoughts."

Harmon raised an eyebrow. "What are you not sure about?"

Aurelia hesitated, then gestured subtly toward Weslin. "Him. I know I can do my part, but Weslin’s still learning. Still finding the edges of what he can do. Are you certain he’s ready for this?"

The room turned, all eyes falling on the boy near the window. Weslin stood in silhouette, framed by the fire-hued sky beyond. His back was straight, his hands laced behind him, but the stiffness in his shoulders betrayed the weight of her words.

Harmon's voice remained calm. "Do you believe he’s not?"

Aurelia exhaled, slow and shallow. “I just don’t want to be the one who puts him in harm’s way.” The words stayed between them longer than she meant them to.

Harmon’s voice hardened, not unkind but immovable. "And if you go in instead, we lose the link. Your gifts don’t work past those runes. The mental tether is essential, and you’re the only one who can keep it stable. You knew that when we began."

He turned his gaze to Weslin, steady. “The boy says he can do it. Then he can.” A brief pause. “Trust what you taught him. Trust his courage.”

Aurelia's lips pressed tight, but she nodded, stepping back.

Nyssa, lounging with one boot on the bench, stood and approached Weslin. She offered him a crooked grin. “You’ve got a whole pack of troublemakers at your back, kid. And me? I’m the one who makes sure people like you come out alive. Stick close. If something explodes, I’ll try to make sure it’s behind us."

Weslin tried to smile. It came out lopsided, but honest.

Harmon looked around. "Any other questions?"

Nyssa raised a hand. "Yeah. Where can a girl find a decent stash of bluefire powder in this town?"

Harmon smirked. "That’s on you. We’ve got a few hours after this to gather what we need. Use them wisely."

He turned then, gesturing to Dain. "You’re dismissed. Prep what you need. We move tonight."

Dain gave a curt nod and disappeared through the door, silent as a shadow.
Harmon straightened. “Neavin, Dain, and I step back now. Once boots hit the street, Garr takes point. Field command is yours.”
Garr, steady and unreadable, simply nodded. Garr let the silence sit a beat, then said, "Anybody not clear on their part, speak up now. Once we're out that door, there's no going back for second guesses. 
“Aurelia,” Harmon continued, “link them.”
She was already moving. Fingers brushed her temple. A pulse of golden energy shimmered through the room, threading into each of them. Familiar. Comforting. A mind-bridge built of trust and trained will.

"Gather the remainder of your belongings. We leave no traces. Nothing left behind."

He rolled up the maps, stuffing them into a weathered satchel, his hands quick, efficient. But his jaw tightened with every fold. This wasn’t just a strategy. This was something deeper.

Outside, the tavern roared with life, but in this room, it felt far away.

"Ash," Nyssa called as she cinched her belt, "Try not to incinerate anything important until we’re out the door."

The pyrokinetic flashed a grin. "Can’t make promises. Chaos helps."

Weslin shook his head, smiling faintly. "You’re all mad."

Garr clapped him on the shoulder. Solid. Reassuring. "And you’re one of us now. Get used to it."

Aldrek murmured something no one could make out, under his breath, then met Weslin's gaze with a tired but sincere smile. "We do this clean. We do it fast. And we bring him home."

A quiet settled over them. Not peace, but purpose.

Outside, night unfurled its shadowed cloak across Forgehollow.

And the mission began, with no turning back on anyone's minds.
The night air outside the Ironflask had cooled, laced with the faint bite of coal smoke and the sour tang of the tannery downriver. Most of Forgehollow was winding down, but the pocket of alley just east of the tavern still pulsed with life, dim lanterns swinging on rusted hooks, distant shouts from gamblers and drunks, the heavy clatter of boots on stone.
Nyssa adjusted the fit of her gear in the shadow of a boarded-up bakery, her lean frame crouched in the dark. She worked quickly, fingers tightening the clasps of her reinforced leather, checking hidden pouches, blade sheaths, and the twin coils of tripwire looped across her waist like a bandolier. A soft jingle of glass followed as she lifted a small satchel and gave it a light shake, vials and charges, everything she needed for entry.
Weslin stood a few paces away, adjusting the collar of his cloak. His fingers trembled slightly as he fumbled with a clasp. Nyssa watched him out of the corner of her eye.
“You’re doing that thing again,” she said, voice low.
“What thing?”
“That thing where your hands pretend you’re not nervous, but they’re lying.”
Weslin blew out a breath and dropped his hands. “I keep running through it in my head.”
Nyssa slipped a throwing knife into her boot and stood. “Good. Keep running it. Repetition keeps the screaming quiet. ”
He looked at her, uncertain. “That's supposed to be comforting?”
“Nope. Just true.”
She gave him a small smirk, then reached over and fixed the clasp he’d been struggling with. “Breathe slower. Shoulders back. Let the cloak do the work.”
Weslin nodded. “Thanks.”
From around the far side of the bakery, soft footsteps padded in from the street. Ash emerged from the gloom, his coat half-unbuttoned, his gloves cinched tight. 
“You two look cozy,” he drawled.
“We were bonding,” Nyssa said, straightening. “You’re late.”
He gave a crooked grin, then glanced upward at the cracked clock face just barely visible above the smog-stained rooftops. The 10th Bell hand sat one notch shy.
“About ten minutes left,” Ash said. 
 “I had to find more burn paste. You people keep asking me to control myself, so I figured I’d better prep for when I don’t.”
 “Garr’s in position. Dain’s outside the walls with the horses, staging our exit. Aurelia’s with him, but she’s already tethered to Weslin; that link’s steady. She’ll ride his thoughts once he’s inside.”
Nyssa pulled the hood over her hair, flattening the auburn strands. “Then we stick to the rhythm. I’ll get us through the first lock. Weslin’s eyes are on the wards. He could feel Aurelia in his mind again, not words this time, just presence. She’s stronger at reading magical weave and hearing them. They double up, they might be able to hear the hum.”
She paused, fingers brushing her hip idly, eyes distant for just a moment. “Wish Dain was on this side of the wall.”
Ash snorted. “Of course you do. Maybe if he were here, we could solve everything with smirks and sweat.”
Nyssa gave him a look. “It’s not like that. He just knows how to work with his hands. I mean, with a blade.”
Ash grinned but didn’t push it.
“Right,” she said, clearing her throat. “Let’s move.”
They started toward the bend in the street, where the path forked toward the prison’s rear wall and the lower delivery gate. Their route was narrow, boxed in by slumping brickwork and the occasional shuttered stall.
Then Nyssa froze, one hand raised.
Footsteps. Heavy. Unhurried. Not drunk. Not rushed. Three sets, approaching from the mouth of the side street.
Weslin ducked behind a broken barrel, heart hammering. Nyssa eased herself into the shadows near a stooped doorway, her hand brushing the hilt at her side. Ash didn’t hide. He just stepped back into the deeper dark, the faint heat around him fading until even the rune-light died down to nothing.
Around the corner came three guards in polished black-and-brass, faces bored but wary. Between them, shackled at the wrists and dragged forward by a heavy collar chain, walked a hunched, familiar figure.
Brother Aldrek Thorne.
His robes were torn and streaked with dried blood, one eye swollen shut. But he was upright, moving, and silent. Even in chains, there was weight to him.
Nyssa narrowed her eyes, then gave the smallest nod. “We’re on schedule. No matter what it looks like.” No one argued. That didn’t mean no one noticed.
Ash's voice barely stirred the air while shaking his head. “Well. That’s a wrinkle.”
Once the guards and Aldrek were out of sight, the team moved.
Nyssa led them around the rear of the building, where the stone foundation dropped into a lower service crawl flanked by rusting metal grates. She crouched low, pried one loose with a soft grunt, and slipped inside. Ash followed next, then Weslin, careful to mute the clink of his gear as he slid in behind them.
The tunnel smelled of mildew and oil, a service duct long forgotten except by rats and rainwater. Aurelia’s voice fluttered in Weslin’s thoughts, calm and focused.
The crawlspace narrowed, then widened into a dark corridor of old storage rooms and barrel vaults, some flooded, others choked with debris. Here, the wards began, they believed.
Weslin paused at the first one, a circular marking etched into the stone, pulsing faintly beneath layers of grime. It vibrated like a distant horn, low and resonant in his chest. He swallowed, touched the small wooden case he carried, the jeweler's kit they’d bought earlier that day,  delicate metal picks, silver needles, and other small, finely crafted tools, nestled in velvet.
“There,” he whispered. “It’s… alive.”
Nyssa crouched beside him, eyes darting. Her usual grin was absent. She watched the rune warily, the way a fox might eye a trap. Ash stood behind them like a statue, gaze flicking down the tunnel toward the open iron sewer gate beyond.
Outside, through grates and cracks, the faint sound of the city still bled in, horses on cobbles, a hawker calling about sweetbuns, the wet clang of smiths closing for the night. But down here, beneath the feet of a city that never really slept, the weight of stone and silence bore down on them.
Weslin could feel Aurelia in his mind again, not words this time, just presence. Steady, rooting him in place. Her heartbeat was calm. She trusted him.
Even though he didn’t trust himself.
He heard Harmon’s well-known, steady voice, drawn from years of experience, ‘Find the base rune. I know you can. Follow it. We’re with you in the link.’
He nodded to himself, unwrapped the kit, and began.
Ash broke off from the others near the third left corridor, shoulders hunched low as he slipped into the shadows of the offshoot tunnel. Every footstep sloshed through ankle-deep runoff that churned up flecks of grease and sodden paper, the kind that stuck to your boots no matter how hard you scraped.
This corridor, narrower than the rest, had long since been forgotten. Rusted maintenance doors lined the walls, most hanging on busted hinges or sealed shut with decades of grime and neglect. The floor sloped slightly downward, a natural funnel for whatever the town didn’t want to deal with. He passed through a shattered bulkhead, ducking beneath exposed pipework that dripped brown water onto his hood.
His coat, already soaked from the waist down, dragged at his legs like wet canvas. Ash gritted his teeth, pushing forward.
Get in. Get it done. Get out.
That was the rhythm in his head. A mantra. He needed to keep himself centered. Every sound was too much. Even breathing. 
His boots squelched in silence now, his breath soft and steady as he moved past an old utility junction, the brick walls giving way to concrete and riveted iron supports. The silence was complete, no echoes, no rats, not even the groan of old water through the pipes. It made every breath sound louder, his heartbeat too present to ignore.
Stay calm, he told himself. It’s just another job. You’ve burned half a dozen fort doors and walked out smiling.
But this was different.
They were inside the belly of something older, something meant to never be seen again. It was the kind of place that only existed on building documents, to be discovered again. The last marker he passed was nothing but faded black ink. With a place that has been visited for so long, it is hard to tell what you might encounter. 
The air was warmer now. Not hot, but warmed, as the skin of a dead fire still holding onto the memory of flame. That was the coal room, close.
He reached the rusting ladder just beyond a bent steel valve, its base stained dark from a long-forgotten spill. The ladder creaked under his weight as he climbed slowly, every rung a protest. At the top, a narrow iron hatch. This was his point.
Above him, if the blueprints hadn’t lied, sat the western wall of the prison and the lower storeroom used for dry goods, rice, root vegetables, and coal for the kitchen braziers. He’d seen guards hauling sacks in for the vendor carts.
The coal chute ran into the back corner, a rusted track system fed by pulley bags. It wasn’t part of the main prison, just an ancillary room, but it connected to the hallway near the mess, and more importantly, sat close to the internal barracks stairwell. With the right push, it would burn just enough to force a response.
Ash dropped to one knee in the filth and unshouldered his satchel. Inside, wrapped in a treated cloth, were the components.
First device, a thick glass flask, filled with fast-burning oil and resin chips. At its base, a rune-etched spark coil timed with a slow fuse glyph. Aurelia had said, “It will ignite clean. No magical burst, no flare signature. A kitchen fire got out of control.”
He paused, holding the flask in his hand. It felt warm, even though it shouldn’t. His pulse, maybe. Or nerves. He set it carefully near the chute, tucked behind a broken crate of spoiled grain. He adjusted the glyph, a quarter-turn on the rune to match the delay.
Then he stopped.
His eyes narrowed, scanning the dim light cast by a single flickering Glowshard, which he’d cracked earlier and wedged near the wall. His breath fogged faintly in the cold, not quite fear, but tension. A sense that the lines hadn’t all been drawn yet.
Ash ducked under the rusted ladder and pressed his back to the slime-slick stone, letting the weight of the moment settle on his shoulders.
One fire. Enough to draw attention. But then what? He thought.
His jaw clenched. Water dripped in steady plinks somewhere deeper in the tunnel, the sound as sharp as nails in his skull. The stink of rot clung to his tongue like it had fingers. He could taste rust in the air, copper and filth and something acrid he couldn’t name.
What if they weren’t moving fast enough? What if something went wrong?
And then it hit him. Not like one of his visions. No divine whisper. Just gut instinct, an old itch between the ribs. Like the wind before a storm.
They’re not just here for Riven. That changed everything. Every bad job he’d survived started with that same feeling.
One fire wouldn’t cut it. Not with the prison guards still clustered close to the stairs, and time tightening everywhere else. Not with alarms ready to sound at the first whiff of magic. If they were going to drag three out, they needed more.
He reached into his satchel. His fingers curled around the second flask, cold iron rim, glass belly, the little glyph on the base. A backup. Meant to buy them time. Or save someone’s life.

Ash exhaled slowly. He knelt, moving like a shadow along the slick tunnel wall, back toward the far side of the storage room. Just beyond this point, under the mess hall, was the old grease trap chamber, unused for years, according to the blueprints. Dry, neglected, but still slick with buildup. One good spark, and it would go up as a torch dropped in a grain silo.
He tucked the flask behind an overturned barrel crusted with rust and cobwebs, nudging it until it disappeared into shadow. A flick of his thumb activated the delay rune. Four minutes? Maybe seven if the damp slowed it.
That was the plan. That should’ve been enough.
But something tugged at him, a tension behind his eyes, like heat trapped behind his skull.  You could call it divine intuition.
He glanced up the ladder again. The first hatch, the storeroom he’d scouted. And then, just beyond it, his eyes caught something else.  Another hatch. Lower set. Partially rusted. Not on the blueprints. If it hadn’t been forgotten, it would have been locked tighter.
Ash swore under his breath, already weighing whether this was a gift or a trap. Curiosity flared, quick and dangerous.
He climbed. Slowly. Boots slid against the iron rungs. He eased the second hatch open with both hands and peeked through.
Another storage room, smaller, windowless, and darker than the first. The air here was thick with the smell of oil. Yet that was not it. Ash took another sniff.  This time, he got the hint of something bitter and a heavy scent of tallow. Fat? No, something else, pine resin? He finally came to that conclusion as he worked through his senses.  
His brows lifted as he took in his surroundings.
Barrels. Dozens. Crates marked in faded red paint with the old sigil of the garrison. Practice rounds. Cannon powder. Fat-soaked rope for fire drills.
Explosives.
Ash grinned, eyes glowing faint amber in the gloom.
“Well,” he whispered, “aren’t you just asking to be set on fire?”
He dropped back down, landing with a splash in the ankle-deep water. His coat dragged like kelp, but his stride picked up. A pulse in his chest, adrenaline, excitement. A half-mad idea took shape.
He scrambled back to the grease trap and found the trail, the runoff ditch that cut between the rooms. He fished into the side pocket of his coat, pulled a cracked vial filled with thick black liquid, tar oil, mixed for torches, waterproof, smoky as sin. He popped the cork and poured a winding stream from the edge of the second device’s hiding spot, through the runoff, curving toward the back wall of the explosive storage hatch.
Slow burn. Enough to slither. And when it hits that stash?
Ash snorted under his breath.
“Boom.”
He tucked the empty vial away, grabbed a broken crate splinter, and dipped the tip into the oil trail. A backup fuse,  just in case the delay rune misfired. He wedged the splinter upright in a crack between two stones.
Standing, he looked at the dark path he’d made. Oil fire. Oil to boom. Boom to panic. Panic to…Opportunity! “Hell of a plan,” he muttered with a crooked smirk. “You clever bastard.”
His stomach tightened, but it wasn’t nerves. It was pride. Real, quiet pride. No visions had fed him this one. No mentor whispering tricks. Just him, thinking fast, pulling from the dirt and ash of his past. Then Ash turned and ran.
Weslin was already bent low, eyes scanning the ward etched into the stone just beyond the threshold. It wasn’t one glyph. It never was. A full spread of runes lay out in a precise weave, curling from the center like a spider’s web.
He had the jeweler’s kit open beside him, fingers hovering just above the delicate tools. His brow was furrowed, breath was shallow. He didn’t need to rush. He needed to be right.
This wasn’t about cutting a wire or snapping a hinge. These wards were self-fed, drawn from a shard’s charge, anchored by a single glyph. One rune out of the dozen was the keystone. Not because it was weaker, but because everything else depended on it. Distort that, and the magic would fold in on itself, fast.
But finding it was like trying to pick a single voice out of a crowded room. Every mark shimmered faintly, still holding charge. Still live.
He leaned closer, trying to trace the pattern with his eyes.  And then Aurelia's voice slipped into his head, clear and cool as rain. There. See the bend? Just left of center. The break in the symmetry. That’s your anchor.
His gaze narrowed. The rune wasn’t larger or brighter. But now that he knew what to look for, he saw it,  the way the flow of the circle fed inward, not out. A quiet center. A sinkhole.
Weslin nodded once. Didn’t speak. Just reached for the crystal-tipped stylus.
He steadied his grip, held his breath. And began to distort.
Not a line through, not a slash or a smear. He altered it, carefully warping the edge of the rune, bending the curve until it no longer held meaning.
The glow flickered, another hair’s breadth adjustment. The entire glyph pulse shivered, then collapsed like a lung deflating.
Gone.
The magic died without a sound. Weslin sat back on his heels, exhaling slowly. “Clear.”
Nyssa gave a small nod, already turning toward the door.
He allowed himself the ghost of a smile as he packed up the kit. "Thanks," he murmured to the room, to the silence, to whoever was listening.
But the path ahead was open. And for now, the silence held, as if drawing breath.
